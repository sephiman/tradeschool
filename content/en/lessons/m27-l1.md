# The order book: depth and imbalance

m26 was about trades that have already happened — flow that executed. This module is about the other
half of the market: the orders sitting there **waiting**. m02 introduced the order book early on, as
part of learning what an exchange is. Now it is worth reading properly, because the book is where the
absorption of the last module physically lives.

One thing to settle before anything else: this lesson is **conceptual**. There is no chart exercise at
the end of it, and that is deliberate rather than an omission — the reason is explained near the end,
along with the tools that do show you a book for real.

## Resting orders, not trades

**What it is.** The order book is the list of unexecuted limit orders at every price, on both sides:
**bids** (offers to buy, below the current price) and **asks** or offers (offers to sell, above it). Each
price level shows a quantity — how much is queued there. The gap between the highest bid and the lowest
ask is the **spread**, and the current price is really just wherever the last trade happened between
them.

**Why it is a different kind of data.** Everything else in this course is a *time series*: one value per
period, extending left into history. A book is not that. It is an **instantaneous snapshot** — this is
what is resting right now, arranged by price — and a moment later it is different. A candle is a record
of the past. A book is a photograph of intention in the present, and it has no history in it at all.

**In practice.** Trades and resting orders relate to each other directly: a taker's market order eats
into the resting book, price level by price level, until it is filled. That is what "aggressive" meant in
m25. A big market buy that consumes three levels of asks *is* the mechanism by which price moves up.
Volume is the record of that consumption; the book is the fuel it consumed.

## Depth and walls

**Depth** is how much is resting near the current price. A deep book has large quantities at every level
close by; a thin book has very little.

The consequence is **slippage** (m21). In a deep book, a large market order eats a level or two and
fills close to where you saw the price. In a thin book, the same order tears through ten levels and
fills far worse. Same order, same nominal price on screen, very different outcome — and this is why
liquidity, not just price, decides what a trade actually costs you.

A **wall** is a conspicuously large resting order at one price — say 400 BTC bid at 60,000 when
neighbouring levels hold 15 or 20 each. While it sits there it acts as a floor: sellers must chew through
400 BTC before price can go lower. Traders watch walls for exactly that reason, and often price does
stall at one.

:::note{type=warning}
A wall is only information for as long as it is there — and it can be cancelled in a millisecond.
Resting orders are not commitments. **Spoofing** is placing large orders with no intention of letting
them fill, purely so other traders see them and react, then pulling them before price arrives. It is
illegal in regulated markets and endemic in less-supervised crypto venues. So a wall means "someone is
*currently showing* size here," never "someone will *definitely* buy here." Genuine size often hides
instead: an **iceberg** order shows a small quantity and refills each time it is eaten — which is exactly
the absorption of m14 and m26, and it is deliberately invisible in the book.
:::

## Imbalance as a crowding read

**What it is.** Compare the total resting quantity on the bid side with the ask side within some band
around price — say 0.5% either way. If the bids hold much more than the asks, the book is
**bid-imbalanced**, and vice versa.

**Worked example.** The book shows 120 BTC resting on the bid at 60,000 and 40 BTC on the ask at 60,100.
That is a 3:1 bid imbalance. The naive read is "three times more buyers, so price rises." The honest read
is more careful. It says there is currently more *passive willingness to buy* than to sell nearby —
which means it will take much more aggressive selling to push price down through that bid than aggressive
buying to lift it through the thinner ask. Price is *easier to move up* in that configuration. That is a
statement about resistance to movement, not a prediction.

**Why it is fragile.** Read imbalance the way m17 taught you to read funding: as a **crowding** measure,
not a direction. It is also the flimsiest reading in this block, for three reasons — the orders can be
cancelled, the largest players deliberately do not display their size, and the imbalance you are looking
at may well be a consequence of the move that already happened rather than a cause of the next one.

## The book right before a scheduled event

m15 told you what happens around a scheduled release — a CPI print, an FOMC decision: the spread widens,
liquidity evaporates, and the first move is often violent and then fully retraced. This module explains
the *mechanism* behind that symptom.

Market makers earn the spread by keeping resting orders on both sides. Their risk is being filled just
before a big move — holding the wrong side of it. Ahead of a known, timed event with an unknown outcome,
that risk spikes for a few minutes in a way they can predict precisely. The rational response is to
**pull their quotes**: cancel the resting orders, or move them further out and shrink them.

So in the seconds before the release, the book *thins dramatically* and the spread widens — not because
anyone became bearish, but because the people who normally provide depth stepped back. Then the number
prints, and market orders hit a book with almost nothing in it. Price gaps several levels on modest size,
because there is nothing there to absorb it. Moments later the makers return, depth refills, and much of
that first spike retraces.

That is why m15's advice was to stand aside for the release rather than to predict it. The first move
frequently is not the market's opinion of the news at all — it is a thin book being crossed.

## Where you would actually look at this

This course cannot show you a live book, so here is honestly what does:

- A **DOM** ("depth of market") or price ladder — the classic vertical display of resting quantity per
  price level, standard in futures-trading platforms.
- An **order book heatmap**, which plots resting depth over *time* as colour intensity, so you can see
  walls appear, persist, and get pulled. Bookmap and Tiger Trade are well-known examples; several
  exchanges provide a basic version natively.
- Most exchanges' own trading interface shows a simple aggregated book beside the chart. It is crude
  compared with a dedicated tool, and it is free and enough to develop intuition.

Why there is no exercise here: a depth ladder is that instantaneous, price-indexed snapshot described
above, and every chart this course generates is a time series. Building a credible interactive book —
with orders appearing, refilling, and being cancelled in real time, which is the *only* version that
teaches anything true — is a different project from this one. Rather than fake a static picture and call
it practice, this lesson gives you the concept and points you at the real instruments. The quizzes below
check the reasoning, using given numbers.

## The book's false promises

"There's a 400 BTC wall at 60,000 — it'll hold." Some version of that sentence is the single most common
order-book mistake: seeing large size resting below and treating it as a guaranteed floor, leaning on it,
or worse, moving a stop under it. Walls get pulled, and they get pulled precisely when they would have
been tested. Never let a resting order that someone can cancel for free be the reason your risk sits where
it does.

The same over-reading runs through everything else the book tempts you into. Depth tells you what it
currently costs to move price, not where price is going — a thin ask side means up is cheap *today*, not
that up is coming. Everything resting there is revocable, and a good deal of what matters (icebergs,
orders held off-screen by algorithms) is not in the book at all.

Which leaves the release, where understanding the mechanism pays off mostly as the discipline to do
nothing:

:::note{type=warning}
That first violent candle into a vacuum is not a signal; it is an artefact of the book being empty.
Trading it is paying maximum spread and slippage for the least reliable information in the day — which is
exactly what m15 told you, now with the reason attached.
:::
