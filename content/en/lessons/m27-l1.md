# Anatomy of one complete trade

You have learned the pieces separately: structure, confluence, the reaction at a level, sizing from a
stop, expectancy. A trade is those pieces assembled into one unbroken chain, decided in advance and
then simply executed. This lesson walks a single trade end to end, with numbers, so you can see the
whole process at once — and see that by the time money is at risk, almost every decision has already
been made.

We will trade a **10,000 USDT** account and risk **1%** — **100 USDT** — on this one idea. Hold that
number in mind: everything downstream is built to make being wrong cost exactly that and no more.

## The context: what the market is offering

Start with the widest lens, the block-D question: what is the market *doing*, and does it offer
anything? On the daily chart BTC is in a clean uptrend — a sequence of higher highs and higher lows —
and it has just pulled back after a push to **64,000**. A pullback inside an uptrend is the one thing
we want: a chance to join the trend at a better price, with the trend itself on our side.

**Why start here:** the context decides whether there is a trade at all. In a range or a downtrend
this same setup would be a different, worse bet. Most of the time the honest answer to "what is on
offer?" is *nothing clean* — and the best trade is no trade. Today there is a pullback in an uptrend,
so we look closer.

## Top-down: which chart decides what

That read came off the **daily** chart, and which chart it came off is itself part of the method.
Nested timeframes (m03-l2) and the timeframe-per-style choice (m23) both imply a process step neither
one spells out, so here it is, named: **the higher timeframe dictates the bias, the lower timeframe
dictates the entry** — and the order is not reversible.

- **Macro frame → bias and levels.** On the slower chart you decide *which direction you are allowed
  to trade, and at what prices*. Here the daily gives the uptrend, the pullback, and the 60,000 and
  64,000 zones. The pairing shifts with your style — a day trader reads bias on the 4H/1D, a scalper on
  the 1H — but the hierarchy does not.
- **Micro frame → trigger and stop.** Only then drop to the faster chart, and only to answer *when
  exactly, and where is the idea dead*. In this trade the rejection candle below is a **1-hour** close,
  and the stop is measured off that candle's wick.

**Why the order cannot flip:** the micro frame prints signals constantly, in both directions. Let it
choose the direction and you will always find one pointing where you already wanted to go — that is
m03-l2's warning about flipping between zoom levels to justify a position, arriving one step earlier.
So a micro-frame signal that contradicts the macro bias is a **skipped trade**, not a counter-trade
opportunity: a clean 1-hour long inside a daily downtrend is precisely the trade this protocol exists
to refuse.

## The setup: structure meets confluence

Zoom in on the pullback, still on the macro frame. Price is falling toward **60,000**, and three
independent things line up there: it is the **prior swing high** that broke on the way up (old
resistance becomes support), it is the **0,618 retracement** of the last leg, and the **rising 50-EMA**
is climbing into the same zone.
That is **confluence** — three unrelated reasons pointing at one price. None of them *creates* the
level; together they make 60,000 the place the trend is most likely to resume.

**Why confluence and not a single line:** any one level fails often. Three failing at once is far less
likely, so the odds that 60,000 holds are better than any single tool would give. Confluence is how
you turn a coin-flip level into a location worth risking money at.

## The trigger: the reaction at the level

A level is a *place to watch*, never a reason to buy on its own. Price reaching 60,000 is not the
signal; the signal is **how price reacts there**. We wait for the reaction on the micro frame (see
**m08-l2**): price dips to 59,700, a long lower wick forms and the 1-hour candle closes back at
**60,300** — a rejection candle. Sellers pushed below the level, were absorbed, and lost. *That* is the
trigger. We enter at the close: **60,300**.

**Why wait for the trigger:** buying the level blind assumes it will hold; waiting for the reaction
makes the market prove buyers actually showed up before you commit. It costs a little price (you buy
at 60,300, not 60,000) in exchange for evidence — a trade you can refuse if the reaction never comes.

## The stop: where the idea is wrong

Before sizing, decide where the idea is **dead**. The whole thesis is "buyers defend 60,000." If price
closes well below the rejection wick — say below **59,300** — that thesis is simply false: the level
did not hold. So the stop goes at **59,300**, a little under the wick low.

**Why there and nowhere else:** the stop belongs at the price that *invalidates the setup*, not at the
dollar amount you feel like losing. Placed at the structure, being stopped out means something real —
you were wrong about the level — instead of being a random distance the market wicks through by noise.
Entry 60,300, stop 59,300, so the risk per unit is **1,000** in price. Call that distance **1R**.

## The size: from the stop, not the gut

Now — and only now — the size. The budget is 100 USDT; the loss per unit at the stop is 1,000. So:

`size = risk budget ÷ stop distance = 100 ÷ 1,000 = 0.1 units` (0.1 BTC).

This is the m22 formula applied: hold **0.1 BTC**, and being stopped at 59,300 loses `0.1 × 1,000 =
100 USDT` — exactly 1% — no matter how confident you feel. The target is the next obvious supply, the
**64,000** prior high: **3,700** away, about **3.7R**. Risking 1R to make ~3.7R is the kind of
lopsided bet expectancy (m25) is built on.

Those four prices — the level, the entry, the stop and the target — are the whole trade, and they are
what a trader actually draws on the chart. The figure draws them on a generated setup with the same

**Why size last:** the stop comes from the chart, the size comes from the stop. Do it in that order
and risk is constant across every trade regardless of price or leverage; reverse it — pick a size,
then find a stop that "feels" right — and you are back to gambling.

## Managing the live position

The position is open. The hardest skill now is **doing almost nothing**. The plan is already made; live
management is a short list of legitimate actions and a long list of temptations.

- **Move the stop to break-even — against a test, not a feeling.** "Legitimate or fear?" is not a
  question to settle by mood with money on the line, so make it checkable. **The stop moves to
  break-even only once the trade has produced new confirmation in its favour on the execution
  timeframe** — a break of structure in the trade's direction, which for this long means a fresh higher
  high and then the higher low that follows it, both above entry, on the 1-hour chart the entry came
  from. Here price pushes to 62,300 and pulls back to a higher low at 61,200: test passed, so the stop
  goes to 60,300 and you are following the chart. Being green is *not* the test. Green is where noise
  lives — a single candle to 60,600 is a number the market prints and unprints all day — while structure
  is something price had to *build*. So ask it out loud: what has this trade done in my favour that it
  had not done before? If the only honest answer is "it is up", the stop stays where the plan put it.
- **Partial take-profits — a style choice, honestly priced.** The common pattern is to close **30–50%**
  at the first opposing level and let the rest run with the stop at break-even. In this trade the first
  opposing level is the minor swing high at **62,300**, exactly +2R: sell 40% there, move the stop to
  60,300, and leave 60% aiming at 64,000. It is a legitimate way to bank progress and take the emotional
  weight off the remainder — but the phrase that sells it, a *risk-free runner*, is where the honesty has
  to come in, because the runner is not free and you can price it exactly. This winner returns `0.4 × 2R
  + 0.6 × 3.7R = 3.0R` instead of **3.7R**: the free runner cost 0.7R of this trade's payoff. Cutting
  winners short systematically lowers your average payoff, and payoff is half of expectancy (m25). What
  you buy with it is a higher share of small wins — the trade that stalls at 62,300 and comes back now
  scratches at **+0.8R** instead of 0. Whether that exchange is worth making is not something anyone can
  tell you in the abstract: it is two versions of *your* system, and the journal (m27-l2) is the only
  instrument that can compare them. Decide it in the plan, run it the same way every time, and let the
  record say which version wins.
- **Do nothing — most of the time.** If price is drifting between entry and target and nothing about
  the structure has changed, the correct action is none. The trade needs room and time to work.
- **The one inviolable rule: the stop only ever moves in the trade's favour.** Up for a long, down for
  a short — **never** away. The moment you widen a stop to give a losing trade "more room," you have
  abandoned the plan and uncapped your risk.

We get the good case: price grinds up, prints a higher low at 61,200 (stop to break-even), and tags
**64,000**. Exit. Result: **+3.7R ≈ +370 USDT** — or **+3.0R ≈ +300 USDT** if we ran the partials
version above — a planned trade, executed.

## The daily stop: the rule that closes the day

Everything so far caps the loss on *one* trade at 100 USDT. Nothing yet caps the loss on a **day** —
and a bad day is rarely one trade. So the plan carries a second, separate limit: a **daily stop**, which
is m26's daily loss limit made mechanical. Write it as one sentence: *if realised PnL for the day
reaches −X% of the account, I stop trading manually until tomorrow.* No exceptions, no "one more
setup".

**In practice.** Set X = 2% on this 10,000 USDT account and the day's budget is **200 USDT** — exactly
two full −1R losses. Take two stop-outs and the session is over: charts closed, no third idea, however
good the third idea looks. The X is yours to pick, typically **1–3%** (faster styles taking more trades
a day belong at the low end), and it is set **in the plan, before the losing day** — never adjusted
during one.

**Why a mechanical rule instead of judgement:** because of *who* would be exercising the judgement. The
person deciding whether to keep trading after −3% is the version of you all of m26 is about: anchored on
getting back to even, feeling urgency, and the least qualified person in the world to assess their own
state. m26's answer was pre-commitment — decide it when nothing is at stake — and the daily stop is that
doctrine turned into a number and a hard cutoff. It works precisely because it does not consult you at
the moment you are least able to answer.

:::note{type=warning}
The daily stop is the **anti-revenge-trading** rule, so the pressure to break it arrives exactly when it
is doing its job. "Just this once, the setup is too good" is how one −2% day becomes a −10% week: you
re-enter tilted, size up to recover, and the loss you refused to accept at 200 USDT gets accepted later
at several times the price. A daily stop you override is not a rule, it is a preference.
:::

## The record

The trade is not finished until it is written down: the setup, the plan (entry, stop, target, size),
what you actually did, and the result **in R**. That record is the raw material for everything in
**m27-l2** — without it you cannot tell a losing trade from a broken system.

## Where the plan breaks in real time

:::note{type=warning}
Almost every account is not killed by bad setups but by **mid-trade improvisation** — turning a planned
trade into a made-up one after it is live.

**Widening the stop.** Price approaches 59,300 and, rather than take the planned 100 USDT loss, you
slide the stop to 58,000 "just to give it room." You have converted a known, budgeted loss into an
open-ended one. This single move — moving the stop *away* — is the cardinal sin, and at leverage it is
how a 1% risk becomes a **liquidation**.

**Choking the winner.** The opposite fear: you snap the stop to break-even at the first green candle,
before any new structure, and get shaken out by normal noise for nothing — then watch the trade run to
target without you.

**Improvising an exit.** Closing at a random price because it "looks scary," or holding past the target
because it "might keep going," replaces your planned target with a feeling. The plan named the exit for
a reason; the live, emotional version of you does not get to renegotiate it.
:::

## The trade you leave open on Friday

The market is open 24/7, so a trade you place on Friday is live and unwatched all weekend, on thin
books where 60,000 can be wicked hard on low volume — another reason the stop and the size, not your
attention, must do the protecting. And because leverage sits one click away, the discipline of *sizing
from the stop* is what stops "0.1 BTC" from quietly becoming a position whose stop is a liquidation.

24/7 also means there is no closing bell to end a bad day for you, so the "day" in *daily stop* is one
you have to define yourself: pick a reset hour, write it down (00:00 UTC, say), and let that be the bell.
Without it, a −2% evening simply rolls into the same session at 3 a.m., which is the hour tilt is
least supervised.
