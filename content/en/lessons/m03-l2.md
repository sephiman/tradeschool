# Trend, range, and levels

The previous lesson gave you the raw vocabulary — candles, timeframes, volume — and a first glance
at trend versus range and at support and resistance. This lesson is about **structure**: reading
whether price is trending or ranging, and treating levels as zones rather than lines. It is not
decoration on top of the last lesson; it is the single most important question you can put to a
chart, because it decides which tactics even make sense.

## What a trend actually is

A **trend** is not "the price went up." It is a *sequence* of swing points that step in one
direction.

- A **swing high** is a peak with lower candles on either side of it; a **swing low** is a trough
  with higher candles on either side. These pivots are the skeleton of structure — everything else
  is reading how they line up.
- An **uptrend** prints **higher highs and higher lows** (HH + HL): each peak clears the last, and
  each pullback bottoms out above the previous pullback.
- A **downtrend** is the mirror image: **lower highs and lower lows** (LH + LL).

The **why** is simple: a trend exists only while one side keeps winning the auction. As long as
buyers are willing to step in earlier each time (higher lows) and push past the last ceiling (higher
highs), the imbalance is intact. The trend is "broken" not when price dips, but when that *sequence*
breaks — when a pullback undercuts the previous higher low.

Worked example. Price rallies to **100**, pulls back to **92**, then pushes to **108**: 108 clears
the old high and 92 sits above the earlier low, so the uptrend is healthy. If the next pullback
slices to **89** — below that 92 low — the higher-low sequence is broken, and "uptrend intact" is no
longer a fact you can state. One dip does not do it; a *lower low* does.

The figure puts the two regimes side by side on the same scale. On the left, a trend: a staircase of
higher highs and higher lows. On the right, a range: price rolling between a floor and a ceiling with
no net progress. Train your eye to sort every chart into one of these two before you do anything
else.

## What "sideways" really is

A **range** is price oscillating between a rough floor and a rough ceiling, with roughly equal highs,
roughly equal lows, and no net progress. The floor is a **support** zone, the ceiling a
**resistance** zone, and price ping-pongs between them.

The **why**: a range is *balance*. Neither side can force a breakout; buyers defend the floor,
sellers cap the ceiling, and the result is chop. This is the market's default state. Trends are the
exception, because a sustained one-sided imbalance is expensive to maintain and rare — which is
exactly why a real trend is worth riding when you find one.

For example, BTC spending two weeks bouncing between roughly **59,000** and **62,000**, printing
three highs near 62k and three lows near 59k, is ranging — even though any single day inside it might
look like a mini-trend.

## Why this distinction is the whole game

Tactics are regime-specific, and using the wrong one is how accounts bleed out:

- In a **trend**, you trade *with* it: buy the pullbacks in an uptrend, sell the bounces in a
  downtrend, and let it run. Fading a strong trend because it "looks too high" is a classic way to
  get run over.
- In a **range**, you do the opposite: you *fade the edges* — lean against resistance, buy near
  support — and you take profit quickly, because there is no net progress to ride. Buying a
  "breakout" inside a range usually just buys the top of the box.

So the first question is never "up or down?" It is **"trending or ranging, on my timeframe?"** — and
that last clause matters, because a market can be a clean daily uptrend built out of dozens of hourly
ranges. Trend and range are nested. Pick the timeframe you are trading and read structure on *that*
one, consistently, rather than flipping between zoom levels to justify a position you already hold.

## Support and resistance, deeper

The last lesson said levels are zones, not lines. Here is *why*, mechanically:

- The orders and decisions that create a level are **spread across a band of prices**, not stacked on
  one number. Round numbers (60,000), prior candle bodies, old wicks and clustered stop orders all
  sit near each other, not on top of each other.
- A zone works because of **memory**: traders remember where price reacted before and act there
  again, which is partly self-fulfilling — buyers place bids into an old support because they expect
  *other* buyers to do the same. It holds because people act on it, and it fails when enough of them
  stop.

**Role reversal (the flip).** When price breaks convincingly *through* a zone, that zone often flips:
broken resistance tends to become support, and broken support tends to become resistance. The **why**
is human — the traders who sold at the old resistance and watched price leave without them now want a
second chance, so they buy the retest, turning the old ceiling into a new floor.

**Testing cuts both ways.** More touches means more traders watching, which strengthens a zone in the
short run. But every test also *consumes* the orders sitting there. A zone that has held five times
is heavily watched — and also closer to exhausting the buyers defending it. "Well-tested" is not the
same as "unbreakable"; often it means the opposite is coming.

## How to mark a zone

Draw a **band**, not a line. Use the cluster of reactions: let the candle *bodies* define the core of
the zone and the *wicks* mark its outer edge, since a wick is price that was touched and rejected.
And weight by timeframe — a zone drawn from daily swings is watched by far more participants than one
you found on the 1-minute chart, so it deserves far more respect (the same timeframe logic from the
last lesson).

## How this burns people

:::note{type=warning}
Two beginner beliefs do most of the damage. The first is **exact-line thinking**: treat a level as a
one-pixel line and every wick that pokes a fraction past it screams "broken!", so you react to false
alarms all day. Read it as a band and that same wick is just price testing the edge — which is
normal. The second, and more expensive, is **"a level always holds."** No level always holds. A level
is a *probability*, not a wall. Traders who treat support as a guaranteed floor add to a losing long
"because it has to bounce here," skip the stop, and hand the market their account when it doesn't.
Every level breaks eventually; your job is to be positioned for the day this one does.
:::

## Crypto-specific nuance: the false break

Crypto trades **24/7**, with no closing bell and no daily auction to reset the book. That changes how
structure behaves:

- **Ranges persist and levels carry across "days."** There is no session close to wipe the slate, so
  a range can grind on for weeks and a level from ten days ago is still live. There are no overnight
  *gaps* the way stocks have — but there is something arguably worse.
- **Thin books produce false breaks.** On weekends, holidays and dead off-hours, the order book thins
  out. With fewer resting orders, a relatively small market sell — or a deliberate stop hunt — can
  spike price cleanly *through* a support zone and snap right back within one or two candles. The
  break looks real on the chart but had almost no **volume** or participation behind it (which is
  exactly why volume from the last lesson matters). The defence is discipline: wait for a candle to
  *close* beyond the zone, ideally on a higher timeframe and with real volume, before you believe a
  break.
- **Alt-books are worse.** On a small-cap altcoin the resting liquidity is so thin that a single
  large player can sweep a level almost at will, so zones there are far less trustworthy than the same
  zones on BTC.
- **Crowded leverage adds fuel.** In a strongly trending perpetual, when positioning gets one-sided
  (see funding, later), a sharp move can trigger a cascade of forced closes — liquidations — that
  briefly pierces a level far past where genuine order flow would take it, then recovers. Another
  false break dressed up as a structural one.

:::note{type=info}
Put it together into a habit. Before anything else, ask: *trend or range, on my timeframe?* Mark
support and resistance as **bands**, weighted by timeframe. And never trust a break on the wick
alone — wait for a close, with volume, especially on a thin weekend book. A level is a place where
people have acted before, nothing more and nothing less.
:::
