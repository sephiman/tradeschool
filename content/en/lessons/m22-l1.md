# Whether the system actually makes money

Beginners fixate on one number: how often they win. It feels like the measure of a good trader. It is
not. A system that wins nine times out of ten can still bleed money, and a system that loses six times
out of ten can be a quiet machine. The number that tells them apart is **expectancy**, and getting
comfortable with it changes how you read every result you produce. This lesson is one connected
argument: two raw numbers, the single figure that combines them, the losing streaks that figure
guarantees, and why none of it can be judged from a handful of trades.

## Win rate vs payoff

Two numbers describe any trading system, and neither means anything on its own:

- **Win rate** — the fraction of trades that end in profit. A 55% win rate means 55 of every 100
  trades win.
- **Payoff** — also called the reward-to-risk ratio: how big your average win is compared with your
  average loss. A payoff of 3 means your winners are, on average, three times the size of your losers.

**Why neither works alone:** win rate counts your wins but ignores their size; payoff measures their
size but ignores how often they arrive. A system's profit comes from the two multiplied together, so a
great value in one can be cancelled by a terrible value in the other. A sky-high win rate with a
terrible payoff loses money: win 90% of the time, but let the occasional loss wipe out ten of those
wins, and you are underwater. A low win rate with a big payoff makes money: win just 35% of the time,
but let the winners run to several times the size of the losers, and the wins more than pay for the
frequent small losses.

:::note{type=warning}
"I win most of my trades" is not a claim that you make money — it is half of a claim. Until you also
know the payoff, a high win rate tells you nothing about whether the account is actually growing.
:::

## Expectancy: the number that actually decides

Expectancy folds both numbers into the only figure that matters — the average result of a single
trade:

**expectancy = win% × average win − loss% × average loss**   (where loss% = 1 − win%)

**Why this is the right number:** it is a weighted average — each outcome multiplied by how often it
happens — so it already contains both win rate and payoff. Positive expectancy means the system makes
money over many trades; negative means it bleeds, no matter how good any single trade felt. Work an
example: a win rate of 40% (0.40), an average win of 300 USDT, an average loss of 100 USDT.

- expectancy = 0.40 × 300 − 0.60 × 100 = 120 − 60 = **+60 USDT per trade**

So even losing 60% of the time, each trade is worth +60 on average. Now flip it — a system that wins
80% of the time but with an average win of 50 and an average loss of 300:

- expectancy = 0.80 × 50 − 0.20 × 300 = 40 − 60 = **−20 USDT per trade**

Winning four out of five, and still losing money. Expectancy is what separates the two; win rate on
its own would have ranked them backwards.

:::note{type=info}
You met **R-multiples** in risk management: a 40% win rate can still profit if winners average +2R and
losers −1R. Expectancy is the number that makes that precise. Expressed in R instead of USDT, it does
not depend on your position size — same idea, portable units.
:::

## Break-even: the win rate a payoff demands

Set expectancy to exactly zero and you learn the **break-even win rate** — the frequency you must
clear, for a given payoff, just to avoid losing. Writing the payoff as *b* (average win ÷ average
loss), expectancy is zero when the wins and the losses balance:

win% × *b* = (1 − win%) × 1  →  win% × (*b* + 1) = 1  →  **break-even win% = 1 ÷ (1 + payoff)**

Why this matters: it turns the vague "you need a good payoff" into a hard threshold you can check
before ever placing a trade. Worked cases:

- Payoff **3** → 1 ÷ 4 = **25%**. Winners three times your losers: win one in four and you break even.
- Payoff **2** → 1 ÷ 3 = **33.3%**. Winners twice your losers: win more than one in three and you profit.
- Payoff **1** → 1 ÷ 2 = **50%**. Equal-sized wins and losses: the coin-flip threshold.
- Payoff **0.5** → 1 ÷ 1.5 = **66.7%**. Winners half the size of losers: you must win two out of three
  *just to tread water*.

This closes the loop on the two examples above. The first (payoff 300 ÷ 100 = 3) needs only 25% to
break even and actually wins 40% — comfortably profitable. The second (payoff 50 ÷ 300 ≈ 0.17) needs a
punishing 85.7% to break even and only wins 80% — so despite four wins in five, it loses. Same lesson,
now as a threshold instead of a full calculation.

:::note{type=info}
Break-even is the *floor*, not the target — and the real floor is higher than the formula says, because
every trade pays fees (and, in futures, funding). A payoff that break-evens on paper loses money once
costs come out, so the win rate you actually need is always a little above 1 ÷ (1 + payoff).
:::

## Losing streaks are normal — and computable

Even a solidly positive system loses often, and losses cluster. If your win rate is 40%, then any
single trade has a 60% chance of losing, and a run of losses is not a malfunction — it is arithmetic.
The chance of losing five in a row is 0.60^5 (0.60 multiplied by itself five times), about 0.078 —
roughly **1 in 13**. Push it further: eight losses in a row is 0.60^8 ≈ 0.017, about **1 in 60**.
Neither is rare. Over a few hundred trades a five-loss streak is expected to happen more than once, and
an eight-loss streak somewhere in a long career is close to certain.

**Why they cluster:** the trades are (near enough) independent, so a bad run carries no memory and no
self-correction — the market does not "owe" you a win after four losses. And the more trades you take,
the deeper the worst streak you should expect to meet: a larger sample does not smooth the streaks
away, it *guarantees* you eventually hit a run long enough to test your nerve.

:::note{type=warning}
This is exactly where people abandon a working system. A losing streak feels like proof the edge is
"broken," so they tear up the rules mid-drawdown — often right before the streak ends. Decide in
advance how deep a losing run your system can normally produce, so that when it arrives you recognise
it instead of panicking.
:::

## Variance and sample size

You cannot read expectancy off a handful of trades. Ten trades, even twenty, is noise. A losing system
can show a tidy profit over its first fifteen trades; a winning one can be underwater after thirty.
This spread of outcomes around the true average is **variance**, and it shrinks only as the number of
trades grows.

**Why small samples lie:** with a 40% win rate, a run of ten trades can easily come out 7 wins or 2
wins purely by chance — and those two runs tell opposite stories about a system that has not changed at
all. The measured average only settles onto the true expectancy as trades pile up; early on it swings
wildly around it. So judging whether you have an edge takes a real **sample** — think hundreds of
trades taken the same way, not the last five. Someone showing you a screenshot of eight green trades is
showing you variance, not a proven edge — and your own good week proves nothing until it repeats across
a real sample.

:::note{type=info}
Two systems with identical expectancy can feel completely different to trade: a high-win-rate one hands
you frequent small wins and the occasional painful loss; a low-win-rate one makes you sit through long
strings of losses for the occasional big win. Same maths, very different psychology — choose the one
you can actually follow.
:::

## Why the numbers fool you

Three mistakes, and they are really one mistake — reading too much certainty into too little evidence, or
into the wrong number. Each of them has a sentence the trader says to themselves first.

*"I win nine out of ten."* The most seductive trap. A trader protects a 90% win rate by cutting winners
early and letting losers run, and the win rate stays gorgeous while the account shrinks. Win rate without
payoff is half a number.

*"Twenty trades in, and it's working."* Eight or twenty results are indistinguishable from luck in either
direction. People adopt a system after a lucky first week and bin a good one after an unlucky one — both
are reactions to variance, not evidence.

*"This system is broken."* Said in the middle of a losing streak, and the cruellest of the three, because
it punishes the people doing everything else right. The streak you computed above *will* arrive; quitting
in the middle of it locks in the drawdown and forfeits the recovery the maths was about to deliver.

The honest stance underneath all three: you will almost never have enough data to be certain, and your
feelings during a drawdown are the least reliable evidence you own. Decide the rules — expectancy, payoff,
tolerable streak — *before* the emotion arrives, and then let a real sample, not a bad afternoon, be the
thing that changes your mind.

## Always another candle

Crypto runs **24/7** — no closing bell, no weekend, always another candle. That is precisely what makes
it dangerous for expectancy. Your edge belongs to a *specific setup*; it is the positive expectancy of
that setup, not of "trading" in general. But a market that never closes tempts you to keep clicking,
and every marginal trade taken out of boredom or FOMO is a lower- or negative-expectancy trade mixed
into the sample. You are not adding to your edge — you are **diluting your average** with trades that
never had one.

Two costs make this worse. First, **fees and funding**: every extra trade pays the round-trip fee, and
a thin per-trade edge is exactly the kind that a stream of low-quality, high-frequency trades eats
alive — the break-even win rate quietly rises with every position you open. Second, **compressed
time**: because you can take a week's worth of trades in a single all-night session, a normal losing
streak that a daily-swing trader would meet over months can hit you in a few hours — which feels far
more like a "broken system" than it is, and feeds straight into the abandon-during-a-streak trap above.

:::note{type=info}
The discipline the 24/7 clock demands is *not taking* trades. Fewer, higher-quality trades keep the
sample clean and the measured expectancy honest; overtrading buries a real edge under noise and fees
long before variance alone would have.
:::
