# What crypto actually is

Before you ever open a trade, you need a clear, un-mystical picture of what you are trading. Crypto
is not magic internet money and it is not a scam by definition — it is a set of technologies with
specific properties, some genuinely useful and some wildly oversold. The aim of this lesson is a
working mental model, so that when someone later waves a chart or a "narrative" at you, you can ask
the only question that matters: *what is actually backing this?*

## A blockchain, at user level

A **blockchain** is a shared ledger: a list of transactions that many independent computers
("nodes") keep a copy of and agree on. Transactions are bundled into **blocks**, and each block
cryptographically points back to the previous one, so the chain is a tamper-evident history — you
cannot quietly alter an old entry without every later block no longer fitting. You do not need the
cryptography to trade, but you do need three ideas, and the reason each one holds:

- **No central operator.** There is no company whose database "is" Bitcoin; thousands of nodes hold
  the same history and reject anything that breaks the rules. This matters because no single party
  can print extra coins, freeze your balance, or reverse a payment to please a regulator or a
  friend — the rules are enforced by everyone at once. In practice, nobody can "call Bitcoin" and
  ask for the supply to be doubled; changing a core rule takes convincing the whole network, which
  is slow and rare by design.
- **Settlement is final.** Once a transaction is confirmed and buried under enough later blocks,
  reversing it is, in practice, impossible — undoing it would mean out-running the entire honest
  network to rewrite every block stacked on top, which is economically absurd on a large chain. The
  practical consequence is blunt: send coins to the wrong address or to a scammer and there is no
  chargeback and no support desk. The finality that protects you from censorship also removes your
  safety net.
- **You hold keys, not accounts.** Ownership is proven by a **private key** — a secret number —
  because the chain has no notion of "your account" tied to your name; it only knows which key
  authorised a transfer. Control of the key *is* ownership, with nothing else attached. So lose the
  key and the coins are frozen forever (millions of BTC are gone exactly this way); leak it and they
  are stolen instantly, with nothing to reverse. That is the whole point of self-custody and the
  whole danger of it.

:::note{type=warning}
"Not your keys, not your coins." If your coins sit on an exchange, you own an IOU from that
exchange, not the coins themselves. That distinction becomes very real the day an exchange freezes
withdrawals or goes bankrupt — as several have, taking customer balances with them.
:::

## Bitcoin vs altcoins vs stablecoins

These three words get thrown around as if they were the same asset class. They are not, and treating
them alike is how beginners get hurt.

- **Bitcoin (BTC)** is the oldest and most decentralised chain, designed to be scarce (a fixed
  issuance schedule capped at 21 million coins) and deliberately hard to change. It is treated as
  the reserve asset of the space because it has the longest track record, the widest distribution,
  and no company or foundation that can be leaned on — the closest thing crypto has to a neutral
  benchmark. In practice, most other coins are quoted and mentally measured against BTC: when people
  say "alts are bleeding against Bitcoin," they mean BTC is holding value while the rest fall
  relative to it.
- **Altcoins** are literally everything that is not Bitcoin — from serious platforms with real usage
  down to tokens that exist only to be sold to you. The quality range is enormous because launching
  a token is nearly free and permissionless: anyone can mint one in an afternoon, so "it's an
  altcoin" tells you *nothing* about quality. A chain thousands of developers build on and a coin a
  stranger shilled in your replies are both "altcoins" — you still have to judge each on its own
  merits.
- **Stablecoins** (USDT, USDC, …) are tokens that aim to hold a fixed value, usually 1 USD, so you
  can hold a dollar-like unit *on-chain* without touching a bank. They exist because you cannot
  easily hold real dollars on a blockchain, and hopping to a bank between trades is slow or
  impossible in a 24/7 market — so they are the **cash leg** of crypto trading, where you park value
  between trades and, on futures venues, usually the collateral your positions are margined in. The
  honest ones claim to hold reserves (dollars and short-term government debt) one-for-one so each
  token is redeemable for a real dollar; their stability depends *entirely* on the issuer actually
  holding what they claim.

## What confers durable value

Value in crypto comes from the same boring places it comes from anywhere: **demand for a real use**
(the network does something people actually want — a reliable way to move value, a functioning
market, a platform others build on), **credible scarcity** (supply is limited *and* the limit is
genuinely hard to change — scarcity a team can undo at will is not scarcity), and **network
effects** (a chain everyone already builds on and quotes against is hard to displace, because its
usefulness grows with the number of people using it). Each of these is a reason someone will still
want the asset tomorrow, independent of today's mood — and "someone will still want this" is the
only thing that supports a price over time.

What does *not* confer durable value: a slick website, a celebrity endorsement, a "partnership"
announcement, a large **market cap** that is really a tiny float multiplied by a fantasy price, or
the mere fact that a price went up last week. A token can moon on pure **narrative** and round-trip
all the way back down — narrative is a real force over days and weeks and worthless collateral over
years. Never confuse the two.

## Two myths with a price tag

Two classic beginner mistakes flow straight from misreading the ideas above.

**Mistaking "market cap" for real money.** Market cap is just `price × total supply`, and that price
is set by the *last trade of whatever thin slice actually changes hands*. Work it through: a token
trades at 2 USD with a total supply of 1 billion tokens, so the headline market cap is 2 billion
USD. But suppose only 1% of the supply — 10 million tokens — really circulates, and the order book
holds perhaps 200,000 USD of genuine bids. That "2 billion" is almost entirely a tiny float times a
fantasy price; a large holder trying to sell even a few hundred thousand dollars' worth would
collapse the thin book long before getting out. A big market cap on a thin float is not wealth and
not safety — it is a number that exists only while nobody sells. In crypto this is acute, because a
fresh token's real liquidity can be a rounding error next to its quoted "cap."

**Believing "stablecoin" means "guaranteed."** "Stable" describes an *aim*, not a law of nature; a
stablecoin is only as good as its reserves and its ability to honour redemptions. In May 2022 the
algorithmic stablecoin **UST**, propped up by mechanism rather than real reserves, lost its peg and
collapsed toward zero within days, erasing tens of billions. Even a well-reserved coin can wobble:
in March 2023 **USDC** briefly traded near 0.88 USD when part of its reserves sat in a failing bank,
recovering only once those funds were backstopped. The takeaway is not "never touch stablecoins" —
you will use them constantly — but that a stablecoin is a **credit exposure to an issuer**, not
risk-free cash, and a peg can break exactly when you most need it to hold.

:::note{type=warning}
On a futures venue your margin is usually held in a stablecoin, so a de-peg does not merely dent one
holding — it can distort every open position at once. "Stable" is an assumption to check, not a
guarantee to lean on.
:::
