# Price structure

Price does not wander at random. It leaves a visible record of where buyers and sellers fought, and
that record — the **structure** — is the most honest chart-reading you can do, because it is the
price itself, not an indicator derived from it. Everything in this lesson is a way of reading that
record: where price stalled, where it broke, and whether the breaks meant anything.

## Support and resistance are zones, not lines

A **support** is a price area where buying has repeatedly stepped in and stopped a fall. A
**resistance** is an area where selling has repeatedly capped a rise. The word to hold onto is *area*.

Why does buying reliably reappear at the same place? Because the place is remembered, and memory
turns into orders. Traders who bought the last bounce and watched it work leave bids to buy it again.
Traders who missed the bounce wait there for a second chance. Traders who bought too high and are
underwater put their break-even exits there. A support is not magic in the number — it is a shelf of
resting buy orders that the last few visits taught people to leave. Resistance is the same shelf built
from sell orders.

That is also why it is a **zone**, not a line — a band a few candles wide — because:

- Different traders placed their orders at slightly different prices.
- Wicks and closes tell different stories; the "level" is smeared across both.
- The more times an area is tested, the wider and more real the zone becomes, not the more precise.

Concrete case: say BTC has turned up three times in the same neighbourhood — once it wicked down to
58,050 and closed back at 58,600, once it based around 58,300 for several candles, once it dipped to
58,150 intraday and recovered. There is no single support *price* in that story. There is a shelf
roughly 58,000–58,400 wide. Draw the zone from the **bodies and wicks of the swings that actually
turned**, and expect price to react *somewhere inside it*, not at one exact number.

:::note{type=warning}
The most common self-inflicted wound: drawing a level, watching price miss it by 0.3%, and
concluding "support failed." It didn't fail — you drew a line where the market drew a band. A zone
that gets poked and holds is doing its job.
:::

## Breakout vs fakeout

Sooner or later price leaves the range. The question is whether it *meant* it.

- A **genuine breakout** closes decisively **beyond** the level — a full candle body on the far side
  — and then **holds** there, ideally coming back to *retest* the old level as new support (or
  resistance) and bouncing away. Extra credit if the break happens on **higher volume**: it took
  real participation to get through.
- A **fakeout** (false break) pokes **beyond** the level — often on a long wick — and then closes
  **back inside** the range. The break is rejected. These trap the traders who bought the first
  candle past the line.

Both have a mechanism, and it is worth understanding rather than memorising. A genuine breakout tends
to **retest and hold** because of *role reversal*: the sellers who defended the old resistance have
been overrun, and many cover their shorts on the pullback to the level, turning former sellers into
buyers; meanwhile the buyers who refused to chase finally get their entry at the old line. The level
that used to cap price now catches it — resistance becomes support. The retest is the market voting a
second time.

A **fakeout** happens because the obvious level is exactly where resting orders pile up just beyond
it: the stop-losses of traders positioned into the level, and the buy orders of breakout traders who
leave "buy if it breaks" instructions. Push price a hair past the level and you trigger all of them
at once — a burst of buying with nothing real behind it. Once that fuel is spent, price falls back
inside, and the ones who bought the poke are now trapped above the level and motivated to sell. The
false break didn't fail by accident; it ran out of the very orders that caused it.

Concretely, using the numbers in the figure below: resistance sits around **2,130**, and the two
panels are literally the same chart — the same range, the same candles — right up to the decision. On
the left, a genuine break: price closes beyond the line, pushes on to **2,225**, and never comes back.
On the right, a fakeout: the same poke reaches **2,166**, holds half a dozen candles above the line,
then loses them — it closes back under 2,130, gives up **2,035** within a few candles, and keeps
sliding from there. Same level, same first candles; only the close *that holds* and what follows tell
them apart.

::figure{id=fig-m08-breakout-vs-fakeout}

The single most useful discriminator: **wait for the close, and for that close to hold.** A wick
through a level is a rumour; a few candles closing on the other side are an unconfirmed headline —
the right-hand panel has them and still fails; a body that closes beyond and *stays* is the news.

:::note{type=info}
Nobody can tell a breakout from a fakeout at the instant price crosses the line — not you, not
anyone. That is why patient traders wait for the close beyond, and often for the retest, accepting
they will miss the very first move in exchange for not being trapped by most fakeouts.
:::

## Market structure: HH/HL and LH/LL

Zoom out from the single level to the sequence of swings.

- An **uptrend** is a staircase of **higher highs (HH)** and **higher lows (HL)**: each rally exceeds
  the last peak, and each dip bottoms above the last dip.
- A **downtrend** is the mirror: **lower highs (LH)** and **lower lows (LL)**. Each bounce fails
  lower, each drop goes further.

Why bother naming the staircase? Because the sequence tells you who is winning without asking any
indicator. Higher highs mean buyers keep finding other buyers willing to pay more; higher lows mean
every dip is bought before it gives back the last gain. As long as that holds, the path of least
resistance is up, and betting against it is betting against the visible balance of force. The moment
the staircase stops, so does the assumption.

A clean uptrend might read: low at 100, high at 110, dip to 104 (a higher low), high at 118 (a higher
high), dip to 109 (higher low), high at 126. Each low above the previous low, each high above the
previous high — a staircase you could describe to someone over the phone.

A **pullback** is the counter-move *within* a trend — the dips to 104 and 109 above. It exists
because not everyone acts at once: some buyers take profit into strength, some late sellers try their
luck, and price gives back part of the last leg before the trend resumes. It is the trend catching
its breath, not reversing. The catch is that at the moment it forms you cannot tell a pullback from
the start of a reversal — only the next swing settles it. A dip to 109 that turns back up into a new
high *was* a pullback; the same dip that keeps going and breaks 104 was the first crack.

## Change of character

A trend stays intact as long as the staircase continues. It is broken by a **change of character
(CHoCH)**: the first swing that violates the pattern.

In an uptrend, that is the first time price makes a **lower low** — it fails to hold above the
previous HL. In a downtrend, it is the first **higher high**. Why does that first break carry so much
weight? Because the whole uptrend rested on one mechanism — every dip being bought above the previous
dip. A lower low is the first time that mechanism visibly fails: the buyers did not show up where they
had been showing up. In the staircase above, price falling from 126 down through 109 to a low of 106
is a change of character — the first lower low in a run that had made only higher ones.

The figure puts the two staircases side by side with every swing labelled: on the left the one that
keeps climbing, on the right the same ladder whose final swing fails.

The change of character is not a promise that the trend has reversed; it is the first *evidence* that
the old trend's rule has stopped working. Often it precedes a longer transition or a range, not a
clean flip.

:::note{type=warning}
"Change of character" gets sold as a magic reversal signal. It isn't. It's a broken pattern — a
reason to stop assuming the old trend and start watching for what replaces it. One lower low in an
uptrend can be a deep pullback that resumes; treat the CHoCH as a question, not an answer.
:::

## The expensive misread

Two mistakes do most of the damage this topic causes.

**Chasing the first candle past a level.** The break looks obvious, the candle is green and fast, and
the fear of missing it drowns out the plan — so you buy the poke. But the poke is exactly what a
fakeout is made of: you have bought the burst of triggered stops and breakout orders at the worst
possible price, moments before it reverses back inside. Waiting for the close, and often the retest,
costs you the first few points and spares you most of these traps. The first candle past a level is
the single most expensive candle to trade.

**Calling a reversal on one change of character.** A single lower low feels like permission to flip
short and fade the whole prior trend. But one CHoCH is a broken pattern, not a new trend, and strong
trends print the odd lower low inside a deep pullback all the time. Flip your entire bias on the first
one and the trend will run you over on the resumption. Treat the CHoCH as a question — *has control
actually changed?* — and wait for the answer: a lower high after the lower low, a failed attempt to
reclaim, a second confirming break.

:::note{type=warning}
Both mistakes share a root: acting on the *first* sign instead of the *confirmed* one. The market
pays a premium to whoever is willing to be a candle or two late in exchange for being trapped far less
often.
:::

## Crypto is open 24/7 — and that bends these patterns

Traditional markets close; crypto never does, and the always-on order book changes how structure
behaves.

**Weekend and thin-book fakeouts.** With no closing bell, the dead hours — weekends, and the stretch
when every major timezone is asleep at once — run on a thin order book. Fewer resting orders means
far less size is needed to shove price through an obvious level, so false breaks cluster in exactly
these windows. A "breakout" printed at 04:00 on a Sunday deserves more suspicion than the same break
in a busy weekday session; the level may simply have been pushed through empty air and will snap back
when real volume returns.

**Stop-hunt wicks.** Leverage concentrates stop-loss orders and liquidation prices just beyond the
obvious swing highs and lows — everyone watches the same levels, so everyone's protective orders end
up in the same narrow band. In a thin book a large player can push price into that band, trip the
clustered stops and force the liquidation of over-leveraged traders, absorb the resulting flood of
orders, and let price snap back — leaving a long wick that pierced the level and closed far away from
it. (Remember: a *liquidation* here is the exchange force-closing those over-leveraged positions, not
ordinary selling.) This is why the wick that pokes your level is often longer and nastier in crypto
than the textbook version — and why the **close**, never the extreme, is the only honest reading of
whether a level actually broke.

## Reading a chart in order

1. Mark the obvious zones first — the ones a stranger would circle. If you have to squint, it isn't a
   level.
2. Read the swing sequence: are you in HH/HL, in LH/LL, or in a messy range with neither?
3. At a level, wait for the **close**. Beyond and holding = breakout; beyond then back inside =
   fakeout. And ask *when* the break happened — a break in dead weekend liquidity is a weaker signal
   than the same break in a busy session.
4. Let the market confirm before you rename a pullback a reversal. The change of character is the
   earliest honest warning — no earlier.
