# Derivatives data and liquidity

Perpetual futures generate data that spot markets don't: how many contracts are open, who is
paying whom to hold them, and where forced exits are stacked up. Read honestly, this data tells you
how *crowded* and how *committed* the market is. Read lazily, it becomes another set of magic
numbers. None of it predicts direction on its own — it describes the conditions a move is happening
in.

## Open interest: read it *with* price

**Open interest (OI)** is the total number of derivative contracts currently open — positions that
have been entered and not yet closed. It is not volume (that counts trades) and it is not a price.
On its own, a rising OI number means nothing. It only becomes a reading when you pair it with what
**price** is doing over the same window:

- **Price up, OI up** — new money is entering on the long side. The move is *backed* by fresh
  positions: the healthiest kind of trend.
- **Price up, OI down** — the rally is old shorts closing out (**short covering**), not new buyers
  arriving. Price can rise on this, but there is no fresh conviction behind it, so it tends to
  stall.
- **Price down, OI up** — new shorts are pressing. Fresh money betting the other way.
- **Price down, OI down** — longs are giving up and closing. A washout, not necessarily new selling.

The one sentence to keep: **OI tells you whether a move is backed by new positions or just by people
closing old ones.** A rising price with falling OI is not the same animal as a rising price with
rising OI, even though the candles can look identical.

:::note{type=warning}
The classic beginner error is reading OI alone — "OI is rising, that's bullish." It is not bullish
or bearish by itself. The same OI increase means new longs in one context and new shorts in another.
Always read it against the direction of price over the same window.
:::

## Funding rate: positioning, not a signal

Perpetual futures never expire, so exchanges use a small periodic payment — the **funding rate** —
to tether the perp price to spot.

- **Positive funding:** the perp is trading above spot, so **longs pay shorts.** The crowd is
  leaning long.
- **Negative funding:** the perp is trading below spot, so **shorts pay longs.** The crowd is
  leaning short.

Day to day the payment is tiny and just a cost of holding a position. What's worth watching is the
**extreme**. Deeply positive funding says almost everyone is already long and paying for the
privilege — the positioning is *crowded and one-sided*. Crowded positioning is fragile: if nearly
everyone is long, there is little new buying left, and a lot of people are one bad candle away from
being forced out.

:::note{type=warning}
Funding is a positioning gauge, not an entry signal. "Funding is positive, so go long" is exactly
backwards — extreme positive funding is a caution flag that the long side is overcrowded, not a
green light to join it.
:::

## Liquidations, clusters, and squeezes

Recall from the leverage module: a **liquidation** is the exchange **forcibly closing** a leveraged
position when its margin can no longer cover the loss. It is not ordinary selling and it is not
"liquidity" — it is a forced close, and nothing else.

Because leverage traders cluster their entries and stops around the same obvious levels (round
numbers, just under visible support), their liquidation prices bunch up into **clusters** — pools of
positions that would all be force-closed near the same price. These clusters act like **magnets**:
reaching one triggers a wave of forced orders, and that forced flow tends to pull price the last
stretch toward the cluster and then accelerate through it.

When a cluster goes off in a chain, you get a **squeeze**:

- **Short squeeze:** price rises into a cluster of shorts, they are force-closed (which means
  *buying* to close), that forced buying pushes price higher, which liquidates more shorts — a
  cascade *up*.
- **Long squeeze:** price falls into a cluster of longs, they are force-closed (*selling* to close),
  that forced selling pushes price lower, liquidating more longs — a cascade *down*.

This is where the three readings meet: extreme positive funding (crowd is long) plus a fat cluster
of long liquidations just below the current price is a setup where a small dip can snowball into a
long squeeze. The data didn't predict the drop — it described a market primed for one.

:::note{type=info}
Liquidation "heatmaps" are estimates, not x-ray vision. A cluster is a zone price is *drawn toward*
and where moves can turn violent — a probability, not an appointment. The practical takeaway is
defensive: don't park a tight stop inside an obvious cluster, and expect volatility, not calm, when
price reaches one.
:::

## What to actually do

1. Never read OI without price. Same window, both at once: is the move backed by new positions or
   just closing old ones?
2. Watch funding **extremes** as a crowding flag, not a direction. One-sided positioning is fragile
   positioning.
3. Treat liquidation clusters as magnets and accelerants. Plan for volatility there; don't hide a
   stop inside one.
