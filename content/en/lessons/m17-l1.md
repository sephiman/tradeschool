# Derivatives data and liquidity

Perpetual futures generate data that spot markets don't: how many contracts are open, and who is
paying whom to hold them. Read honestly, this data tells you how *committed* and how *crowded* the
market is — whether a move has real money behind it, and whether one side has become dangerously
one-sided. Read lazily, it turns into another set of magic numbers. Neither figure predicts
direction on its own; each one describes the *conditions* a move is happening in.

## Open interest: read it *with* price

**Open interest (OI)** is the total number of derivative contracts currently open — positions that
have been entered and not yet closed. It is not volume (volume counts trades, and a single contract
can change hands many times) and it is not a price.

Why is it informative at all? Because every futures contract has two sides: for every long there is
a short. A contract only comes into existence when a new buyer *and* a new seller both step in, and
it only disappears when both sides close out. So the *change* in OI is a clean count of positions
being created or destroyed: **rising OI means fresh positions are being opened; falling OI means
existing positions are being closed.** The number alone doesn't say which side is winning — for that
you pair the change in OI with the direction of **price** over the same window. That gives four
readings:

- **Price up, OI up** — aggressive buyers are opening new longs. The move is *backed* by fresh
  positions: the healthiest kind of trend.
- **Price up, OI down** — the rally is old shorts buying back to close (**short covering**), not new
  buyers arriving. Price can rise on this, but there is no fresh conviction behind it, so it tends to
  stall once the shorts are done.
- **Price down, OI up** — new shorts are pressing. Fresh money is betting the other way.
- **Price down, OI down** — longs are giving up and closing out. A washout, not necessarily new
  selling.

::figure{id=fig-m17-oi-quadrants}

The figure shows the two rising-price cases side by side, and the two panels do not merely resemble
each other: they are **the same price series, candle for candle**. On the left OI climbs with price —
new money backing the move, so it continues; on the right OI falls while price rises — a short-covering
rally with nothing fresh behind it, so it stalls and rolls over. Identical candles, opposite meaning.

Concrete version, using the figure's numbers. Price grinds from **2,000** to **2,300** — a **15%**
move, and exactly the same move in both panels. On the left, open interest climbs alongside it, from
**10,000** to **13,000** contracts: about 3,000 contracts of fresh conviction came in with the move,
the trend has fuel, and price keeps going to **2,670**. On the right, price does exactly the same thing
but OI *falls*, from 10,000 to **7,750** contracts: no new money arrived; the rise is trapped shorts
buying to get out. Same candle, opposite meaning — and the second one runs out of buyers the moment the
shorts have finished covering: it hands back the entire leg and ends at **2,020**, where it began.

The one line to keep: **OI tells you whether a move is backed by new positions or just by people
unwinding old ones.**

:::note{type=warning}
The classic beginner error is reading OI alone — "OI is rising, that's bullish." It is neither
bullish nor bearish by itself. The same OI increase means new longs in one context and new shorts in
another. Always read it against the direction of price over the same window.
:::

## Funding rate: what it is and why it exists

Perpetual futures never expire. A normal futures contract has a settlement date, and as that date
approaches its price is dragged toward spot because the two *must* meet at expiry. A perpetual has no
expiry and therefore no natural force pulling it back to spot — so exchanges built one: the
**funding rate**, a small payment exchanged directly between longs and shorts every few hours.

- **Positive funding:** the perp is trading *above* spot, so **longs pay shorts.** Paying to hold a
  long makes longs a little less attractive and shorts a little more, which nudges the perp back down
  toward spot.
- **Negative funding:** the perp is trading *below* spot, so **shorts pay longs**, nudging it back
  up.

Funding is therefore a self-correcting tether, and its *sign* is a read on positioning: the side
that is paying is the crowded, eager side — the one willing to bleed a little to keep its bet on.

How big is the payment? A typical funding rate is around 0.01% per 8-hour interval — three times a
day, so roughly 0.03% daily, or about 11% annualised. On a 10,000 USD long that is about 1 USD every
8 hours: a rounding error, just a cost of carry. Which is exactly why the day-to-day number is not
something to trade off — it is noise.

## Funding extremes: crowding, not direction

The number becomes interesting at the **extreme**. Suppose funding on an alt perp runs at 0.15% per
8 hours — that annualises to over 160%. Nobody pays that as a cost of carry; it means the long side
is so crowded that longs are willing to bleed heavily just to stay in. And crowded positioning is
*fragile* positioning: if almost everyone is already long, there is little new buying left to push
price higher, and a large block of those longs are sitting close to the price at which they would be
force-closed. One sharp adverse move can tip the whole crowd out at once.

Picture a low-cap alt that has gone vertical for three days while funding has climbed to +0.2% per 8
hours and stayed there. That is not a buy signal — it is the market telling you the trade everyone is
already in is *this one*, and that the fuel (new buyers) is nearly spent. The honest read is "primed
for a violent flush," not "join the winners." The data didn't predict a drop; it described a market
leaning hard enough to one side that a drop is easy to trigger.

## The basis: reading the tether itself

Funding exists because the perp and spot *can* drift apart. The **basis** is that drift measured
directly: **the perpetual's price minus the spot price**, normally quoted as a percentage of spot.
Funding is the correction; the basis is the thing being corrected. Everything above read the
correction — this reads the gap itself.

- **Perp above spot: a premium.** Traders want leveraged long exposure badly enough to pay *more* for
  the contract than the coin itself costs.
- **Perp below spot: a discount.** The mirror — leveraged shorts pressing, or longs deleveraging in a
  hurry, drag the contract below the coin's own price.

**Why it earns a look of its own.** Because the basis and funding are one fact seen twice: a perp
trading rich over spot is *precisely* what makes funding positive, and funding is the toll charged for
that richness. The basis says how much the crowd is willing to overpay for leverage; funding says what
the overpayment costs them per interval. So a sustained premium is a **leverage-mania thermometer** —
crowded longs paying for the privilege, with a funding bill to match — and a sustained discount is the
same reading inverted, a crowded and fearful short side.

**Worked numbers.** Spot BTC is **60,000** and the perp trades **60,300**. The basis is `60,300 −
60,000 = 300`, that is `300 ÷ 60,000 = 0.5%` of spot. On BTC that is a lot. Majors normally sit well
inside a tenth of a percent, because the gap is an arbitrage: sell the rich perp, buy the cheap spot,
and the two prices get pulled together for a nearly risk-free difference. A 0.5% premium therefore
means leveraged-long demand is outrunning that arbitrage — and you should expect to find funding
strongly positive at the same moment, because it is the same crowding measured from the other side.
(Compare the perp against its *own* venue's spot or index price. A gap between two different exchanges
is usually plumbing — fees, transfer frictions, a stuck market maker — not positioning.)

**When the basis snaps: an exhaustion tell.** The number earns its keep in the violent cases. In a
squeeze (lesson 2) the forced flow lands on the *perpetual*, not on spot: a liquidated short is
force-**bought** on the contract, so the perp can rip a percent or more above spot for minutes while
the coin itself barely moves. That dislocation is the signature of mechanical buying. Organic demand
for the asset shows up in spot and drags the perp along behind it; a perp overshooting spot means the
bid is *forced*, and forced bids come out of a finite pool of trapped positions. So read a wild premium
spike inside a fast move as **local exhaustion** — nearer the end of that forced move than the start of
a trend — and expect the basis to collapse back toward zero as the flow finishes. Lesson 2 takes that
mechanism apart phase by phase.

:::note{type=warning}
**A premium is not a direction**, for exactly the reason funding isn't. "The perp is above spot, so the
market is bullish" reads a crowded, expensive long side as confirmation of itself. And the small,
steady premium a calm bull market carries is just the ordinary price of leverage — the noise, not the
signal. Same discipline as funding: the day-to-day number is a cost, the **extreme** is the
information.
:::

## Funding as a crystal ball

Almost every mistake here is the same mistake: reading one number **in isolation**, stripped of the
context that makes it mean anything.

:::note{type=warning}
**Funding is a positioning gauge, not an entry signal.** "Funding is positive, so go long" is exactly
backwards. Positive funding means the crowd is *already* long; extreme positive funding means the
long side is packed and fragile. The sign tells you where the crowd is, not where price is heading —
and a crowd all on one side is usually a reason for caution, not an invitation to pile in behind it.
:::

The same trap catches OI: "OI is up, so buy" ignores that the identical increase is new longs in an
up-move and new shorts in a down-move. Both numbers are descriptions of *who is positioned how* —
neither is a forecast. Used alone they feel like signals and behave like coin flips; used together,
and read against price, they tell you the honest state of the market.

## Funding is a crypto invention

- **Funding is a crypto-native mechanism.** Traditional futures markets don't have it — they have
  expiry dates and let the contract converge to spot at settlement. The perpetual swap, a futures
  contract that never settles, was popularised in crypto, and funding is the machinery that makes a
  never-settling contract behave. If you came from stocks, this is a genuinely new instrument, not a
  relabelled one.
- **The clock: usually every 8 hours.** Most venues charge funding three times a day on a fixed
  schedule (commonly 00:00, 08:00 and 16:00 UTC); some use 4-hour or 1-hour intervals, and a venue
  can raise the frequency when the perp runs far from spot. Because crypto trades 24/7, funding
  accrues around the clock — weekends and holidays included. Holding a crowded position across a long
  weekend can quietly cost several intervals of funding.
- **Alt perps go to bigger extremes.** BTC and ETH funding rarely strays far, because their books
  are deep and arbitrageurs step in quickly to close any gap to spot. A thin, low-cap alt perp is
  different: a shallow book gets one-sided fast, so its funding can spike to numbers you would never
  see on BTC — in either direction. The smaller and hotter the coin, the more violently its funding,
  and the crowding it reveals, can swing.

## Reading the two together

One rule outranks the rest: **never read OI without price**. Same window, both at once — is the move backed
by new positions, or just old ones closing?

Around that rule sit three habits. Watch funding *extremes* rather than the daily drip, and read them as
crowding rather than direction, because one-sided positioning is fragile positioning. Let the two agree
before you lean on either: price rising on rising OI *and* still-calm funding is a healthy trend with room
to run, while price rising on falling OI with funding already screaming is a crowded move living on
borrowed time. And read the basis alongside the funding — a perp trading rich over spot while funding is
already high is one crowding counted twice, and a violent premium spike inside a fast move is forced flow,
which, unlike conviction, runs out.
