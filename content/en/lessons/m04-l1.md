# Perpetual futures

A **perpetual future** — a "perp" — is the instrument most crypto traders actually use. It is not a
coin, and you never touch the underlying asset. It is a **contract**: an agreement between two
traders whose value tracks a reference price. Buy the contract and you profit when that price rises;
the coin itself stays exactly where it was, in someone else's hands.

The "perpetual" part means it has **no expiry date**. A traditional future settles on a set day; a
perp just keeps running. That single design choice creates the whole rest of the mechanism below.

## A contract, not a coin

Opening a perp does not put any Bitcoin in your account. You hold a position in a contract:

- **Long** — you profit if the price goes up, lose if it goes down.
- **Short** — you profit if the price goes down, lose if it goes up. You did not borrow or sell any
  real coin to do this; the contract is symmetric, so a short is just as native as a long.

Because you never own the asset, the exchange only ever settles the **difference** in the contract's
value, usually in a stablecoin like USDT. This is why you can go short as easily as long, and why the
size of your position is measured in **notional** value, not in coins you possess.

## Mark price vs last price

Two different prices sit on the screen, and confusing them is expensive.

- **Last price** is simply the price of the most recent trade on that one exchange. It is jumpy and
  easy to spike, especially on a thin order book.
- **Mark price** is a smoothed *fair value*, built from the wider index of spot prices across
  venues. It is what your unrealised PnL and — critically — your **liquidation** level are measured
  against.

:::note{type=warning}
Liquidations track the **mark price**, never the last price. This is deliberate. If liquidations used
the last trade, a single momentary wick on one venue could force-close thousands of otherwise healthy
positions. The mark price exists precisely so that one manipulated or thin print cannot liquidate
you. (Here "liquidation" means only the exchange force-closing your position — nothing else.)
:::

## Funding: what keeps a perp near spot

A perp never expires, so nothing *automatically* drags its price back to the spot price. The
**funding** mechanism is that tether. At regular intervals (often every eight hours) a payment
changes hands **directly between longs and shorts**, sized by the current **funding rate**:

- **Positive funding rate → longs pay shorts.** This happens when the perp trades *above* spot: too
  many eager longs. Paying to hold the long discourages the crowd and nudges the perp back down
  toward spot.
- **Negative funding rate → shorts pay longs.** The perp is trading *below* spot; now the shorts are
  the crowded side and pay for the privilege.

The amount each side moves is its **notional × the funding rate**. A positive number means you pay
out; a negative number means you receive.

:::note{type=info}
Funding is **not a fee the exchange charges**, and it is not interest on your leverage. It is a
transfer *between traders* — the exchange only moves it from one side to the other. If you are on the
side being paid, funding is money in; if you are on the crowded side, it is a steady cost that quietly
adds up on a position held for days.
:::

## What to actually do

Before holding a perp overnight, check the funding rate and which side you are on. A small rate is
noise; a persistently large one is the market charging you rent to sit in a crowded trade. And never
plan your risk around the last price — the number that can actually end your position is the mark
price.
