# Perpetual futures

A **perpetual future** — a "perp" — is the instrument most crypto traders actually use, and it barely
exists outside crypto. It is not a coin, and you never touch the underlying asset. It is a
**contract**: an agreement between two traders whose value tracks a reference price. Buy the contract
and you profit when that price rises; the coin itself never moves — it stays exactly where it was, in
someone else's wallet.

The "perpetual" part means it has **no expiry date**. A traditional future settles on a set day; a
perp just keeps running, indefinitely. That single design choice — no expiry — is the seed of
everything below, because it removes the natural force that normally keeps a future honest, and
something has to be invented to replace it.

## A contract, not a coin

**What it is.** Opening a perp does not put any Bitcoin in your account. You hold a *position* in a
contract, and the exchange tracks only the profit or loss on that position:

- **Long** — you profit if the price goes up, lose if it goes down.
- **Short** — you profit if the price goes down, lose if it goes up. You did not borrow or sell any
  real coin to do this; the contract is symmetric, so a short is exactly as native as a long.

**Why it matters.** Because you never own the asset, the exchange only ever settles the
**difference** in the contract's value, usually in a stablecoin like USDT. That is what lets you go
short as easily as long: there is no coin to locate and borrow first, just a contract to take the
other side of. It is also why your position size is measured in **notional** value — the full value
the contract controls — not in coins you possess.

**In practice.** Say BTC is at 20,000 and you open a long perp with 10,000 USDT of notional (the
contract controls the equivalent of 0.5 BTC). Price rises 5% to 21,000. Your position gains 5% of its
notional — **500 USDT**, credited in USDT — even though not a single Bitcoin ever entered or left your
wallet. A trader who was **short** the same 10,000 USDT of notional lost exactly that 500 USDT to you.
The contract is a zero-sum transfer between the two sides; the coin sat untouched the whole time.

## Mark price vs last price

Two different prices sit on the screen, and confusing them is expensive.

- **Last price** is simply the price of the most recent trade *on that one exchange*. It is jumpy and
  easy to spike, especially on a thin order book where a single large order can punch through several
  price levels.
- **Mark price** is a smoothed **fair value**, built from the wider index of spot prices across many
  venues (plus a small funding-based adjustment). It moves less violently because no single venue can
  drag it far.

**Why two prices exist.** Your unrealised PnL and — critically — your **liquidation** level are
measured against the **mark price**, not the last price. The reason is defensive. If the forced-close
level tracked the last trade, a single momentary wick on one thin venue could force-close thousands of
otherwise healthy positions in a heartbeat. The mark price exists precisely so that one manipulated or
accidental print cannot end your position.

**In practice.** Reuse the numbers from the liquidation module: a long entered at 20,000 with 10×
leverage has its forced-close level (measured on **mark**) near 18,100. Now one thin exchange prints a
last-price wick down to 17,900 for a second — below your 18,100 number — while the cross-venue index,
and therefore the mark price, never drops under about 19,000. Nothing happens to you. The last price
stabbed straight through the level that *looks* like your liquidation, but the only price that can
actually trigger the forced close — the mark — stayed comfortably above it.

:::note{type=warning}
Liquidations track the **mark price**, never the last price. (Here "liquidation" means only the
exchange force-closing your position — nothing else.) This is deliberate: the mark is built so that
one manipulated or thin print cannot liquidate you. When you set a stop and size a trade, the number
that can end your position is the mark price — plan around that one.
:::

## Funding: what keeps a perp near spot

**What it is.** At regular intervals — in crypto, typically **every eight hours** — a payment changes
hands **directly between longs and shorts**, sized by the current **funding rate**:

- **Positive funding rate → longs pay shorts.** This happens when the perp trades *above* spot: too
  many eager longs bidding the contract over the real asset.
- **Negative funding rate → shorts pay longs.** The perp is trading *below* spot; now the shorts are
  the crowded side and pay for the privilege.

The amount each side moves is its **notional × the funding rate**. A positive number means you pay
out; a negative number means you receive.

**Why it exists.** A traditional future has a built-in anchor: at expiry it *must* equal spot, or
there is free money, so arbitrage drags the two together as the date approaches. A perp never expires,
so that anchor is gone — nothing *automatically* pulls its price back toward spot. Funding is the
manufactured replacement. By making the crowded side pay the other side, it does two jobs at once: it
discourages the crowd from piling on, and it pays arbitrageurs to take the opposite side. If funding
is strongly positive, a trader can short the perp to collect the funding while buying spot to hedge
the price risk — and that extra selling of the perp is exactly what pushes it back down toward spot.
The tether is not magic; it is a paid incentive.

**In practice.** You hold a **long** with 10,000 USDT of notional. Funding comes in at **+0.01%**
(0.0001), a common quiet-market rate. Because the rate is positive and you are long, you **pay**:
`10,000 × 0.0001 = 1 USDT` this window. Three windows a day → about **3 USDT/day**. Trivial. But
funding is not fixed. In a euphoric rally the rate can run to **+0.1%** per window: now
`10,000 × 0.001 = 10 USDT` every eight hours, **30 USDT/day** — **0.3% of your notional every day**,
bleeding out of a crowded long whether or not price moves your way. Held for a week that is over 2% of
the position, paid straight to the shorts, purely for the privilege of sitting in the popular trade.

## How this burns people

:::note{type=warning}
**"Funding is a fee the exchange charges."** It is not. Funding is a transfer *between traders* — the
exchange only moves it from one side to the other and keeps none of it. Two things people miss because
of this: you are not always the payer (on the unpopular side you *receive* funding, which is a real,
if small, income), and you should never budget it like a fixed commission — a rate that is noise one
week can quietly become the single largest cost of a multi-day position the next.

**Panicking at a last-price wick.** A trader watches the last price stab down through their
liquidation number during a violent candle and closes the position themselves, in a panic, at a
terrible tick — when the exchange, reading the calmer mark price, would never have force-closed them
at all. The wick that "should have" ended them existed only on the last price. Learn which number
actually triggers the forced close before you react to a spike.
:::

## Why this is a crypto thing

Perps are effectively **crypto-native**. Traditional futures markets close on weekends and overnight
and settle on fixed dates; the perpetual — with a funding tether instead of an expiry — was
popularised on crypto exchanges and fits a market that **never closes**. Crypto trades **24/7**, so
funding is charged around the clock, commonly three times a day every eight hours, with no weekend
pause. That has practical edges: a crowded position keeps paying funding straight through the weekend
while traditional markets sleep, and the thin liquidity of late nights and holidays is exactly when a
last-price wick is most likely to appear — which is exactly when the mark price matters most.

## What to actually do

Before holding a perp overnight, check the **funding rate** and which side you are on. A small rate is
noise; a persistently large one is the market charging you rent to sit in a crowded trade — and
occasionally an income, if you are on the quiet side. And never plan your risk around the last price:
the number that can actually end your position is the **mark** price.
