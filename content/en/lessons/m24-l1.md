# Getting the order you meant

A trade idea only becomes a position through an **order** — an instruction you hand the exchange. The
same idea, entered with the wrong order type, can fill at a price you never agreed to, or fail to
fill at all when it matters most. Execution is not a detail; it is where your plan meets the order
book.

An **order book** is just the live list of everyone else's resting orders: bids (offers to buy)
stacked below the current price, asks (offers to sell) stacked above it. Every order you send either
*takes* one of those resting orders or *becomes* one. Which of the two it does is the whole
distinction between the order types below.

## Market vs limit

Every order is either a **market** order or a **limit** order.

- A **market order** says "fill me now, at whatever the book offers." You are a **taker** — you take
  the liquidity that is resting there. You get **immediacy** and give up **price control**.
- A **limit order** says "fill me only at this price or better." If it rests in the book waiting, you
  are a **maker** — you add liquidity. You get **price control** and give up the certainty of being
  filled at all.

That trade-off is the whole thing: **immediacy vs price**. A market order will get you in, but the
price is whatever is there. A limit order names your price, but the market may simply never come back
to it, and you sit unfilled while the move happens without you.

Worked example. You want to buy 1 BTC and the best ask is **60,000**. A market order fills instantly
at 60,000 — and a little worse if 1 BTC is more than the top ask holds, because it walks up to the
next ask. A **limit** buy at **59,850** instead waits: if price dips a touch it fills and you save
150; if price runs up without dipping, it never fills and you watch the move from the sidelines. Same
idea, two outcomes chosen entirely by the order type — cheaper-or-nothing versus now-at-market.

:::note{type=info}
Most exchanges charge takers more than makers, sometimes rebating makers. That fee gap is small per
trade and large over hundreds of trades — one reason scalpers fight to use limit orders. But a fee
saved on an order that never fills is not a saving.
:::

## Stop orders: market vs limit, and the fill trap

A **stop** is an order that stays dormant until price touches a **trigger**, then activates. It comes
in two forms, and the difference is where beginners get hurt.

- A **stop-market** order triggers and then fires a *market* order — it will fill, taking whatever the
  book offers at that instant.
- A **stop-limit** order triggers and then places a *limit* order at a price you set — it will only
  fill at that limit or better.

Here is the trap, and *why* it springs. You set a stop-limit to get out of a losing long: trigger at
100, limit at 99.9. In a fast drop, price gaps straight through — the next trades print at 98, then
97. The stop fires and drops a *sell* limit at 99.9 into the book. But a sell limit only fills at 99.9
**or higher**, and price is now 97 and falling: nobody will buy from you at 99.9 when the market lets
them buy at 97. Your order sits there, above the market, untouched — **you do not fill**, and you are
still in the position, now losing far more than you planned. A stop-limit protects your price and, in
exactly the moment you most need out, can fail to protect you at all.

## Take-profit and stop-loss

Two orders do the routine work of managing a position, and both are usually stop-type orders sitting
on the other side of your entry:

- A **stop-loss (SL)** closes the position if price moves *against* you to a level where you have
  decided the idea is wrong. It caps the loss.
- A **take-profit (TP)** closes the position if price moves *in your favour* to a target. It banks
  the gain before the market can hand it back.

Worked example: you go long at **60,000**, set the SL at **58,800** (1,200, or 2%, below) and the TP
at **63,000** (3,000, or 5%, above). The moment both are placed the trade is fully defined — you risk
1,200 to make 3,000 — and it will close itself at one end or the other whether you are watching or
asleep. Placing them together defines the trade before emotion arrives: the SL says how much you will
lose if wrong, the TP says where you will take the win.

You rarely place those two orders on their own. Almost every perpetuals venue lets you **attach** the
TP and the SL to the entry order itself: one instruction goes to the market, the entry fills, and its
two exits are already resting behind it — sized to the position, and wired so that whichever one
fills cancels the other. Entry, target and stop as a single unit is a **bracket**, and at minute
timescale it is not a convenience. It is the only way the plan enters the market *with* you: typing
three orders by hand while a 1-minute candle prints is not execution, it is m26's psychology
happening in real time, and the order that gets forgotten under pressure is always the stop.

This is a *voluntary* exit that you control — not to be confused with **liquidation**, which is the
exchange forcibly closing a leveraged position when your margin runs out. A stop-loss is you deciding
to leave at a level you chose; liquidation is the exchange deciding for you when your collateral is
gone. A well-placed stop-loss should trigger long before liquidation is ever in question — that is
much of its job.

## Reduce-only

On perpetual futures you can hold a long or a short, and an order can either grow a position or shrink
it. A **reduce-only** order is one the exchange promises will *only ever reduce* your current
position, never open or enlarge one.

Why this matters: suppose you are long 1 BTC and place a sell to take profit. If price has already
moved and part of the order behaves unexpectedly, or the order is duplicated, a plain sell for more
than you hold can close your long **and open a short** — you meant to exit and instead you have
flipped to the opposite side, now positioned against the very move you just banked. Marking that exit
**reduce-only** makes the exchange cap it at your position size and discard the excess, so the worst
case is a clean, complete exit and nothing more.

:::note{type=warning}
Always tag your TP and SL as **reduce-only** on perps. Without it, a mis-sized or duplicated exit can
flip you into a fresh position facing the wrong way, or stack you into a larger one — the exact
opposite of "I was closing my trade."
:::

## Slippage and liquidity

**Slippage** is the gap between the price you expected and the price you actually got. Market orders
cause it: your order eats through the resting orders in the book, and if there aren't enough at the
top, it fills deeper and deeper at worse prices.

How much you slip depends on **liquidity** — how much size is resting near the current price. A deep,
liquid book absorbs your order with barely a wobble. A **thin** book, common in small altcoins or at
3 a.m., has little resting size, so even a modest market order walks the price several ticks and you
get a visibly worse average fill.

Worked example on a thin book. You send a market buy for **10 contracts**, and the resting asks are:
**2 at 100.0**, **3 at 100.5**, **5 at 101.0**. Your order sweeps all three levels: 2 fill at 100.0,
3 at 100.5, 5 at 101.0. Your average fill is `(2×100.0 + 3×100.5 + 5×101.0) / 10 = 100.65`, even
though the price you saw quoted was 100.0. That 0.65 gap is slippage — and on a deep book, where
100.0 might hold 500 contracts, the same order would have filled entirely at 100.0 with nothing worse.

:::note{type=info}
Liquidity is not liquidation. **Liquidity** is how much can trade without moving the price;
**liquidation** is a forced close by the exchange. Thin liquidity widens slippage; it does not, by
itself, liquidate you.
:::

The practical defence: on thin markets, prefer **limit** orders so you name your price, size down so
you are not forced to eat the whole book, and never assume a market order fills near the last printed
price when the book behind it is empty.

## When your stop doesn't save you

:::note{type=warning}
**A protective stop-limit that gaps through, and never fills.** This is the most dangerous single
mistake in this lesson, because it fails silently *in the exact scenario you placed the stop to
survive*. Make a stop-limit your only protection on a leveraged long, and in a violent drop price can
gap clean past the limit: the stop fires, the limit rests unfilled above the market, and you are
still fully exposed. There is no automatic exit left. Your loss is now bounded only by how far price
falls, which in a crash can be very far indeed — an effectively **unbounded** loss where you thought
you had a floor. The fix is the earlier rule: protective stops are **stop-market**.

**A market order dumped into a thin book.** The mirror mistake. Fire a large market order into a
shallow altcoin book and you sweep every resting level, driving the price several percent against
yourself and handing that whole move to whoever was sitting on the other side. You did not get "the
market price" — you *became* the market price, and a bad one. On thin books, break large orders up,
use limits, or simply size down.
:::

## Where the fill risk becomes real

The generic rules above bite harder in crypto, for reasons specific to how these venues run.

- **24/7 markets with no closing bell make the stop-limit fill risk real, not theoretical.**
  Traditional markets close overnight, so a gap happens once, at the open, where you can react.
  Perpetuals never close: a thin Sunday-night book can gap several percent in seconds with nobody
  around, which is *precisely* the condition that skips a stop-limit and leaves a protective order
  stranded. The fill risk that looks like an edge case on a liquid weekday is a routine weekend event
  here.
- **Thin alt books turn ordinary orders into their own worst enemy.** Majors like BTC have deep
  books; small altcoins often do not. On a thin alt the slippage example above is not a curiosity — a
  normal-sized market order really can walk the price several percent, and a skipped stop-limit really
  can leave you stranded, because there was never much resting size to fill against in the first place.
- **Reduce-only is a perpetuals-specific safeguard.** On spot you can only sell what you hold, so an
  oversized sell simply sells everything and stops. On perps, "sell" past flat opens a short — the
  position flips sign. That is why reduce-only exists and why it belongs on every exit you place on a
  perp: it restores the spot-like guarantee that closing a position can never accidentally open the
  opposite one.
