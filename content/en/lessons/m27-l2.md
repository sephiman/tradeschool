# The journal, validation, and real money

A trading plan is a hypothesis: "this process makes money." m27-l1 built one complete trade; this
lesson is about the machinery that turns a pile of trades into an answer to the only question that
matters — *does my process actually work, and how do I know?* Three parts: the journal that records
the evidence, the validation that gathers enough of it before you risk real money, and the disciplined
step to real money once it does.

## The journal: the only instrument that can tell you the truth

Memory lies. It remembers the big win and forgets the three rule-breaks around it. A **journal** is the
external record that memory cannot edit. For every trade, write down:

- **The setup** — what you saw (context, level, confluence) and why it was a trade.
- **The plan** — entry, stop, target, and size, *as decided before entering*.
- **Execution vs plan** — what you actually did. Did you take the planned entry, or jump early? Hold
  the stop, or move it? Exit at target, or improvise?
- **The result in R** — not in dollars. "+2R" and "−1R" are comparable across trades of any size;
  dollars are not. A quick **screenshot** of the chart at entry and exit makes the record honest and
  fast to review later.

**Why R and not dollars:** measuring in R strips out position size and account size, so a hundred
trades become one clean distribution you can actually judge (this is the m25 sample). Dollars mix the
quality of the decision with how big you happened to bet, and hide the signal.

Then the **weekly review**: read the week's entries and sort every trade into two piles — *followed the
plan* or *did not*. This is the single most valuable thing the journal does, because it separates two
failures that feel identical in the moment but need opposite responses:

- **"The system failed."** You followed the plan exactly and still lost. If a run of *by-the-book*
  trades loses money, the edge itself may be the problem — time to study or adjust the system.
- **"I didn't follow the system."** You lost because you moved a stop, oversized, or chased. The system
  was never tested; *you* were the variable. The fix is discipline, not a new strategy.

**Why this matters more than the score:** without the journal these two look the same — a red number —
and beginners consistently misread them, abandoning a good system after self-inflicted losses or
clinging to a bad one they never actually followed. Only the record tells them apart.

## Risk tiers: an advanced practice the journal has to earn

Sooner or later someone will tell you to size by setup quality: risk **0,5–1%** on ordinary setups and up
to **2%** on your best ones, so the money follows the conviction. The idea is genuinely sound — *and* it
carries a precondition the pitch always leaves out. **Grading setups presupposes that you can grade**, and
the only instrument that can prove you can is the journal above. That is why this section sits after it
and not before.

**The default stays the default.** Fixed fractional risk (m22) — the same 1% on every trade, regardless of
how the setup feels — is the discipline for everyone, and for most of a trading life. It is not a
beginner's crutch you graduate out of; it is the version that cannot be corrupted by your mood, because it
has no dial to turn.

**What earning the tiers looks like.** Grade every trade in the journal *at entry* — A, B, C, against
written setup criteria — while still risking the same 1% on all of them. Then let a real sample pile up and
compare the average R per grade. If after a hundred-odd trades your A trades average, say, **+0,6R** and
your B trades **+0,05R**, your grading is doing real work and tiering the risk is simply acting on measured
information. If A and B come out indistinguishable — the common result — then "A" means *I felt sure*, and
there is nothing there worth sizing up for.

**Why the evidence is not optional:** m25's sample-size lesson applies with extra force here, because
grading *splits* your sample into smaller ones. Twelve A trades prove nothing, and the temptation is to
declare the ladder proven on exactly the lucky run that made you feel like a grader in the first place.
Without the evidence, "risk more on better setups" collapses into *risk more when I feel more convinced* —
which m26 identifies as the moment you are most dangerous to yourself. Two guard-rails hold either way:
never above 2%, and the grade is assigned before entry — never revised upward mid-trade, and never after a
loss to make the next trade bigger.

## Validation before risk

A new system is an untested claim, so test it where being wrong is free.

- **Paper or minimum size first.** Trade the plan on a demo account, or at the smallest size the
  exchange allows, until the process is automatic. The goal is not profit; it is proving you can
  *execute the plan* without improvising.
- **Enough trades before judging.** A handful of trades is noise (m25): a 60%-win system can easily
  show 3 losers in its first 5, and a bad system can luck into 4 winners. You need a real sample —
  think dozens of trades, not a weekend — before the results mean anything about the edge.
- **The honest limits of demo.** Paper trading validates the *process* — can you find setups, size
  correctly, follow the plan — but not the *psychology*. It has no real slippage and, more importantly,
  no real fear: risking play money feels nothing like risking rent. Demo proves the mechanics; only
  small real money starts testing the trader.

**Why validate at all:** the alternative is discovering whether your system works using money you
cannot afford to lose — paying tuition at the most expensive possible rate. Validation moves the
learning to where mistakes are cheap.

## The step to real money

When the journal shows a real, positive edge over a real sample, and only then, take the step — carefully.

- **Start where losing feels like nothing.** Pick a size where a full −1R is genuinely trivial to you.
  The first job of real money is not to make money; it is to let you feel real emotions while your
  decisions still cost almost nothing.
- **Scale only after the journal proves it.** Increase size in steps, and only when the record keeps
  showing the edge holding *and* you following the plan at the new size. Each step up re-tests the
  psychology, because bigger positions feel different.
- **Never scale to win it back.** Raising size after a loss to "make it back faster" is the exact
  inversion of the rule: it is the losing trader's reflex, and it turns a drawdown into a blown account.
  Size follows proven edge, never the need to recover.

## The four ways this ends badly

:::note{type=warning}
**Trading real size on an unvalidated system.** Skipping straight to meaningful money means every
lesson is paid for at full price, and a normal early losing streak (m25) can end the account before you
have learned anything.

**Judging the system from a handful of trades.** Declaring a system "broken" after 5 losers, or
"proven" after 5 winners, is reading noise as signal — and usually leads to abandoning something that
was fine or trusting something that was luck.

**No journal, so every loss is ambiguous.** Without the record you cannot tell a system failure from an
execution failure, so you "fix" the wrong thing — rebuilding a sound strategy you simply didn't follow,
or drilling discipline on a strategy that has no edge.

**Scaling to recover.** Increasing size to win back a loss feels like taking control; it is the single
fastest way to convert a manageable drawdown into a catastrophic one.

**Tiering risk on ungraded confidence.** Jumping to "2% on my A setups" before the journal shows your A
setups actually outperform your B setups is not advanced risk management — it is the old habit of betting
more when you feel more sure, wearing the vocabulary of one. The tiers are a conclusion the record earns,
not a starting configuration.
:::

## No excuse, and every temptation

Crypto makes validation both easier and more tempting to skip. Demo accounts and tiny position sizes
are trivially available 24/7, so there is no excuse not to validate — but the same 24/7 access, high
leverage, and feed full of other people's wins push hard the other way, toward sizing up fast on an
untested edge. And note the honest limit again: because crypto's real slippage and funding costs and
overnight gaps (m20 unlocks, m19-l2 liquidity) are absent on demo, treat paper results as an optimistic
ceiling, not the number you will actually earn.
