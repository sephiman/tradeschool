# Macro and capital flow

When you trade one coin you are also, whether you like it or not, trading its place inside a larger
tide. Capital does not sit still in crypto: it moves between Bitcoin, the big alternatives and the
long tail of small tokens in patterns that repeat often enough to be worth knowing — and irregularly
enough that you must never treat them as a clock. This module is about reading that tide: where money
sits, where it tends to flow next, and how the whole space breathes with the traditional economy
outside it.

## Bitcoin dominance

**Bitcoin dominance** is Bitcoin's share of the total crypto market capitalisation. If every coin
together is worth 2 trillion and Bitcoin accounts for 1 trillion of that, dominance is 50%. It is a
*ratio*, not a price — it can rise even while Bitcoin's own price falls, as long as the alts are
falling harder.

- **Rising dominance** means money is concentrating in Bitcoin relative to everything else. This
  happens in two very different moods: in a **risk-off** flight, traders sell alts for the relative
  safety of BTC; and early in a bull run, fresh money tends to enter through Bitcoin first.
- **Falling dominance** means capital is spreading out into alts — the classic backdrop for an "alt
  season."

Because dominance is a *share*, you must always read it together with whether the total market is
rising or falling. Rising dominance in a rising market is a Bitcoin-led advance; rising dominance in a
falling market is a flight to BTC while alts bleed harder. Same number, opposite mood.

:::note{type=warning}
A common beginner error is "dominance up means Bitcoin's price is up." Not necessarily. In a broad
crash BTC dominance often rises precisely *because* everything is falling and the alts are falling
faster. Dominance tells you about relative share, never about direction on its own.
:::

## The ETH/BTC ratio

The **ETH/BTC ratio** is simply the price of Ether measured in Bitcoin rather than in dollars. Because
Ethereum is the largest and most liquid of the altcoins, traders use this one pair as a rough
thermometer for risk appetite across the whole alt complex.

- A **rising ETH/BTC** means Ether is outperforming Bitcoin — usually read as risk appetite warming
  up, money willing to step out of the reserve asset into the next rung.
- A **falling ETH/BTC** means Bitcoin is outperforming — capital retreating toward the safer end of
  the space.

It is a thermometer, not a trigger. It tells you the temperature of risk appetite; it does not tell
you the minute to act. "Warm" does not mean *buy now* — it means the environment currently favours
alts, and that environment can cool overnight.

## The rotation

There is a rhythm that repeats often enough to have a name. Money tends to move down the risk curve
in stages:

**BTC → ETH → large-cap alts → small-cap alts.**

- New money arrives and buys Bitcoin first; dominance rises.
- As confidence grows, profits rotate into Ether; ETH/BTC turns up.
- Then into the large, established alts — the top of the alt board.
- Finally, in the euphoric phase, into small, speculative tokens — the point at which the crowd feels
  most certain and risk is actually highest.

When fear returns the rotation runs in reverse: money climbs back up the curve toward Bitcoin, and the
smallest, least liquid tokens bleed first and worst.

:::note{type=warning}
This sequence is a **tendency, not a timetable**. It does not complete on every cycle, the stages
overlap, and plenty of "alt seasons" have been called that never arrived. Use it to understand
*where* risk is flowing — never as a promise that stage N is followed by stage N+1 on schedule.
Traders who bought small alts because "the rotation says they're next" have been left holding them for
years.
:::

## Crypto and the wider macro

Crypto likes to imagine itself as detached from the old financial world. It is not. Over the last
several years Bitcoin has traded largely as a **risk-on asset**: it rises when investors are happy to
hold risk and falls when they flee to safety, moving in loose sympathy with tech stocks rather than
against them.

Two macro dials matter most for that mood:

- **Interest rates.** When central banks raise rates, safe cash and bonds pay more, and speculative
  assets — crypto very much included — become relatively less attractive, so money tends to leave.
  Cuts, or the mere expectation of them, tend to do the opposite. Crypto is among the most sensitive
  assets to this "cost of money."
- **The US dollar index (DXY).** DXY measures the dollar against a basket of other major currencies. A
  **strong, rising dollar** has broadly coincided with weakness in crypto and other risk assets; a
  **weakening dollar** with strength. It is an inverse *tendency*, not a mechanical seesaw.

:::note{type=info}
"Risk-on / risk-off" is just shorthand for the market's collective appetite. **Risk-on:** money flows
toward assets that pay off if things go well (stocks, crypto). **Risk-off:** money flows toward assets
that protect capital if they don't (cash, short-term government bonds, sometimes the dollar itself).
Crypto sits firmly at the risk-on end.
:::

None of these correlations is a law. They drift, they break for months at a time, and they are
relationships of *tendency and context*, not levers you can time trades with. What they give you is
the weather — a sense of whether you are trading with the wind at your back or in your face — and that
context is worth having before you ever look at a single chart.
