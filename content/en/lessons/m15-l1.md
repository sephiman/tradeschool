# Macro and capital flow

When you trade one coin you are also, whether you like it or not, trading its place inside a larger
tide. Capital does not sit still in crypto: it moves between Bitcoin, the big alternatives and the
long tail of small tokens in patterns that repeat often enough to be worth knowing — and irregularly
enough that you must never treat them as a clock. This module is about reading that tide: where money
sits, where it tends to flow next, and how the whole space breathes with the traditional economy
outside it.

## Bitcoin dominance

**Bitcoin dominance** is Bitcoin's share of the total crypto market capitalisation — its market cap
divided by the market cap of every coin added together. If the whole market is worth 2 trillion and
Bitcoin accounts for 1 trillion of that, dominance is 50%. It is a *ratio*, not a price, and that
single fact is the source of almost every mistake made with it.

Why does a share matter at all? Because it tells you *where the money is standing* rather than how
much of it there is. Fresh capital arriving in crypto tends to enter through Bitcoin first — it is
the most liquid, most widely listed and most understood asset, the reserve of the space and the
natural first purchase for someone stepping in. So money coming in usually shows up as *rising*
dominance before it shows up anywhere else; money getting adventurous shows up as *falling*
dominance as it spreads into everything else.

Here is the part that traps people, shown with numbers. Start with a 2-trillion market, Bitcoin at
1 trillion — dominance 50%. Now a broad sell-off hits: Bitcoin falls 10% to 0.9 trillion, but the
alts, which are riskier, fall 30%, from 1 trillion to 0.7 trillion. The total is now 1.6 trillion,
and dominance is `0.9 / 1.6 = 56%`. **Dominance rose from 50% to 56% while Bitcoin's own price fell
10%.** Nothing good happened to Bitcoin; the alts simply bled faster. Same arithmetic, and it is why
dominance and price are not the same statement.

- **Rising dominance** means Bitcoin is gaining share. That happens in two opposite moods: a
  **risk-off** flight, where traders sell alts for the relative safety of BTC, and the early part of
  a bull run, where fresh money enters through Bitcoin first.
- **Falling dominance** means capital is spreading out into alts — the classic backdrop for an "alt
  season."

Because dominance is a *share*, you must always read it next to whether the total market is rising or
falling. Rising dominance in a rising market is a Bitcoin-led advance — money entering, BTC leading.
Rising dominance in a falling market is a flight to BTC while alts bleed harder. Same number,
opposite mood; the total market is what tells them apart.

:::note{type=warning}
The classic beginner error is "dominance up means Bitcoin's price is up." The worked example above
shows why it is false: in a broad crash dominance often rises *because* everything is falling and the
alts are falling faster. Dominance is a statement about relative share, never about direction on its
own.
:::

## The ETH/BTC ratio

The **ETH/BTC ratio** is the price of Ether measured in Bitcoin rather than in dollars — if Ether is
2,000 dollars and Bitcoin is 40,000, then ETH/BTC is `2,000 / 40,000 = 0.05`.

Why quote one crypto in terms of another instead of in dollars? Because it strips out the move that
Bitcoin and the alts share. Almost everything in crypto rises and falls *with* Bitcoin to some
degree; pricing Ether in BTC cancels that common move and leaves only the part that is Ether
specifically gaining or losing ground against the reserve asset. And because Ether is the largest and
most liquid altcoin, that one pair works as a rough thermometer for risk appetite across the whole
alt complex.

The subtlety is that ETH/BTC can move opposite to Ether's dollar price. Suppose Bitcoin rallies to
50,000 (+25%) and Ether to 2,300 (+15%). Ether is up in dollars, yet ETH/BTC has *fallen* to
`2,300 / 50,000 = 0.046` — Bitcoin outperformed, so in BTC terms the alts lost ground. If instead
Ether had run to 2,600 while Bitcoin only reached 44,000, the ratio would be `2,600 / 44,000 = 0.059`,
clearly rising: Ether outperforming, appetite warming.

- A **rising ETH/BTC** means Ether is outperforming Bitcoin — usually read as risk appetite warming,
  money willing to step off the reserve asset onto the next rung.
- A **falling ETH/BTC** means Bitcoin is outperforming — capital retreating toward the safer end of
  the space.

It is a thermometer, not a trigger. It tells you the temperature of risk appetite; it does not tell
you the minute to act. "Warm" does not mean *buy now* — it means the environment currently favours
alts, and that environment can cool overnight.

## The rotation

There is a rhythm that repeats often enough to have a name. Money tends to move down the risk curve
in stages:

**BTC → ETH → large-cap alts → small-cap alts.**

- New money arrives and buys Bitcoin first; dominance rises.
- As confidence grows, profits rotate into Ether; ETH/BTC turns up.
- Then into the large, established alts — the top of the alt board.
- Finally, in the euphoric phase, into small, speculative tokens — the point at which the crowd feels
  most certain and risk is actually highest.

Why does capital travel in this order rather than all at once? Because appetite for risk builds
gradually, not instantly. Newcomers and returning money start with the asset they trust, then,
emboldened by profits, chase larger returns further down the curve. The final leg into small caps is
violent for a mechanical reason: those books are **thin**. A small-cap token has little capital
sitting in it, so the same inflow that nudges Bitcoin a few percent can double a micro-cap — which is
exactly why small tokens moon last, hardest, and most seductively.

When fear returns the rotation runs in reverse: money climbs back up the curve toward Bitcoin, and
the smallest, least liquid tokens bleed first and worst — the thin book that amplified the rise
amplifies the fall, and there is often no bid to sell into.

A stylised cycle makes the shape concrete. Dominance grinds up from, say, 40% into the high 40s as
new money pours into BTC. It stalls; ETH/BTC turns up as the first profits rotate into Ether. Large
caps catch a bid; then, in the final weeks, obscure small caps triple while the crowd declares the
"alt season" permanent — dominance bottoming just as risk peaks. Then something spooks the market,
and the whole ladder is climbed back down in a fraction of the time it took to go up.

:::note{type=warning}
This sequence is a **tendency, not a timetable**. It does not complete on every cycle, the stages
overlap, and plenty of "alt seasons" have been called that never arrived. Use it to understand
*where* risk is flowing — never as a promise that stage N is followed by stage N+1 on schedule.
Traders who bought small alts because "the rotation says they're next" have been left holding them for
years.
:::

## Crypto and the wider macro

Crypto likes to imagine itself as detached from the old financial world. It is not. Over the last
several years Bitcoin has traded largely as a **risk-on asset**: it rises when investors are happy to
hold risk and falls when they flee to safety, moving in loose sympathy with tech stocks rather than
against them.

Two macro dials matter most for that mood, and each works through a real mechanism, not superstition:

- **Interest rates.** Rates are the price of safe money. When central banks raise them, cash and
  short-term government bonds pay more for zero risk, so every speculative asset — crypto very much
  included — must compete against a higher risk-free return and looks relatively less attractive.
  Money tends to leave. Cuts, or the mere expectation of them, do the opposite. The clearest recent
  demonstration was 2022: central banks raised rates sharply to fight inflation, and crypto fell hard
  in lockstep with tech stocks. Crypto is among the most sensitive assets there is to this "cost of
  money."
- **The US dollar index (DXY).** DXY measures the dollar against a basket of other major currencies.
  A rising dollar tightens global financial conditions — dollars become scarcer and more valuable,
  and dollar-priced risk assets tend to weaken; a falling dollar loosens them and tends to coincide
  with strength. Through the same 2022 tightening, DXY ran to multi-decade highs while crypto and
  other risk assets sank. It is an inverse *tendency*, not a mechanical seesaw.

:::note{type=info}
"Risk-on / risk-off" is just shorthand for the market's collective appetite. **Risk-on:** money flows
toward assets that pay off if things go well (stocks, crypto). **Risk-off:** money flows toward assets
that protect capital if they don't (cash, short-term government bonds, sometimes the dollar itself).
Crypto sits firmly at the risk-on end.
:::

## Crypto's own wrinkle: it never closes

The macro relationships above are shared with stocks, but crypto reacts to them differently for one
structural reason: **it trades 24 hours a day, 7 days a week**, while stock and bond markets keep
office hours.

- **It reacts to macro prints in real time — including when nothing else can.** When a big US data
  release (an inflation print, a jobs report, a central-bank decision) lands during Wall Street
  hours, crypto jumps in the same instant as equities. But when news breaks at night or over a
  weekend — a bank failure, a geopolitical shock — crypto is often the *only* large, liquid market
  open, so it moves first and becomes a live proxy for global risk sentiment until traditional
  markets reopen. It sometimes overshoots, precisely because it is reacting alone.
- **Weekend drift.** Volume thins out on weekends: institutions, market makers and much of the
  professional flow step back. The same order size moves price further into a thin book, so weekend
  moves can be exaggerated and then partly unwind when full liquidity returns on Monday. Regulated
  venues that *do* close, such as CME Bitcoin futures, reopen with a **gap** to wherever spot drifted
  over the weekend — a gap that often gets filled in the first hours of the week.

## How this burns people

Everything above is a lens for *context*, and each part of it becomes a way to lose money the moment
you treat it as a signal to act on directly.

:::note{type=warning}
Three traps account for most of the damage:

- **Trading dominance as if it were direction.** Buying or shorting BTC "because dominance is rising"
  is trading a ratio you have not paired with the total market. Rising dominance in a crash and
  rising dominance in a healthy advance look identical on the dominance chart alone.
- **Betting the "alt season" is guaranteed.** The most expensive version is buying illiquid small caps
  *early* because the rotation "says they're next," then holding a thin, bleeding book for years while
  the stage that was promised never arrives.
- **Treating crypto as a safe haven.** The "digital gold" story says Bitcoin should rise when stocks
  fall. Over recent years the opposite has been closer to the truth: in the sharpest risk-off episodes
  BTC fell *with* equities, often harder. Using it to hedge a stock portfolio has tended to double the
  loss, not offset it.
:::

None of these correlations is a law. They drift, they break for months at a time, and they are
relationships of *tendency and context*, not levers you can time trades with. What they give you is
the weather — a sense of whether you are trading with the wind at your back or in your face — and that
context is worth having before you ever look at a single chart.
