# Trendlines and channels

Every level you have drawn so far has been horizontal. **m03-l2** explained why that works: a price is
a place in the order book, and orders rest at prices. Somebody's stop sits at 25,900, somebody's limit
buy sits at 25,900, and next week they are still there. The level remembers.

A **trendline** is the same idea turned on its side, and the turn costs it almost everything. Nobody's order
rests at "the price that rises 0.7% a day starting from last month's low". This lesson teaches the tool
anyway, because half of what you will read about charts is drawn with it — and it teaches it with the
weakness said out loud rather than buried: **the diagonal is the weakest instrument in this course.**

## What a diagonal actually claims

A horizontal level claims *a price mattered*. A diagonal claims something quite different: **a constant
rate of advance**. Not "buyers appear at 1,800" but "buyers appear about 20 higher each week than they
did the week before".

That is a claim about a *regularity of demand*, and the honest thing to notice is that **nobody is
obliged to maintain it**. There is no mechanism underneath. Compare the two:

- **Horizontal (m03-l2):** resting orders remember a price. If a shelf held twice, the reason it might
  hold a third time is that some of the same orders — and some new ones placed *because* it held — are
  still sitting there.
- **Diagonal:** nothing rests at a moving price. What you are pointing at is that buyers have so far
  been willing to pay a little more each time. The only thing carrying that forward is that they keep
  choosing to.

So a trendline is not a level with a slope. It is a *description of a rhythm that has held*, and the
rhythm is the thing that can stop without anything breaking.

:::note{type=warning}
Two traders, two lines. Give ten people the same chart and you will get ten trendlines, because the
line depends on which lows you decide count and whether you anchor on wicks or bodies. That subjectivity
is not a reason to skip the tool — it is a reason to be strict about the next section, and to hold what
a diagonal tells you more loosely than what a horizontal level tells you.
:::

## Drawing discipline

Three rules, and they exist to stop you from drawing what you already believe.

**1. Two touches propose, the third validates.** Any two points define a line — that is geometry, not
evidence. The third visit is the first piece of information: price came back to roughly where the line
said it would and turned there. Until then you have a candidate, not a trendline.

**2. Declare your anchor and keep it.** Wicks or bodies, never mixed. Mixing is how a line gets fitted
to a thesis: you take the wick where the wick is convenient and the body where the body is. **This
course anchors on closes**, so the lines you see in these figures run just under the closes of a rising
market and the wicks dip below them. That is a choice, stated so you can hold it against us — and it
is consistent with what the next section does with a break.

**3. A line you redraw every week was never a line.** If each new candle moves your anchor, what you
have is a curve fitted to the past, and it will keep fitting the past for as long as you keep refitting
it. The test is whether the line you drew a month ago still describes what happened since.

## Reading the break

Here the diagonal borrows its discipline wholesale, and nothing about it is new:

- **The body decides, not the wick** — exactly **m08-l1**'s rule for a horizontal level. A close beyond
  the projected line is the event; a wick through it is not.
- **Participation has to be behind it** — **m14**, volume and confirmation. A break nobody joined is
  the raw material of a fakeout.

If anything, both rules matter *more* here. A wick through a horizontal level at least tells you price
traded where a lot of orders were sitting. A wick through a diagonal tells you price traded where a line
somebody drew happened to be. **It is even less information than usual.**

The figure walks one. Price came back to the same rising line four times — near **9,550**, then
**9,935**, then **10,300**, then **10,570** — and turned each time, so by the third visit the line was
validated. The line is drawn from about **9,510** and, projected forward, reaches **11,270** at the
right edge. The decision is the candle that closes at **10,575** against a projected line at **10,740**
— a body clearly through it — on volume more than three times the quiet baseline of the rhythm before
it. The closing stretch, the half an exercise cuts off, carries price to **9,290**.

### Diagonal fakeouts

The obvious line is dangerous precisely because it is obvious. If everybody can see the same rising
support, everybody puts their stop just under it — and that is a pocket of resting sell orders sitting
at a known place, which is the whole of **m19-l2**. A move into it triggers them, and the burst of
forced selling can print a candle straight through the line before price closes back above.

The defence is the one you already have and is worth nothing if you do not use it: **wait for the close,
and check whether anyone came with it.** A break through a crowded line on thin volume is the setup a
stop hunt is made of.

## Channels: the rhythm with a band

Draw the trendline through one side of the move, then project a **parallel** from the opposite extreme,
and you have a **channel**. It is not a second discovery — it is the same line, copied.

What a channel adds is a *rhythm you can read on both sides*: price leaves the floor, reaches the
ceiling, comes back. The figure shows the construction both ways up, because there is nothing bullish
about it — a falling channel is the same object with the drawn line overhead.

It also ends in one of two ways, and they are not the same event. Price can **accelerate out through
the line it was heading for** — the far edge, in the direction the channel was already going — or the
**rhythm can give out through the line it was built on**. Neither is promised, and neither is a
property of the slope: a rising channel can lose its floor exactly as easily as it can run out of its
ceiling. Which line price left through is what tells you which of the two happened.

::figure{id=fig-m15-channel}

And here is the sentence that keeps this honest. **The channel describes a regularity that has held.
Trading it assumes the regularity persists, and that assumption is the bet.** Everything the chart shows
you is on the left of the decision; everything you are paid for is on the right of it. A channel is
unusually good at hiding that, because a well-drawn one looks like a machine.

## When not to use it

**In a market with no rhythm.** A range (**m03-l2**) has horizontal bounds and no rate of advance, so a
diagonal drawn across it is a line through noise. If you find yourself needing a very shallow trendline
to describe a sideways market, the horizontal level was the right tool and you have swapped a mechanism
for a slope.

**When the thesis came first.** This is the failure mode of the whole instrument. You believe the trend
is intact, so you find the anchors that keep it intact — a lower low here, a wick instead of a body
there — and the line obediently agrees with you. Fit the thesis to the line, never the line to the
thesis, and if you cannot draw one that survives rule 3, the answer is that there is no line, not that
you need a different pair of anchors.
