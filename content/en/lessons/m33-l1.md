# Footprint charts and volume profile

Every chart in this course puts **time** on the horizontal axis: one candle per period, one volume bar
per period, one CVD value per period. This last module of the block is about the two tools that rotate
that idea — they organise volume by **price** instead of by time — and about being straight with you
regarding what this course can and cannot generate for you to practise on.

Like m31, this lesson is **conceptual**: a quiz at the end, no chart exercise. The reason is explained
below rather than left as a gap.

## Footprint: what happened inside one candle

**What it is.** A **footprint** chart (also called a cluster or bid/ask chart) takes a single candle and
breaks it open. Instead of one body and two wicks, each price level *within* that candle's range gets a
row, and each row shows how much traded there — usually split into taker buy volume and taker sell volume
at that exact price.

**What you see that a candle hides.** m29 argued that a candle is an aggregate that discards who was
pressing. A footprint is what un-discarding it looks like. One hourly candle becomes something like:

- 60,300 → 40 bought / 15 sold
- 60,200 → 85 bought / 30 sold
- 60,100 → 120 bought / **310** sold
- 60,000 → 55 bought / 90 sold

That reads as a specific story rather than a shape: buyers pushed up, and at 60,100 they ran into
heavy selling that ate most of the effort. Notice this is the delta of m29, but resolved **by price
level** rather than summed over the period — the finest resolution in this block.

**Why it is a different shape of data.** A candle is one row of numbers per period. A footprint is a whole
*distribution* per period. That is the honest reason there is no exercise for it here: nothing else in
this course produces distribution-within-a-bar data, and the visualisation is not a time series at all.
Inventing one would mean teaching you to read a picture that does not match what real tools display.

## Volume profile: where the session actually traded

**What it is.** A **volume profile** applies the same rotation to a longer stretch — a session, a week,
an entire range — and plots total volume traded at each price as a horizontal histogram beside the chart.
Tall bars are prices where a great deal changed hands; short bars are prices the market passed through
quickly.

Two terms carry most of the value:

- The **POC** (point of control) is the single price with the most volume traded — the level where the
  market spent the most agreement.
- The **value area** is the band containing the bulk of the volume, conventionally 70%. Prices inside it
  are where the market was comfortable; prices outside it are where it was not.

**Why this is an upgrade on "support and resistance".** m03 and m08 taught levels as places price has
reacted to — drawn from highs and lows, honest but eyeballed, and vulnerable to the criticism that you
can always find a line that fits. A volume profile replaces the eyeball with a measurement: the POC is
not a line you chose, it is where the most trade *occurred*. A high-volume node is a price many
participants agreed on and therefore one they are likely to defend or re-engage with; a **low-volume
node** is a price the market rejected quickly, and price often traverses such gaps fast because there is
little history of interest there.

This does not make levels mystical or mechanical. It gives you a reason for a level beyond "it looks
like one" — which is precisely what m08's warning about drawing lines to fit your bias was asking for.

## How it connects to what you already do

Read a volume profile as a better-founded version of the structure work in block C:

- The POC and high-volume nodes are candidates for the levels m08 taught you to test, with evidence
  attached.
- Low-volume nodes explain the fast, uneventful travel between levels that a pure support/resistance view
  treats as inexplicable.
- The value area's edges give the range boundaries of m09's trading range a measured definition rather
  than a hand-drawn one.

And all the m08 caveats survive intact. A level with volume behind it is still only a level: it tells you
where a reaction is *more likely*, not that one will happen, and it does not remove the need for
confirmation, a stop, or sizing.

## Why this course does not generate these

:::note{type=info}
Stated plainly, because a course that quietly skips things is worse than one that says what it skips.

Every chart you have practised on here is generated as a **time series** — a value per period — and
rendered with the same candle/line engine. A footprint chart needs a volume *distribution inside each
bar*, and a volume profile needs volume aggregated *across* bars and drawn on a price axis. Neither is a
time series, and both need a visualisation this course does not have.

Building them credibly is its own project: the data model, the renderer, and — hardest — generating
distributions realistic enough that practising on them teaches you something transferable rather than the
quirks of a generator. Faking a plausible-looking picture would be worse than this honest gap, because
you would learn to read an artefact.

So this module teaches the concepts and the vocabulary properly, and sends you to real tools to see the
real thing. The same stance as m08's named-candle-pattern dictionary and m31's order book: the frontier
of the course is marked, not hidden.
:::

## Where to actually practise this

- **Volume profile** is the more accessible of the two and is widely available: TradingView includes
  fixed-range and session volume profile (the more advanced variants sit behind paid tiers), and most
  serious charting platforms ship some version.
- **Footprint** charts are specialist. Bookmap, ATAS, Sierra Chart and Exocharts are the names you will
  meet in crypto; TradingView's footprint is a paid add-on. All of them need a venue-level trade feed
  with taker-side labelling, which is why the tooling is narrower than for ordinary charts.
- Start with volume profile on a chart you already read. The POC of yesterday's session, drawn on today's
  chart, is a genuinely useful level and costs you nothing to try.

## Finer data, the same mistakes

The trap this whole block should inoculate you against is the belief that finer data means more certainty.
Order flow, the book, footprints: each is a genuine upgrade in resolution, and none of them converts a
probability into a guarantee. The traders who blow up with a footprint chart open do it the same way as
everyone else — position too large, stop too tight or absent, and a conviction that better data made them
right. Resolution is not certainty.

Two specific versions are worth naming, because both are this module's tools turned against their owner.
Treating the POC as a magnet that must be hit is the first: "price always returns to the POC" is folklore,
and a high-volume node is only a price with a history of agreement, which makes a reaction there more
likely than at a random price — a probability, not an appointment. Trading toward a POC with no other
reason, and with no stop because "it has to get there", is m26's material.

Over-reading a footprint is the second. At the finest resolution most of what you see is noise: a single
price level printing 310 sold against 120 bought is one moment of one candle, and countless such imbalances
resolve into nothing. Footprint reading is a professional skill built on top of everything in this course,
not a shortcut around it — and the more granular the data, the higher the ratio of noise to signal.
