# When the mistake isn't the chart

Most accounts are not destroyed by bad analysis. They are destroyed by a short list of ordinary
emotional patterns that repeat in almost everyone and feel completely reasonable while they are
happening. That last part is the trap: each of these mistakes arrives dressed as a sensible
decision, backed by a sentence you tell yourself and half-believe.

You do not fix this by trying to be calmer or more disciplined in the moment — the moment is exactly
when your judgment fails. You fix it with **pre-commitment**: a rule written down while you are flat
and calm, that removes the decision at the point where you are least able to make it well. The rest
of this lesson is four patterns and the rule that defuses each.

## Revenge trading

You take a loss. Immediately you want it *back* — not eventually, now. So you re-enter, usually
bigger, to recover it in one trade. The rationalization is always some version of **"I just need one
good trade to get back to even."**

The market did nothing to you and owes you nothing. "Even" is a number that matters to your ego, not
to the next setup, which has the same odds it always had — worse, actually, because you are now
sizing up out of emotion rather than reading the chart. Revenge trading is how a normal red day
becomes the day you talk about for years.

**The rule:** a fixed **daily loss limit** that ends the session when it is hit, no exceptions, plus
a short mandatory cooldown after any loss. Your next position is never larger than your normal size
— a loss is a reason to size *down or stop*, never up.

## Moving or removing the stop

Your stop sits at a small, planned loss. Price drifts toward it. Then: **"let me give it room — the
level is basically fine, it'll bounce."** You slide the stop further away, or you delete it.

You have just converted a small loss you chose into a large loss you didn't. The entire value of a
stop is that you set it when you were calm and had no position pressure; widening it in the heat
throws away the one honest decision you made. The only legitimate direction to move a stop is
*toward profit* — to breakeven, or to lock gains — never wider.

:::note{type=warning}
On a leveraged position, deleting your stop does not remove the exit — it just hands the exit to the
exchange. Your effective stop becomes the **liquidation** price: the forced close, and the worst and
most expensive way to leave a trade. "Giving it room" with no stop is quietly choosing liquidation
as your plan B.
:::

## Over-leverage after a winning streak

A few wins in a row feel like skill. They are usually mostly variance, but from the inside a hot
streak feels like you have finally "got it." So you raise your leverage and size because you are
**"reading it perfectly right now."** Then one perfectly ordinary loss, taken at the inflated size,
erases the streak and a chunk of what came before it.

Overconfidence is dangerous precisely because it feels like competence. The fix is to not let your
mood touch your sizing at all.

**The rule:** position size is a fixed function of account risk — the same small percentage risked
per trade whether you are on a five-win streak or a five-loss one. Good results are allowed to change
your confidence; they are not allowed to change your size.

## FOMO and chasing green candles

Price is running without you. Every candle you sit out feels like money walking away, and eventually
you snap: **"I'm missing it, I have to get in."** You buy late, at the worst price of the move, with
no entry logic and no invalidation — arriving exactly where the earlier buyers are getting ready to
sell to you.

FOMO trades have no plan by definition, because the reason for the trade is that the price went up,
not that a setup appeared. There is always another setup; the one you are chasing is the one you are
worst positioned in.

**The rule:** if you cannot state your entry, your stop, and your invalidation *before* you click,
it is not a trade, it is a chase — and you skip it.

## The same four, per style

These wear different clothes depending on your timeframe (see the trading-styles module), but they
are the same four mistakes.

- **Scalping:** revenge looks like machine-gun re-entries seconds apart, and FOMO is chasing every
  tick. The tell is your *frequency* climbing whenever you are frustrated.
- **Day trading:** revenge is blowing through the daily loss limit "to save the day," and the stop
  gets widened on an intraday level that "always holds."
- **Swing trading:** the slow-motion version. A stop gets nudged wider over *days* as a thesis
  quietly hardens into hope, and a good month tempts you to oversize the next swing. More time does
  not protect you — it just gives you more room to rationalize.

:::note{type=info}
Notice the common thread: all four decisions are made worst in the moment and best in advance. That
is why the fix is never "have more willpower." It is a rule you wrote down when nothing was at stake,
precisely so the frightened, greedy, or furious version of you does not get a vote.
:::
