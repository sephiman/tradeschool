# When the mistake isn't the chart

Most accounts are not destroyed by bad analysis. They are destroyed by a short list of ordinary
emotional patterns that repeat in almost everyone and feel completely reasonable while they are
happening. That last part is the trap: each of these mistakes arrives dressed as a sensible
decision, backed by a sentence you tell yourself and half-believe.

They also share a shape. In every one, a calm, sensible plan you made in advance gets quietly
overruled by an in-the-moment version of you that is running on fear, greed, or wounded pride — and
that version always has a reason ready. You do not fix this by trying to be calmer or more
disciplined in the moment, because the moment is exactly when your judgment fails. You fix it with
**pre-commitment**: a rule written down while you are flat and calm, that removes the decision at the
point where you are least able to make it well.

The rest of this lesson is four patterns. For each one we look at the same three things — **what it
is**, **why it happens** (the emotional machinery underneath), and **what it looks like with real
numbers** — and then the pre-commitment rule that defuses it.

## Revenge trading

**What it is.** You take a loss. Immediately you want it *back* — not eventually, now. So you
re-enter, usually bigger, to recover it in one trade. The rationalization is always some version of
**"I just need one good trade to get back to even."**

**Why it happens.** A loss hurts roughly twice as much as the same-sized gain feels good, so your
brain files a red number as a wound and demands you close it immediately. You have also silently
anchored on "even" — your starting balance — as the natural state of the world, so sitting below it
registers not as a neutral fact but as an ongoing threat you must end. The urgency is the tell: a
sound trade is never something you *need* to happen right now. A close cousin rides along with it —
**"a bounce is due"** — but the market has no memory of your two losses and owes you no third win.
That is the gambler's fallacy, and it is how you talk yourself into sizing up.

**In practice.** Your account is 2,000 USDT and you normally risk 1% — 20 USDT — per trade. You take
two stop-outs and you are down 40 USDT, a completely survivable 2% day. Then you decide to win it
back in one shot at five times your normal size, risking 100 USDT. It loses — ordinary trades lose
all the time — and now you are down 140 USDT, a 7% hole, and the pull to do it *again*, bigger, is
stronger than ever. That is the loop: each attempt to erase the loss deepens it and tightens the
grip.

**The rule:** a fixed **daily loss limit** that ends the session when it is hit, no exceptions, plus
a short mandatory cooldown after any loss. Your next position is never larger than your normal size
— a loss is a reason to size *down or stop*, never up.

## Moving or removing the stop

**What it is.** Your stop sits at a small, planned loss. Price drifts toward it. Then: **"let me give
it room — the level is basically fine, it'll bounce."** You slide the stop further away, or you delete
it.

**Why it happens.** The moment you are holding the position, you own its outcome, and the stop firing
means admitting the entry was wrong. To dodge that small hit to the ego, the mind quietly rewrites the
plan. Underneath sits the **sunk-cost trap**: you swap a *certain* small loss now for an *uncertain*
large one later — and because a sure loss stings so badly, taking a gamble to avoid it actually feels
like the smart move. The pressure of holding an open trade is precisely the bias the stop was built to
overrule; widening it in the heat throws away the one honest, pressure-free decision you made.
Averaging down — adding to the position to "defend the level" — is the same trap wearing a helpful
face: it piles more risk onto something already going against you.

**In practice.** You go long BTC at 60,000 with your stop at 59,400 — a planned 1% loss, your normal
single unit of risk (call it **1R**). Price reaches 59,400; instead of taking the small loss, you slide
the stop to 57,000 "to give it room." Price keeps falling and takes you out there anyway. You have now
lost 5% — **5R** — five times the loss you had actually agreed to accept, and none of the extra four was
a decision you made on purpose.

:::note{type=warning}
On a leveraged position, deleting your stop does not remove the exit — it just hands the exit to the
exchange. Your effective stop becomes the **liquidation** price: the forced close, and the worst and
most expensive way to leave a trade. "Giving it room" with no stop is quietly choosing liquidation
as your plan B.
:::

**The rule:** you never move a stop wider, ever. The only legitimate direction to move a stop is
*toward profit* — to breakeven once the trade works, or trailing it to lock gains as price advances. A
stop only ever moves in the direction of the trade going right.

## Over-leverage after a winning streak

**What it is.** A few wins in a row feel like skill. So you raise your leverage and size, because you
are **"reading it perfectly right now."** Then one perfectly ordinary loss, taken at the inflated
size, erases the streak and a chunk of what came before it.

**Why it happens.** A handful of results is dominated by variance, but the mind refuses to see it that
way. It credits the wins to your skill and would have blamed the losses on bad luck, so a run of noise
reads as proof you have finally "got it." Worse, overconfidence feels *identical* to competence from
the inside, so no internal alarm ever goes off to warn you. And the last five trades feel far more real
than the thousands-of-trades truth that short streaks mean almost nothing.

**In practice.** You put together five green trades at 1% risk each and your confidence is sky-high. On
the sixth you jump from 5× to 20× leverage and triple your size. The sixth trade is nothing special — an
ordinary loser — but at triple size it costs you 3R in one go, and the higher leverage means the
**liquidation** price now sits inside routine noise, so a move that used to be a small planned loss can
force you out entirely. One unremarkable trade, taken at the wrong size, gives back the whole streak.

**The rule:** position size is a fixed function of account risk — the same small percentage risked
per trade whether you are on a five-win streak or a five-loss one. Good results are allowed to change
your confidence; they are not allowed to change your size.

## FOMO and chasing green candles

**What it is.** Price is running without you. Every candle you sit out feels like money walking away,
and eventually you snap: **"I'm missing it, I have to get in."** You buy late, at the worst price of
the move, with no entry logic and no invalidation.

**Why it happens.** FOMO is really fear of *future regret*: the anticipated pain of watching it run on
without you feels larger, right now, than the real risk sitting in front of you. The herd amplifies it
— when price and everyone around you are moving and you are not, the move itself starts to feel like
evidence, when all it actually proves is that price already went up. You end up buying a feeling, not a
setup, and arriving exactly where the earlier buyers are getting ready to sell to you.

**In practice.** A coin rips +40% in an hour. Three green candles in, you cannot stand it and you buy
at the top, no stop, no plan. Minutes later it mean-reverts 15% — you are instantly underwater at the
single worst price of the move, and because you never defined an invalidation you have no idea whether
to hold or fold. Buying "a little" and *averaging up* as it keeps running is the same chase paid in
installments; it just means your worst-priced size is your largest.

**The rule:** if you cannot state your entry, your stop, and your invalidation *before* you click,
it is not a trade, it is a chase — and you skip it. There is always another setup.

## The pattern behind all four

Look at the four together and the same skeleton shows through every one.

- Each is your **calm, flat self being overruled** by an in-the-moment self running on a different,
  worse set of incentives.
- Each **arrives wearing a reasonable sentence** — "get back to even," "give it room," "I'm reading it
  perfectly," "I'm missing it" — that you half-believe precisely because it is tailored to what you
  already want to do.
- Each is **decided worst in the moment and best in advance**, which is why every fix is a rule
  written when nothing was at stake, not more resolve summoned when everything is.
- Each has a **tell you can feel** before it costs you: rising urgency, the itch to "fix" it right
  now, a flush of feeling unstoppable, the ache of being left out. That feeling is the alarm — the
  signal to fall back on the rule, not to override it.

This is why "just have more willpower" is not the answer. Willpower is the very thing that fails under
fear and greed; a pre-commitment works *because* it does not rely on you being strong at your weakest
moment.

## Which mistake your style invites

The four are universal, but your timeframe (see the trading-styles module) decides which ones you are
most exposed to.

- **Scalping** is most exposed to **revenge** and **FOMO**, because speed removes the pause that would
  let you catch yourself. Revenge looks like machine-gun re-entries seconds apart; FOMO is chasing
  every tick. The tell is your *frequency* climbing whenever you are frustrated.
- **Day trading** sits in the middle: revenge shows up as blowing through the daily loss limit "to
  save the day," and the stop gets widened on an intraday level that "always holds."
- **Swing trading** is most exposed to the **slow-motion** mistakes — stop-widening and post-good-run
  oversizing. A stop gets nudged wider over *days* as a thesis quietly hardens into hope, and a strong
  month tempts you to oversize the next swing. More time does not protect you; it just gives you more
  room to rationalize.

## Crypto-specific nuance

Everything above is real in any market. Crypto sharpens all of it.

- **24/7 means no forced cool-off.** A stock market closes; the closing bell is an external circuit
  breaker that ends a revenge spiral whether you like it or not. Perpetuals never close. At 03:00 on a
  Sunday the market is still open and your tilt still has somewhere to go, so the only closing bell you
  get is the one you build yourself — which is exactly what the daily loss limit is for.
- **High leverage makes a single tilt fatal.** In a cash stock account, a revenge trade or a FOMO
  chase costs you a bad trade. At **100×**, your margin cushion is about **1%**, so one tilted decision
  can reach the **liquidation** price and end the account before you have had time to calm down.
  Leverage compresses the distance between a single bad moment and a blown account to almost nothing.
- **Social FOMO is engineered.** Crypto lives on Twitter, Telegram, and Discord, where the feed is a
  stream of other people's screenshotted wins and almost never their losses. That turns FOMO from a
  private feeling about one chart into constant, amplified social pressure. A timeline showing only
  winners is not evidence about the trade in front of you.

:::note{type=info}
The common thread, one more time: all four decisions are made worst in the moment and best in advance.
That is why the fix is never "have more willpower." It is a rule you wrote down when nothing was at
stake, precisely so the frightened, greedy, or furious version of you does not get a vote.
:::
