# The PnL you actually keep

Every trade shows you a profit-and-loss number, but that number is not one thing. It changes meaning
depending on whether the position is open or closed, and the figure the exchange flashes in green is
almost never the figure that lands in your balance. This lesson is about the gap between the two.

## Realized vs unrealized PnL

**Unrealized PnL** is the profit or loss on a position that is still open. It is a mark-to-market
estimate: "if I closed right now, at this price, this is what I'd have." It moves tick by tick, and it
is not yours yet. Nothing has settled.

**Realized PnL** is what you lock in the moment you close the position — or the part of it you close.
Only then does the number stop moving and become an actual change in your balance.

The distinction sounds pedantic until it costs you money. A position showing +300 USDT unrealized is
not +300 USDT in the bank; it is a snapshot that the next candle can erase. Unrealized losses are
also what the exchange watches to decide whether your margin still covers the position: let them grow
far enough and the position is liquidated — force-closed — at a loss you did realize, whether you
wanted to or not.

:::note{type=info}
A useful habit: treat unrealized PnL as *weather* and realized PnL as *climate*. The weather is loud
and changes constantly; only the climate goes into your record.
:::

## Maker vs taker fees

Every fill costs a fee, and there are two rates.

- A **maker** order adds liquidity to the book — a limit order that rests and waits to be hit.
  Because it helps fill the book, it is charged the lower rate (sometimes zero, occasionally a
  rebate).
- A **taker** order removes liquidity — a market order, or a limit order that crosses the spread and
  executes immediately. It pays the higher rate.

The rates look tiny — often something like 0.02% maker, 0.055% taker — so beginners ignore them. That
is a mistake, for two reasons. First, the fee is charged on **notional** (position size × price), not
on your margin, so leverage multiplies it. Second, you pay it **twice**: once to open, once to close.

## Fees and funding both accumulate

A single round trip's fee is small. The problem is that costs stack:

- **Fees** scale with how often you trade. Ten round trips a day at taker rates is a real,
  compounding drag that has nothing to do with whether your calls were right.
- **Funding** is the periodic payment between longs and shorts on a perpetual (covered in its own
  module). Hold a position across several funding windows and those payments add up, quietly, in the
  background.

By the time you close, your **net result** is the gross price move, minus the open fee, minus the
close fee, minus (or plus) every funding payment you sat through. On a trade held for days, funding
and fees together can be the difference between a winner and a loser.

## The gross move is not what you keep

Here is the trap that catches almost everyone:

:::note{type=warning}
"The price moved +2%, so my PnL is +2%." No. The +2% is the **gross** move. What you keep is that
move minus two fees and any funding — and if you were leveraged, the percentage return on your
*margin* is a different number again. A +2% price move can easily net you well under 2%, and a
barely-positive move can net you a loss once the fees are paid.
:::

The gross price move is the raw material. Your net result is what survives after the exchange takes
its cut on both fills and funding settles up. Always reason about the number that actually reaches
your balance — the realized, net figure — not the green number on the open position.

## What to actually do

1. Read the open-position number as **unrealized** and provisional. It is not money until you close.
2. Before entering, know your **taker fee** and remember you pay it on entry *and* exit, on notional.
3. For anything held more than a few hours, factor **funding** into the expected result.
4. Judge a trade by its **net realized PnL**, never by the peak green number it showed while open.
