# The PnL you actually keep

Every trade shows you a profit-and-loss number, but that number is not one thing. It changes meaning
depending on whether the position is open or closed, and the figure the exchange flashes in green is
almost never the figure that lands in your balance. This lesson is about the gap between the two —
and how to compute the number you actually keep.

## Realized vs unrealized PnL

**What it is.** Unrealized PnL is the profit or loss on a position that is still open; realized PnL is
what you lock in the moment you close it, or the part of it you close.

**Why the distinction exists.** Unrealized PnL is a *mark-to-market* estimate — "if I closed right
now, at this price, this is what I'd have." The exchange recomputes it tick by tick against the mark
price so it always knows what your position is worth, but nothing has settled and no money has moved.
Realized PnL is the opposite: the instant you close, the estimate stops being an estimate and becomes
a final, booked change in your balance. The number stops moving because there is nothing left to mark
— the trade is over.

**In practice.** A position showing +300 USDT unrealized is not +300 USDT in the bank; it is a
snapshot the next candle can erase. And the asymmetry cuts against you: unrealized *loss* is exactly
what the exchange watches to decide whether your margin still covers the position. Let an unrealized
loss grow far enough and the position is liquidated — force-closed by the exchange — at a loss you
did realize, whether you wanted to or not. Unrealized is provisional in your favour but decisive
against you.

:::note{type=info}
A useful habit: treat unrealized PnL as *weather* and realized PnL as *climate*. The weather is loud
and changes constantly; only the climate goes into your record.
:::

## Maker vs taker fees

**What they are.** Every fill costs a fee, and there are two rates. A **maker** order adds liquidity
to the book — a limit order that rests and waits to be hit. A **taker** order removes liquidity — a
market order, or a limit order that crosses the spread and executes immediately.

**Why there are two rates.** An exchange is only usable if its order book is deep, and resting limit
orders are what make it deep. So the exchange rewards the trader who *supplies* that depth — the
maker — with the lower rate (sometimes zero, occasionally a small rebate), and charges the trader who
*consumes* it — the taker — more. The fee gap is the exchange paying for its own liquidity.

**In practice.** The rates look tiny — often something like 0.02% maker, 0.055% taker — so beginners
ignore them. That is a mistake, for two reasons. First, the fee is charged on **notional** (position
size × price), not on your margin, so leverage multiplies its bite relative to the capital you put
up. Second, you pay it **twice**: once to open, once to close. On a 20,000 USDT position at the
0.055% taker rate, that is 11 USDT to open and about 11 USDT to close — roughly 22 USDT, or 0.11% of
notional, gone before the price has justified anything. Enter as a maker instead and the open leg
drops to 0.02% (4 USDT), which over hundreds of trades is real money.

:::note{type=info}
**Crypto nuance:** a market order in *and* a market order out means you pay the taker rate on **both
fills**, not one. Stop-market orders and forced closes execute as takers too. If your style is "hit
the market to get in, hit the market to get out", price the round trip at two taker fees.
:::

## Funding accumulates while you hold

**What it is.** Funding is a periodic payment exchanged directly between longs and shorts on a
perpetual contract (it has its own module). Depending on the sign of the funding rate, either longs
pay shorts or shorts pay longs.

**Why it exists.** A perpetual never expires, so there is no settlement date to drag its price back
in line with spot. Funding is the tether: when the perp trades above spot the rate turns positive and
longs pay shorts, which discourages longs and pulls the perp back down; when it trades below spot the
payment flips. It is a nudging fee, not a fine — but you are on one side of it the entire time you
hold.

**In practice.** Funding is charged every window — commonly every 8 hours, i.e. three times a day —
on your position's notional. One window is trivial: at a 0.01% rate on a 20,000 USDT position, that
is 2 USDT. The problem is that it accrues *every* window you stay open. Hold that position for three
days and you have paid it nine times — 18 USDT — quietly, in the background, whether or not the price
moved at all.

:::note{type=info}
**Crypto nuance:** unlike a stock market that closes overnight and at weekends, a perpetual runs 24/7
and so does funding. Windows tick over at night, on Saturdays, on holidays. A position you "forget
about" over a long weekend keeps paying — funding does not take the weekend off.
:::

## Gross move vs true net — a worked example

The gross price move is the raw material. Your **net result** is what survives after the exchange
takes its cut on both fills and funding settles up:

`net = gross PnL − open fee − close fee − funding`

Put real numbers on it. You go **long 1 BTC at 20,000** USDT (so notional is 20,000) with **5×**
leverage, which means your margin is 4,000 USDT. You hold for three days and close at **20,400** — a
clean **+2%** move in your favour.

- **Gross PnL:** (20,400 − 20,000) × 1 = **+400 USDT**. This is the +2% everyone quotes.
- **Fees (taker on both sides):** open 20,000 × 0.055% = 11.00; close 20,400 × 0.055% = 11.22. Total
  **−22.22 USDT**.
- **Funding:** 3 days × 3 windows × (0.01% × 20,000) = 9 × 2 = **−18.00 USDT** (rate positive, and you
  were long, so you paid).

`net = 400 − 22.22 − 18 = 359.78 USDT`

So the "+2% move" is really **+1.80% on notional** (359.78 / 20,000), not +2%. And because you were
leveraged 5×, the return *on your margin* is a third number again: 359.78 / 4,000 ≈ **+9.0%**. Three
different percentages for one trade — gross-on-notional, net-on-notional, and net-on-margin — and only
the last is the change in your account.

Now flip it. Had that same three-day hold produced only a **+0.15%** move (+30 USDT gross), the ~22
in fees and 18 in funding would turn a "winning" trade into `30 − 22 − 18 = −10 USDT`. A green gross
number, a red balance.

## Where the PnL you expected went

:::note{type=warning}
**"The price moved +2%, so my PnL is +2%."** No. The +2% is the *gross* move; net is that minus two
fees and any funding, and the return on your *margin* is a different number again. A barely-positive
move can net a loss once the costs are paid — the worked example's +0.15% gross became a real loss.

**Ignoring the round trip.** Beginners price one fee, not two. Every position costs a fee to open
*and* to close, so at the 0.055% taker rate the price has to move about **0.11% of notional just to
break even** — before any funding. At 5× leverage that is 0.55% of your margin gone before you are
even.

**Funding bleed on a long hold.** A position you are quietly winning on still leaks funding every 8
hours, night and day. Over a multi-day hold that trickle — 18 USDT in the example, and much more on
bigger size or a high rate — can quietly eat a real slice of a modest gain, or deepen a loss you are
already sitting in.
:::

## What to actually do

1. Read the open-position number as **unrealized** and provisional. It is not money until you close.
2. Before entering, know your **taker fee** and remember you pay it on entry *and* exit, on notional —
   budget the round trip, not one leg.
3. For anything held more than a few hours, factor **funding** into the expected result, and remember
   it keeps accruing overnight and at weekends.
4. Judge a trade by its **net realized PnL** — `gross − fees − funding` — never by the peak green
   number it showed while open.
