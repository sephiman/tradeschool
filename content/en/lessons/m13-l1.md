# Fibonacci retracements

After a strong move, price rarely runs in a straight line — it pushes, pulls back, then (often)
continues. **Fibonacci retracements** are a set of horizontal levels traders draw across that move to
estimate *how deep the pullback will go* before the trend resumes. They are a measuring tool, not a
crystal ball: they tell you where a pullback *tends* to run out of room, not where it *must*.

## The levels

You draw the tool across one clean **impulse leg** — a strong swing from a low to a high, or a high
to a low — and it slices that range into ratios. Each ratio is the fraction of the impulse that price
has given back. Three matter for a near-beginner:

- **0.382** — a shallow pullback. The trend barely paused; strong trends often turn here.
- **0.5** — the midpoint. Price gave back exactly half the move.
- **0.618** — the deep "golden" pullback, the one most traders watch.

A "0.618 retracement" means price retraced 61.8% of the impulse before turning. To make that
concrete, take a clean leg up from **60,000 to 70,000** — a 10,000-point impulse. Each level is just
a fraction of that 10,000 measured *down from the high*:

- 0.382 → 70,000 − 0.382 × 10,000 = **66,180**
- 0.5 → 70,000 − 5,000 = **65,000**
- 0.618 → 70,000 − 6,180 = **63,820**

So a shallow pullback finds a floor near 66,180; a deep one drops toward 63,820. Beyond the pullback
range are **extensions** — 1.272 and 1.618 are the common ones — which project *where the next leg
might reach* if the trend pushes past the old extreme.

:::note{type=info}
Honest footnote on the numbers: 0.618 is the inverse of the golden ratio, and 0.382 is 1 − 0.618, so
those two really do come from the Fibonacci sequence. **0.5 does not** — it is just the halfway
point, kept on the tool because markets respect round halves, not because of any Fibonacci maths.
:::

## How to draw them

Pick one obvious swing — nothing subtle — and anchor the tool to its two ends:

- **In an uptrend:** anchor from the **swing low** (the start of the move) to the **swing high** (the
  end). The retracement levels then fall *below* the high, measuring how far a pullback drops back
  toward the low.
- **In a downtrend:** anchor from the **swing high** to the **swing low**. The levels fall *above*
  the low, measuring how far a bounce climbs back up.

The direction matters more than beginners expect: always drag from the start of the impulse to its
end. Draw our 60,000→70,000 leg backwards — as if it were a downtrend from 70,000 to 60,000 — and the
numbers don't just shift, they invert. The line you *called* 0.382 now sits at 63,820 and the one you
called 0.618 sits at 66,180. Your shallow and deep levels swap places, and every read you take off
them is wrong.

## The golden zone (0.5–0.618)

Most traders don't treat 0.618 as a single line but as the bottom of a **band** running from 0.5 to
0.618 — the "golden zone." On our example leg that band is **65,000 down to 63,820**, roughly 1,180
points wide.

Why that band and not some other? Because a healthy pullback in a real trend usually gives back
*between* a half and roughly two-thirds of the move: shallow enough that the trend is clearly still
intact, deep enough to shake out weak hands and let resting orders refill. A pullback that stalls
above 0.5 barely tested the trend; one that slices clean through 0.618 is warning you the move may be
over, not just resting.

## The honest why

Here is the part most tutorials skip. There is **no mechanism** by which a market is forced to
respect 61.8% of a move. Prices are set by orders, not by a spiral in a sunflower. So why do these
levels do anything at all?

- **They are self-fulfilling.** A huge number of traders — and the algos built to hunt their orders —
  watch the same 0.382 / 0.5 / 0.618 lines. When enough people rest buy orders near 63,820, buying
  near 63,820 becomes, briefly, a real reason price bounces there. The level works *because* it is
  watched.
- **They add confluence.** A fib level is worth far more when something else already sits there.
  Suppose our 0.618 at 63,820 lands a hair below a **round number** at 64,000 that everyone anchors
  to, and right on an old **support** shelf from last week. Now three separate reasons to buy stack
  in one spot. The fib did not create that level; it agreed with levels that already mattered — and
  the agreement is what gives it weight.

## Fibonacci's two false promises

Two mistakes turn Fibonacci from a useful map into a way to lose money, and each begins with something the
tool quietly seems to promise.

*The promise of precision.* Price will turn at 0.618. It won't — that is a *zone*, not a laser line, and
price routinely overshoots, wicks through and reverses, or stops short before it. If you place a stop one
tick beyond 0.618 (63,820) expecting a perfect turn, normal noise poking down to 63,600 will stop you out
and then reverse without you. Read the level as "a reversal is *more likely* around here," leave slack for
the overshoot, and let price confirm before you commit.

*The promise of significance.* Draw the tool on any swing and it paints three tidy lines, which makes each
of them look like it means something. But a level with *nothing else* near it — no prior support, no round
number, no reaction on the way up — is just a guess with a decimal attached. The fib does not create
support; it only flags where support might *coincide*. If 63,820 is floating in empty air, treat it as
noise, not a level.

## Crypto: round numbers and a market that never sleeps

Two things make fibs behave differently in crypto than in the textbooks.

- **Round numbers and fibs cluster.** Crypto traders anchor heavily to big round figures — 60,000,
  65,000, 70,000 on BTC; 1.00 or 0.50 on an alt — and resting orders pile up there regardless. When a
  fib level lands within a few ticks of a round number, the two reinforce each other and the zone
  gets *stickier*. That overlap is some of the cleanest confluence you will find: our 0.618 at 63,820,
  snug under 64,000, is exactly that kind of pairing.
- **A 24/7 market tests levels repeatedly.** Stocks get one shot at a level per session, then the
  close resets everything. Crypto never closes, so a single fib level can be probed over and over —
  through thin weekend books, through the quiet overnight hours — before it finally holds or breaks.
  Expect a level to be wicked through and reclaimed a few times rather than respected once and
  cleanly. That thin off-hours liquidity also makes overshoots past the level larger than you would
  see in a deep, regular-hours market — one more reason to treat 0.618 as a zone with slack, not a
  line.

## What to actually do

1. Find one clean impulse leg and anchor the tool start-to-end (low→high in an uptrend, high→low in a
   downtrend).
2. Watch the **0.5–0.618 golden zone** — that is where most trend pullbacks find a floor.
3. Ask what else lines up there. A fib alone is a guess; a fib on old support or a round number, with
   a real reaction, is a setup.
4. Wait for price to *react* at the level. The level is an alert, not a trigger. (What a genuine
   reaction looks like — the candle forms that record it — is the subject of m08-l2.)
