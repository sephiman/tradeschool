# Accumulation and distribution, phase by phase

The first lesson named the parts: the four-stage cycle, the trading range, the phases A–E, the spring
and the upthrust. This lesson slows down and walks a **single accumulation range from start to
finish**, phase by phase, with rough numbers so each stage is concrete — then flips the whole thing
over to show that **distribution is the same story upside down**. The point is not to memorise a
schematic; it is to see *why* each phase looks the way it does, so you can recognise the shape when
the numbers are different and the chart is messier.

## The example range

Take a coin that has fallen hard: from about **2,050 down to 1,800** over several weeks — a clean
downtrend, sellers in control. Somewhere near 1,800 the fall stops and price starts moving sideways.
That sideways stretch is the range we will walk through. Keep the two boundaries in mind: a **support**
that forms around **1,800** and a **resistance** around **1,930**.

The figure just below is one **generated instance** of exactly this shape, and this lesson's numbers
are its values rounded off: the two lines you will see drawn are that support and that resistance, and
everything we describe from here on happens between them. Read it left to right as we go: the downtrend
into the range, the churn, the stab below support that recovers, and the exit upward.

::figure{id=fig-m09-accumulation}

## Phase A — the prior trend stops

**What:** the downtrend runs out of sellers. The fall loses its momentum as it reaches **1,930** and
price enters the range; the first leg inside drops to **1,800** and finds buyers; the bounce carries
back up to **1,930** and stalls there. After that, the freefall is over and the two boundaries of the
range exist.

**Why:** a trend ends when the last motivated sellers have sold. The bounce off 1,800 is the first sign
that buyers can absorb everything thrown at them; the rally stopping at exactly the price the last one
stopped at says there is supply waiting above. Those two events *draw* the support and resistance
lines — the range does not appear from nowhere, it is built by a floor that holds and a ceiling that
caps.

**Example:** the entry into the range under 1,930, the drop to 1,800, and the bounce back to 1,930 are
three visible turns. Before phase A you had a falling market; after it you have a box.

## Phase B — the cause-building churn

**What:** the long, boring middle. For many sessions price travels between **1,800** and **1,930**,
touching both boundaries over and over without taking either out. Most people lose interest here.

**Why:** this is where the real work happens. A large buyer cannot lift 1,800 to 1,930 in one go
without spiking the price against themselves, so they buy patiently on the dips and let it drift back
up, over and over. Every cycle of the churn transfers a little more supply from tired sellers to
patient buyers. Wyckoff calls this *building the cause*: the more churn, the bigger the eventual move
it can pay for.

**Example:** if the coin spends six weeks oscillating in an 1,800–1,930 band on unremarkable
activity, that band is not "nothing happening" — it is the accumulation itself.

## Phase C — the spring and its test

**What:** near the end of the range, price stabs *below* support — down to **1,745**, 3% under the
1,800 floor — and then snaps back inside within three or four candles. No later dip comes near that
floor again. That stab-and-recovery is the **spring**; the quieter hold behind it is its **test**.

**Why:** the break below 1,800 is bait. A visible new low trips the stop-losses resting under
support and tempts fresh shorts, and those sell orders are exactly the last cheap supply a big buyer
needs to finish. The recovery back inside is the tell that the break was absorbed, not real. The test
that follows — a dip that refuses to go as low, on less activity — confirms there is little supply
left to shake out.

**Example:** the wick to 1,745 fires everyone's stops; price is back above 1,800 three candles later;
and from then on no dip ever loses the support again — the deepest of them all stops at **1,810**. The
spring did its job.

## Phase D — the move toward the edge

**What:** price stops visiting the floor. It settles into a narrow band around **1,870** — mid-range,
some 70 points above support — and stays there to the end of the range.

**Why:** once the supply is gone, buyers no longer need to be patient. Demand now overwhelms what
little selling appears, so each pullback is shallower than the last. The textbook draws that as
**higher lows** leaning on the ceiling; what always holds, and what you see here, is the other half:
**the floor of the range stops being touched.** A market that no longer needs to go down to 1,800 to
find buyers is a market that has changed hands.

**Example:** the difference from phase B is *where the dips happen*. Phase B goes down to 1,800 over
and over; phase D never once trades below 1,810.

## Phase E — the markup leaves the range

**What:** price breaks above 1,930 and holds. The range is behind it and the uptrend resumes — this
is the **markup**.

**Why:** the accumulation is finished. With supply exhausted, the first real push through resistance
meets almost nothing above, so it runs. The range was the cause; the markup is the effect.

**Example:** a close above 1,930 that does not fall back inside, followed by a trend up that reaches
**2,210** — above even the 2,050 where the whole fall started: the payoff for all that patient buying.

## Distribution: the same story upside down

Now flip every sign. **Distribution** is what a range does after an *uptrend*, and it is accumulation's
mirror in every phase. The figure below is, again, a generated instance of that shape with its own
prices: a range between a **support around 28,140** and a **resistance around 29,640**.

::figure{id=fig-m09-distribution}

Walk it the other way from the last one: price has climbed from about **25,400** and stalls. **Phase
A** is the rally losing its momentum as it reaches 29,640, a reaction down to 28,140, and a bounce that
stalls up top again — which draws the two lines. **Phase B** is the long churn between 28,140 and
29,640 where big holders quietly *unload* into eager buyers instead of building. **Phase C** is the
**upthrust**: price pokes above resistance to **30,590** — 3% beyond it — trapping breakout buyers,
then fails back inside. The decisive phase-C upthrust that marks the top has its own name — the
**UTAD** (upthrust after distribution) — to set it apart from smaller, inconclusive pokes that can
happen earlier in phase B. In **phase D** price stops visiting the ceiling and settles around
**29,000**: no later bounce reaches the resistance again, and the highest of them all stops at 29,350.
And **phase E** breaks below 28,140 into the **markdown**, down to **23,900**.

The symmetry is exact: a spring is a fake break *below* support that hints at a *markup*; an upthrust
is a fake break *above* resistance that warns of a *markdown*. Same trap, opposite direction — a
visible break attracts the orders a big player needs to finish on the other side.

## When the schematic lies

:::note{type=warning}
The phases look clean in a diagram and clean in hindsight, and that is the danger. Two traps in
particular:

- **Ranges break both ways for real.** A stab below support that keeps falling was never a spring — it
  was just a breakdown. You cannot know which one you are looking at *while it happens*; you only earn
  the label "spring" once price recovers and holds. Treat an unconfirmed break as a question, not an
  answer.
- **Hindsight bias.** Once a range has resolved, it is easy to point at the low and say "obvious
  spring". Live, that same wick looked identical to the start of a crash. Naming the phase after the
  fact teaches you nothing; the skill is reading the prior trend and the recovery *before* the markup
  or markdown that would prove you right.
:::

## Manufacturing a spring in a thin book

Crypto sharpens phase C in particular. In a **thin alt-coin book**, it takes very little size to push
price through a support or resistance level, so a **stop-hunt wick** — a fast spike that trips the
cluster of stop-losses resting just beyond the boundary and can even force-liquidate over-leveraged
positions — is easy to manufacture and easy to mistake for a genuine spring or upthrust. The wick can
be violent and last minutes.

Two consequences. First, the *depth* of a spring or upthrust means less in crypto than the
*recovery*: a deep wick on a thin book is cheap to produce, so demand a clean snap-back inside before
you trust it. Second, because the market runs **24/7** with no closing bell, these tests often happen
in low-liquidity hours — weekends and dead time between sessions — when a small order moves price
furthest. A "spring" printed at 3 a.m. on a Sunday deserves more suspicion, not less.
