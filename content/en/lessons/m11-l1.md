# Oscillators: RSI and MACD

An **oscillator** is an indicator, derived from price, that moves within a bounded range and tries to
describe *momentum* — how fast and how one-sidedly price has been moving lately, not where it sits.
Because the range is fixed (0–100 for RSI, a zero line for MACD), you can compare today's reading to
last month's on the same scale, which is the whole appeal. Two are everywhere in crypto: **RSI** and
**MACD**. Both are genuinely useful once you know exactly what they compute, and both are misread
constantly by people who treat them as buy and sell buttons.

## What RSI actually computes

**RSI** (Relative Strength Index) compares the average size of recent *up* moves to the average size
of recent *down* moves, over a lookback period (the default is **14** bars), and squeezes the result
onto a **0–100** scale. Mechanically it forms a single ratio — **RS = average gain ÷ average loss** —
and then maps that ratio onto the scale.

:::note{type=info}
**RS** = average gain over the lookback ÷ average loss over the lookback.

**RSI** = 100 − 100 ÷ (1 + RS).

If ups and downs are equal, RS = 1 and RSI = 50. If up moves are three times the size of down moves,
RS = 3 and RSI = 100 − 100 ÷ 4 = **75**. As losses shrink toward zero, RS explodes and RSI is dragged
toward 100.
:::

- If almost every recent bar closed up, average losses are near zero and RSI pins near **100**.
- If almost every recent bar closed down, average gains are near zero and RSI sits near **0**.
- When gains and losses are roughly balanced, RSI hovers around the middle, near **50**.

So RSI is a *relative* measure, and it is bounded for a mechanical reason: RS can range from 0 to
infinity, and the `100 − 100 ÷ (1 + RS)` mapping folds that whole range neatly into 0–100. It is not
asking "is price high?" — it is asking "how lopsided have the last 14 bars been?"

Worked reading: say the last 14 bars averaged a **0.9%** gain on the up bars and a **0.3%** loss on
the down bars. Then RS = 0.9 ÷ 0.3 = 3, and RSI = 100 − 100 ÷ (1 + 3) = **75** — comfortably
"overbought", purely because up bars have been three times the size of down bars. Notice what that
number does *not* say: nothing about whether 0.9% is a big move, nothing about price being high or
low in absolute terms — only that recent action has leaned up by 3-to-1. The conventional bands are
**above 70 = overbought** and **below 30 = oversold**, but read on, because those two words are
exactly where most beginners lose money.

## What MACD actually computes

**MACD** (Moving Average Convergence Divergence) is built from three pieces:

- The **MACD line**: the difference between a fast EMA (12) and a slow EMA (26) of price. When the
  fast average is above the slow one the line is positive — price is pulling away from its recent
  baseline.
- The **signal line**: a 9-period EMA of the MACD line — a smoothed, slower version of it.
- The **histogram**: the MACD line minus the signal line, drawn as bars. It is simply the *gap*
  between the two lines.

Why is a *difference of averages* a momentum read at all? An EMA weights recent bars more heavily, so
the fast (12) EMA reacts to new prices faster than the slow (26) EMA. The gap between them therefore
measures how quickly price is pulling away from its own slower baseline — a rate of change, not a
level. That is exactly what "momentum" means here.

Worked reading: in a rally the 12-EMA sits at 30,500 and the 26-EMA at 30,000, so the MACD line is
**+500** — price is well above its slower baseline. The signal line (the 9-EMA of that MACD line)
lags at **+420**, so the histogram is 500 − 420 = **+80**. If the next bars push the MACD line to
+560 while the signal only crawls to +470, the histogram *grows* to +90: the move is accelerating. If
instead the MACD line stalls at +500 while the signal catches up to +480, the histogram *shrinks* to
+20 — price may still be near its highs, but the push behind it is fading.

Read it as momentum, not as level. A histogram that is *growing* means the move is accelerating; a
*shrinking* histogram means it is losing steam even while price is still rising.

:::note{type=info}
Both indicators are just arithmetic on past prices. They contain no information price does not already
carry — they *repackage* it into a momentum read. That is honestly useful for spotting acceleration
you might miss on the raw candles, and it is also why they can never "predict" anything on their own.
:::

## Reading MACD crossovers

The MACD has two completely different events that both get called "a cross", and conflating them is
the most common way this indicator gets misread. One is a cross of the **signal line**; the other is a
cross of the **zero line**. They are not the same size of news.

### The signal-line cross

The **signal-line cross** is the MACD line crossing its own 9-period EMA — the moment the histogram
flips sign. Because the signal line is just a lagging average of the MACD line, this cross has a
purely mechanical meaning: **the MACD line crossed above its signal precisely because the MACD line
turned up**, and crossed below because it turned down. It is a statement about the *last few bars of
momentum*, nothing more.

Worked reading, continuing the numbers above. An uptrend pauses: the 12-EMA slips to 30,360 while the
26-EMA has caught up to 30,000, so the MACD line has fallen to **+360**. The signal line — the 9-EMA
of the MACD line, still averaging in the bigger readings from the rally — sits higher at **+392**, so
the histogram is 360 − 392 = **−32**. The histogram is *negative in an uptrend*: momentum has dipped
below its own recent average. Two bars later price resumes: the 12-EMA jumps to 30,430 while the
26-EMA has only crept to 30,020, so the MACD line is back to **+410**. The signal line averages nine
bars, so two good bars barely move it — it reads **+393**. The histogram is 410 − 393 = **+17**: it
has flipped positive. That flip *is* the bullish signal-line cross.

Notice the number that never moved: the MACD line stayed positive the whole time (+360, then +410).
The 12-EMA never fell below the 26-EMA. Nothing about the trend changed — only the short-term push
inside it wobbled and recovered.

### The zero-line cross

The **zero-line cross** is a bigger, higher-order event. The MACD line *is* the fast EMA minus the
slow EMA, so the MACD line at zero means those two averages are **equal**, and the MACD line crossing
zero means the **12-EMA has crossed the 26-EMA outright**. That is m10's golden cross (or death
cross), on the 12/26 pair, reported in the oscillator pane: a change of trend *regime*, not a wobble
within one.

Worked reading: a downtrend has the 12-EMA at 29,400 and the 26-EMA at 29,900, so the MACD line reads
**−500** — the recent average is decisively below the older one. Price then turns and rallies hard.
The 12-EMA, weighted toward the new prices, climbs faster: at 29,850 against a 26-EMA that has only
reached 29,700, the MACD line is **+150**. It crossed zero on the way, and what that crossing records
is that the average price of the last 12 bars is now *above* the average of the last 26. The
short-term trend has taken over the longer-term one.

Keep the two straight by what has to happen for each:

- **Signal cross:** the MACD line changed *direction* relative to its own average. Common, fires
  often, and can happen many times inside one trend without the trend ever being in question.
- **Zero cross:** the two EMAs behind the MACD line changed *order*. Rarer, later, and it takes a
  sustained move to produce — the same lag m10 showed for any MA cross. It confirms a regime that is
  already underway; it never calls the turn.

In a clean reversal you see them in that order: the signal cross first, then — if the move is real —
the zero cross some bars later. That ordering is why the signal cross is the earlier, noisier clue and
the zero cross the later, heavier confirmation.

### The histogram shrinking before a cross

You do not have to wait for the flip. The histogram is the *gap* between the two lines, so a
shrinking histogram is the cross approaching: **the histogram reaching zero and the cross happening
are the same event.**

Play it out. The MACD line stalls at +500 while the signal keeps catching up: the histogram prints
**+80**, then **+50**, then **+20**. Price may still be grinding to new highs the whole time — but
the bars behind it are getting shorter, and if the MACD line stays flat while the signal keeps
climbing, the next flip is arithmetic, not opinion. Price making new highs while the histogram makes
smaller ones is a **MACD-histogram divergence** — the same disagreement between price and momentum
that m12 builds on, caught one step earlier, at the histogram rather than the swing highs. It is a
clue that the move is decelerating. It is not a command to sell.

### A crossover is not a standalone signal

Everything above describes what a cross *computes*. What it is *worth* depends entirely on where it
happens — and this is the same point m08-l2 makes about a candle and m13 makes about a fib level.

::figure{id=fig-m11-cross-in-trend-vs-range}

- **The same bullish signal-line cross inside an established uptrend** is *continuation* evidence: a
  pullback turned the histogram negative, that pullback is now over, the trend that was intact the
  whole time is resuming, and the cross tells you when. Note on the left panel that this happens
  several times in one trend without the trend ever being in doubt — which is the point. The cross is
  worth something there because the trend is doing the work and the cross is only timing it.
- **The same bullish signal-line cross inside a range** is *whipsaw*. In a sideways market the MACD
  line saws back and forth across zero, so crosses fire again and again, each one looking exactly
  like the one on the left, and none of them leads anywhere. This is precisely m10's whipsaw warning
  about MA crosses in a range — of course it is, since the MACD *is* two moving averages.

So: a crossover is not a standalone signal, any more than a level or a divergence is. Read the
context first — trend or range, and which side of zero the MACD line is on — and only then ask what
the cross adds. A cross that agrees with a trend you had already identified is confirmation; the
identical cross in a range is noise with a shape.

## The overbought/oversold lie

Here is the single most expensive misunderstanding in this module. Beginners learn "RSI over 70 =
overbought = sell" and "under 30 = oversold = buy", and then bleed money to it for months.

**In a strong trend, an oscillator can stay pinned at its extreme for a very long time.** A market in
a powerful uptrend will push RSI above 70 and *keep it there* for days or weeks, printing new highs
the whole way. Every bar that "should" have been the reversal was just another leg up.

::figure{id=fig-m11-overbought-lie}

The chart above is precisely this trap: RSI vaults above 70 near the *start* of the move and simply
stays there, printing overbought reading after overbought reading while price climbs the entire time.
Overbought does not mean expensive and it does not mean sell — it means momentum has been strongly
one-sided, which in a trend is exactly what you would expect, and is often a sign of *strength*, not
exhaustion. The same is true upside-down: a hard downtrend parks RSI below 30 and keeps it there.

## How this burns people

Play the mistake out with numbers. A low-cap alt rips from **1.00** to **1.60** in two weeks; RSI
crosses 70 at around 1.15 and never looks back, sitting between 78 and 85 the whole climb. A beginner
following the rule shorts at **1.20** ("it's overbought"), gets stopped out at 1.28; shorts again at
**1.35**, stopped at 1.44; shorts a third time at **1.50** with a bigger size to "make it back", and
the push to 1.60 takes the account. Every entry was "correct" by the RSI rule. The trend paid none of
them.

:::note{type=warning}
"RSI is over 70, so I should sell (or short)" is not a trade — it is a way to fight a trend and get
run over. The same trap works in reverse: shorting into a strong downtrend because "RSI is oversold"
just hands your stop to the trend. An extreme reading tells you the move is one-sided; it never tells
you the move is about to end. The reading in the example was never *wrong* — it truthfully said the
move was lopsided — it just never said the move was *done*.
:::

## Crypto-specific nuance

Crypto makes this trap worse, not better.

- **24/7 markets don't reset.** A stock gets a daily close and an overnight gap that let momentum
  cool off; a crypto perpetual trades every hour of every day, so a parabolic move can hold RSI above
  80 for a week straight with no session break to interrupt it.
- **Thin alt books pin the extreme longer.** In a low-cap alt, a modest amount of buying moves price
  a long way, so a narrative-driven rip can keep RSI at an extreme far higher and far longer than any
  large-cap would — the exact conditions that trap the "sell the overbought" reflex.
- **Funding is the more honest exhaustion clue.** When you genuinely want to know whether an up-move
  is overheating, watch **funding** rather than RSI: in these euphoric runs funding turns sharply
  positive (longs paying shorts to keep the position open), and *that* crowding is a real sign of a
  stretched trade in a way that "RSI is 82" simply is not.
- **The reversal, when it comes, is violent.** The same thin book that let price levitate makes the
  eventual drop brutal — so the trader who was "right" about overbought a week too early is rarely
  still solvent to enjoy being right.

## So what do you actually do with them?

- Use RSI to describe **context**, not to trigger entries. Overbought inside a range is meaningful;
  overbought in a rip-roaring trend is normal.
- Watch the MACD **histogram for shifts in momentum** — a shrinking histogram warns that a move is
  decelerating before price turns, and warns you a cross is coming. That is a clue, not a command.
- **Weigh a MACD cross by which line was crossed and by the regime it happened in.** A signal-line
  cross is a momentum wobble; a zero-line cross is the 12- and 26-EMA changing order, a regime
  change. Either one in a trend is confirmation; either one in a range is whipsaw.
- Treat crosses and band-crossings as *confirmation for a thesis you already hold* from structure and
  levels, never as standalone signals. An oscillator that agrees with a level you were already
  watching is worth far more than one shouting in open space.
