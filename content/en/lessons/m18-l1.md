# Reading tokenomics

Two tokens can trade at the same price and be worth wildly different amounts — and one of them can be
quietly set up to fall for reasons that have nothing to do with the chart. **Tokenomics** is the
supply side of that story: how many tokens exist, how many are actually tradable today, and how many
more are scheduled to arrive. You do not need a spreadsheet to trade, but you do need to read these
few numbers before you trust a price. Every one of them is public, and every one of them can override
a clean chart.

## Three supply numbers, not one

People say "supply" as if it were a single figure. There are three, and confusing them is where the
mistakes start.

- **Circulating supply** is the number of tokens tradable *right now* — in wallets and on exchanges,
  free to be bought and sold. This is the float that actually sets the price, because it is all the
  market can currently trade.
- **Total supply** is everything minted so far, including tokens that are locked, vesting or held
  back (team, treasury, investors) and therefore not yet on the market. Minted, but not free.
- **Max supply** is the ceiling: the most tokens that will *ever* exist, once every scheduled
  emission has happened. Some tokens have a hard cap (like Bitcoin's 21 million); others have no cap
  at all and inflate indefinitely.

**Why the gap matters.** The distance between circulating and max is future supply that has not hit
the market yet — but, on a published schedule, it will. If 200 million tokens circulate and the max
is 2 billion, then 1.8 billion tokens — nine times the entire current float — are still queued to
arrive. Every one of them is a future seller the price does not yet have to absorb, but eventually
will.

## Emission and inflation schedules

**What.** New tokens usually do not appear all at once. An **emission schedule** is the pre-announced
plan for releasing them: staking rewards, mining, ecosystem funds, team and investor vesting. The rate
at which circulating supply grows is the token's **inflation**.

**Why it is a headwind.** Unlike a central bank's, this inflation is written down in advance — and it
dilutes every existing holder mechanically. Suppose 500 million tokens circulate at $1.00 (a $500
million market cap) and the schedule emits another 150 million over the next year. That is 150 / 500 =
**30% inflation**. If no fresh money arrives — the market cap stays $500 million — the price is simply
500 / 650 = **$0.77**, a 23% fall, and not one holder had to change their mind. Dilution alone did it.
Put the other way round: just to hold the price flat at $1.00, the market has to absorb 30% more
tokens, which means $150 million of new buying to stand still.

That headwind is invisible on a candlestick; you only see it if you read the schedule. A token quietly
inflating 30% a year is fighting a 30% annual drag that never shows up as a pattern.

## Market cap vs fully-diluted valuation

Price alone tells you almost nothing about size. Two numbers do:

- **Market cap = price × circulating supply.** What the *tradable* float is worth today.
- **Fully-diluted valuation (FDV) = price × max supply.** What the whole thing would be worth if
  every token that will ever exist were already trading at today's price.

**Worked example, one token.** Say token XYZ trades at **$0.50**, with **200 million** circulating and
a **2 billion** max supply.

- Market cap = 0.50 × 200,000,000 = **$100 million**.
- FDV = 0.50 × 2,000,000,000 = **$1 billion**.

Same token, same price, same instant — and two valuations that differ by 10×. Market cap is the real,
present size. FDV is the hypothetical ceiling, a "what if everything were out" figure. They are **not**
the same number, and the distance between them is a warning label.

:::note{type=info}
A quick sanity check: divide FDV by market cap. Here it is 1,000 / 100 = **10**, so only about a tenth
of the tokens are circulating and the other nine-tenths are still waiting in the schedule. A ratio near
1 means almost everything is already out; a ratio of 10 means the market is holding up the price of a
thin float while 90% of the supply waits offstage.
:::

## The low-float, high-FDV trap

Here is the setup that catches beginners. A new token launches with only a **small slice of its supply
circulating** — a "low float". Because the circulating supply is tiny, it takes very little buying to
push the price up, and the *market cap* still looks modest. But the *FDV* — price times the full future
supply — is enormous.

**Worked example.** A token launches with a 1-billion max supply but only **8% circulating** — 80
million tokens — and gets bid to **$3.00**.

- Market cap = 3 × 80,000,000 = **$240 million** — a modest-looking mid-cap.
- FDV = 3 × 1,000,000,000 = **$3 billion**.

The remaining **920 million tokens (92%)** are scheduled to unlock over the next two to three years.
For the price to merely *hold* at $3, the market has to absorb 920,000,000 × $3 = **$2.76 billion** of
new supply — more than **eleven times** the $240 million float that is holding the price up today.

The chart looks like a healthy, affordable coin. The tokenomics say something else: for the price to
survive, the market has to keep absorbing wave after wave of new supply as it unlocks. A modest market
cap sitting under a gigantic FDV is not a bargain — it is a bill that has not arrived yet.

:::note{type=warning}
"The market cap is only 50 million, it's cheap" means nothing on its own. If the FDV is 2 billion,
you are looking at a token where about 97% of the supply is still to come. Always check the FDV before
you call something cheap.
:::

## Unlock calendars and your chart

**What.** Locked tokens come free on a schedule called the **unlock calendar** (or vesting schedule).
Most of the time nothing dramatic happens — supply trickles out and the market absorbs it. But a
**large unlock** — a "cliff", where a big tranche releases on a single date — can put more supply onto
the market in a day than normal trading absorbs.

**Why it is a shock, with numbers.** Say a token has 100 million circulating at $2.00 and trades about
8 million tokens a day. A cliff now unlocks a 20-million-token investor tranche on one date — a **20%
jump in the float overnight**, and **two and a half days of normal volume** dropped onto the book at
once. Even if only a third of that tranche actually sells, that is ~6.7 million tokens hunting for bids
against a book that normally clears 8 million in a *day* — almost a full extra day of selling compressed
into hours. Price gets pushed down by the arithmetic of supply, not by anything the pattern did wrong.

This is why tokenomics belongs inside your technical analysis, not beside it. A clean breakout, a
supportive trend, a textbook setup — all of it can be **invalidated by a scheduled unlock** the chart
had no way of showing. New supply arriving to be sold is a fundamental force that overrides the
pattern.

:::note{type=warning}
Before trading any newer token, check its unlock calendar. A large unlock landing in the next few days
is a reason to distrust an otherwise clean bullish setup: the extra supply can cap or reverse the move
regardless of what the pattern "should" do.
:::

## What "cheap" hides

:::note{type=warning}
**Mistaking market cap for FDV.** The two numbers sit side by side on every data site, and beginners
read whichever is smaller as "the size". Worse is comparing across tokens inconsistently — quoting one
coin's low *market cap* next to another's *FDV* and concluding the first is smaller or cheaper. That is
comparing a down-payment to a sticker price. Compare like with like: market cap to market cap, FDV to
FDV.

**"Small cap = cheap" and "low price = cheap".** Neither the market-cap headline nor the price tag
tells you cheapness on its own. A token at **$0.001** is not cheaper than one at **$50** — the team
simply chose a bigger supply. At $0.001 with a 100-billion max supply the FDV is $100 million; the $50
token might have a 1-million max supply and an FDV of $50 million. The three-decimal "penny coin"
*looks* like a bargain and is the more expensive network. Price per token is arbitrary; only market cap
and FDV — measured against what the thing actually does — say anything about value.

**Buying right before a cliff.** A pristine bullish chart three days before a large cliff unlock is not
an opportunity, it is a trap with a date on it. The supply shock is already on the public calendar, and
the chart cannot price something it cannot see. Standing in front of a scheduled unlock because "the
setup is clean" is buying the last dry day before a flood you were handed the forecast for.
:::

## The unlock calendar is public

Traditional markets have lockup expiries too — insiders cannot dump on day one of an IPO — but crypto
makes the mechanism far sharper, and public.

- **The calendar is exact and everyone has it.** Unlock dates and tranche sizes are published to the
  day, often verifiable on-chain. Because the shock is *known*, it tends to be **front-run**: price
  frequently weakens in the days *before* a big unlock as informed players position ahead of it, not
  just on the day itself. The move can be half over before the tokens actually move.
- **24/7, no closing bell.** The unlock hits a live order book at whatever hour it lands. There is no
  overnight session to digest the new supply and no halt to slow a slide — the same conditions that
  make liquidation cascades vicious make unlock dumps vicious.
- **Thin alt books swallow badly.** A tranche a deep BTC-scale book would absorb can gap a small alt
  several percent in minutes. The thinner the book, the more a scheduled unlock behaves like a supply
  shock rather than a slow bleed.
- **Emissions are a constant background sell.** Separate from discrete cliffs, staking and mining
  rewards are minted continuously, and the farmers earning them routinely sell to cover costs. That is
  a steady, mechanical stream of new supply hitting the book every day, independent of sentiment.

The single idea to carry out of this: a scheduled unlock is a **supply shock the chart cannot show**.
When a large one is near, it overrides the technicals — treat the pattern as provisional until the new
supply has been absorbed.

## A tokenomics check before you buy

1. Look up **circulating, total and max supply** — most data sites list all three.
2. Compute **market cap and FDV** and take their ratio. A huge gap means heavy future dilution.
3. Read the **emission/inflation rate**: is supply growing fast, and who receives it?
4. Check the **unlock calendar** for large releases near your trading horizon, and treat a nearby
   cliff as a reason for caution the chart cannot give you.
