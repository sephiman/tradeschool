# The reaction: reading candles at a level

Over and over this course tells you to "wait for price to react at the level" — at a broken support on the retest (m08), at a Fibonacci pullback (m13 step 4), at the spring that tests a Wyckoff range (m09). This lesson finally says what a *reaction* looks like.

The trick is to stop thinking of candles as a catalogue of shapes to memorise and start reading them as the **footprint of order flow** — a compact record of who tried what in that period, and who won. Almost everything you need reduces to two mechanics: a **rejection** (price was pushed to a price and thrown back) and an **overrun** (one side steamrolled the other). Plus a third category that matters just as much: candles that mean **nothing**, and knowing not to trade them.

## Rejection: the wick that lost a battle

**What it is.** A rejection is a long wick with a small body — the classic *hammer* (long lower wick) or *shooting star* (long upper wick). The body is where price opened and closed; the wick is how far it travelled and was forced back.

**Why it matters.** A long wick is the fossil of a lost battle. Price was pushed to a level, met enough opposing orders to absorb the push, and returned. The side that drove price into the wick *tried and failed*; the side that absorbed it *won that exchange*. That is real information about order flow — but only about the exchange at the wick's tip.

**In practice.** Price falls to a support at **61,000**, wicks down to **60,400**, then closes back at **61,200**. That is a ~600-point lower wick under a tiny body: sellers pushed price ~1% below support, buyers absorbed the whole push and drove it back above. The wick records sellers losing the fight *at 61,000* — a price that, as we'll see, has to matter for the wick to mean anything.

## Overrun: the body that steamrolled

**What it is.** An *engulfing* candle: a large body whose range fully covers the prior candle's body — the previous bar's whole story invalidated in one period.

**Why it matters.** Where a rejection is a battle *won at an extreme*, an overrun is one side simply steamrolling the other. The prior candle said one thing; the engulfing candle erases it. When that happens *at a level*, it says the side defending the level just got run over.

**In practice.** The prior candle is red, closing down **0.8%**. The next candle opens there and closes up **1.9%**, its body swallowing the previous bar's entire range — a bullish engulfing. Buyers didn't just nudge price; they overran everything the last period's sellers built.

The numbers in these examples — 61,000, the 0.8%, the 1.9% — are the *mechanic* stated as proportions, not a quote to go looking for on the charts. Read the shape, then read where it happens.

## Non-information: when a candle says nothing

Half of reading candles is refusing to read the ones that carry no message.

**The doji.** Open and close are almost equal — a tiny body, wicks on both sides. Nobody won: buyers and sellers fought to a draw. A doji is *indecision*, not a signal. Open **50,000**, close **50,050** is a 0.1% body — that candle is the market shrugging.

**The small-range candle.** A very short bar after movement is **compression** — the market pausing, coiling. It predicts no direction; it only tells you volatility dropped. Reading a continuation into it is inventing intent the bar doesn't have. This same phenomenon exists at two larger scales, and says exactly as little at each: across a run of bars it is the wedge and the triangle of **m15-l2**, and across a whole market regime it is the volatility cycle of **m16-l1**.

## Location is 90% of the signal

Here is the rule that governs everything above: **a candle's meaning comes overwhelmingly from where it happens.**

A rejection wick at the 0.618 retracement, over an old support that has already held twice, is information — because the *level* is what buyers were defending, and the wick shows them defending it. The **identical** wick in open space, leaning on no level, is noise with a wick. Same shape, completely different value — because the level, not the candle, does the work. This is exactly m13's stance that "the fib doesn't create the level": the candle doesn't create the signal either. It's a reaction *to* something, and if there's nothing to react to, there's no signal.

::figure{id=fig-m08-rejection-vs-open-space}

So the read is always two questions, in order: *what is the mechanic* (rejection, overrun, or nothing), and — the one that decides its weight — *where did it happen*.

## The names you'll hear

You will meet named Japanese candlestick patterns everywhere: YouTube, TradingView, trading Twitter. You should be able to parse that lingua franca, so here is the dictionary — mapped onto the two mechanics plus location, never as standalone signals:

- **Hammer / pin bar** — a *rejection* at lows (long lower wick).
- **Shooting star** — the same *rejection* inverted at highs (long upper wick).
- **Engulfing** — an *overrun* (one body swallows the prior range).
- **Morning star / evening star** — a *rejection* told across three candles instead of one (down, pause, up — or the mirror).
- **Harami** — *compression* right after an impulse (a small candle inside the prior body).
- **Tweezers** — a *double rejection* at the same level (two candles wicking to the same price).

::figure{id=fig-m08-multi-candle-forms}

The takeaway is blunt: **there aren't 60 patterns, there are two mechanics wearing 60 names.** And like m13's self-fulfilling levels, a widely watched name matters *a little* more precisely because thousands of traders are reading it the same way and acting on it — which is also its limit: the edge is thin, crowded, and never a substitute for the level underneath. Whenever this course uses a name, it's shorthand for "this mechanic, at this location" — nothing more.

## Reading more than the candle says

All three ways this knowledge backfires are the same error wearing different clothes: reading the form and
forgetting the location.

The plain version is spotting a textbook hammer or engulfing *anywhere* and taking it. A rejection that
rejected nothing, an overrun that overran nothing anyone was defending — these fire constantly and mean
almost nothing on their own. If you can't name the level the candle is reacting to, there is no trade.

The studious version is collecting patterns like Pokémon, memorising sixty names as sixty separate
buy/sell buttons. It feels like knowledge and performs like noise, because it skips the only question that
matters. Learn the two mechanics; treat every name as a pointer to one of them at a location.

The wishful version is reading intent into a doji. A doji at a random spot is indecision, full stop.
Traders talk themselves into "reversal doji" after the fact; live, it is the market telling you it hasn't
decided — which is not permission to decide for it.

## Candle reactions in crypto

Crypto sharpens both the signal and the ways it lies. Markets run **24/7**, so a great many candles form in thin weekend and overnight books where a *handful* of orders can print a dramatic wick that looks like a heavyweight rejection and is really a liquidity vacuum. A hammer on a quiet Sunday at a low-liquidity level is a much weaker tell than the same hammer at a defended level during active hours. And because leverage stacks stops and liquidations just beyond obvious levels, a long wick through a level is often a **stop-hunt** — real order flow, but manufactured, not organic conviction. Weight every reaction by the depth of the book it happened in and the level it happened at, and distrust a lonely, spectacular wick in a quiet market.

:::note{type=info}
Read a candle in two moves: name the mechanic (rejection, overrun, or non-information), then ask where it happened. A reaction at a level the chart already respects is information; the same form in open space is noise; a doji or small-range candle is no signal at all. The level does the work — the candle just tells you what happened there.
