# The calendar: events with known mechanics

Some of what moves a market is not on the chart and never will be. It is on a **calendar** — published
weeks or months in advance, with a date, a size and a mechanism anybody can read.

This lesson is the third appearance of a mould you already know, and this time it gets said out loud:

> **None of this predicts direction. All of it predicts conditions.**

That is m17's reading of open interest and funding, and m32's reading of a volatility squeeze, applied
to the diary. A scheduled event tells you *what kind of market* you are about to be in — how liquid,
how volatile, who is likely to be forced to trade — and it tells you nothing at all about which way
price will go. Traders lose money on calendars almost entirely by mistaking the first thing for the
second.

## Token unlocks

A great many tokens are issued on a **vesting schedule**: teams, early investors and funds hold
allocations that become transferable on published dates. **m18** taught the tokenomics of this —
circulating supply against fully diluted value, and why a low float with a big FDV is a promise of
future supply. Here it is as an event.

An unlock is **scheduled, public supply arriving in a book**. That is the whole mechanism, and every
tool you need to read it is already in your hands:

- **Size it against the book, not against the market cap.** 5% of supply unlocking is a number; what
  matters is that number next to the depth (**m27**) available to absorb it. A large unlock into a thin
  book walks price a long way; the same unlock into a deep one may barely register.
- **Watch what open interest does around it.** Positioning ahead of a known date is visible in the
  derivatives (**m17-l1**), and the size of the anticipation often matters more than the unlock.
- **Not every unlocked token is sold.** Unlocked means *allowed to move*, not *moved*. A fund that has
  waited eighteen months may hold, may sell into strength weeks later, or may have hedged the exposure
  with a short long before the date — in which case the selling already happened, on the perp, and the
  unlock day itself is quiet.

### The error: treating a known thing as a surprise

The classic mistake is reacting on the day, as though something had just been discovered. It was on the
calendar for months. **What is known gets anticipated** — traders who wanted to be short were short
before it, and those who wanted out were out before it.

This is the same mechanism as **"sell the news"** below, and it is why unlocks so often produce a
drift down for weeks *before* the date and a bounce on the day itself. The event did not disappoint
anyone. It simply arrived after everybody who cared had already acted, and the last thing left to
happen was the un-positioning.

## Listings and "sell the news"

"Buy the rumour, sell the news" is the oldest adage in this business, and it is usually offered with no
mechanism at all — as though markets were merely fickle. The mechanism is simple and it is about
**who holds what**.

Take an exchange listing announced two weeks ahead:

1. **Who bought before?** Everyone who expects the listing to bring new buyers. They are buying an
   *expectation*, and the price rises on their buying, not on any new demand for the token itself.
2. **What happens on the day?** The event confirms. And the people positioned for it now hold a
   position whose reason has been used up — there is nothing left to expect. So they sell.
3. **Who do they sell to?** To the people who arrive *because* of the news, which is exactly when the
   news is loudest and the new-buyer flow is at its largest. That is the whole trick: the confirmed
   event is the moment of maximum incoming demand, so it is the best moment to be selling into.

So the pattern is not cynicism, it is **the release of the positioned**. And it gives you the reading
that is actually usable: an event that has been public for weeks is largely in the price already; an
event that arrives with no warning is not. The adage is really a statement about *how long the market
has had to prepare*.

## Quarterly and options expiries

Perpetuals never expire — that is the whole point of them (**m04-l1**). So why do dated contracts and
options expiries move the perp?

Because the positions around them have to be unwound, and that unwinding happens in whatever instrument
is most liquid.

- **Quarterly futures.** A dated contract converges to spot at settlement, and everything built on the
  difference between it and the perp — carry trades, calendar spreads, the whole of **m34-l1** — has to
  be closed or rolled at that moment. Those closures land on the perp, because the perp is where the
  liquidity is. **The basis re-anchors**, and the flow that re-anchors it is mechanical rather than
  directional: it happens because a date arrived, not because anyone changed their mind.
- **Options expiries.** The market makers on the other side of options positions hold hedges in the
  underlying, and those hedges are adjusted continuously and then released at expiry. Large expiries
  therefore concentrate real, forced flow into a narrow window — again in the perp — and the hours
  around them are unusually noisy.

:::note{type=info}
No greeks here, deliberately. The mechanics of options — how a hedge is sized, why it changes as price
moves, what "pinning" means — are a second course, not a footnote in this one. What belongs here is
only the part that touches the chart you are trading: **expiry dates are scheduled bursts of mechanical
flow in the perpetual**, and that is a condition, not a direction.
:::

## Venue operational risk

The last item on the calendar is the one nobody puts on a calendar, and the one that has ended more
accounts than any of the above: **the exchange itself.**

- **Scheduled maintenance.** Published in advance, usually harmless, occasionally landing at exactly
  the wrong hour. It is at least knowable, so it is at least avoidable — do not open a position you
  would need to manage during a window the venue has already told you it will be down.
- **Outages under load.** This is the serious one, and it is not random: exchanges degrade **when
  volume spikes**, which is precisely when you have a position that needs attention. The moment the
  app stops responding is the moment everybody is trying to use it.
- **"I cannot close" as a real risk.** Not a hypothetical. A position you cannot exit is a position
  managed entirely by your stop order — if you placed one — and by your liquidation price if you did
  not.

Two seams close here, and they close hard:

- **m02.** Your coins are on the venue, your collateral is on the venue, and both are claims on a
  company. Custody risk is not just about theft; it is about access at the moment access matters.
- **m06.** The exchange's liquidation engine does not go down when the app does. Your position is being
  marked and your maintenance margin is being checked whether or not you can log in. This is the whole
  argument for the protective stop being *resting on the exchange* rather than living in your head, and
  for the position size that survives a move you cannot react to.

And it is the argument for the carry's third risk in **m34-l1** stated once more: a delta-neutral
position whose legs sit on a venue you cannot reach is not neutral, it is two positions being managed
by somebody else's software.
