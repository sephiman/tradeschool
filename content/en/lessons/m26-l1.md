# CVD: construction and divergence

A single period's delta tells you who was pressing during that period. Useful, and very noisy — one
hour's aggression tells you about as much as one candle does. What makes delta a tool you can read is
**accumulating** it, exactly as a running bank balance tells you more than any single transaction.

That accumulation is **CVD** — cumulative volume delta. It is the single most useful order-flow
instrument for a chart reader, and this lesson has one destination: using it to see absorption directly,
instead of inferring absorption from a fat volume bar and hoping.

## What CVD is: a running total of aggression

**What it is.** Take each period's delta — taker buy volume minus taker sell volume — and keep a running
sum from some starting point, usually the beginning of a session or of the window you're looking at.
Plot that sum as a line under the price. That line is the CVD.

Concretely, five hours of a session. Each line is that hour's taker buy volume against its taker sell
volume, the delta that follows from them, and the running total:

- Hour 1: 1,200 bought against 900 sold → delta **+300** → CVD **+300**
- Hour 2: 800 against 1,100 → delta **−300** → CVD **0** (the hour gave back exactly what hour 1 built)
- Hour 3: 1,500 against 700 → delta **+800** → CVD **+800**
- Hour 4: 600 against 1,400 → delta **−800** → CVD **0** again
- Hour 5: 1,000 against 500 → delta **+500** → CVD **+500**

Notice hours 2 and 4: the delta went sharply negative, and the CVD did not become negative — it fell
back toward where it had started. The delta is the hour's flow; the CVD is the position of the session.

**Why the starting point does not matter.** The CVD's *level* is arbitrary — it depends entirely on when
you started counting, which is why a CVD pane rarely has a meaningful zero of its own. What you read is
its **shape**: is it climbing, falling, flat, and where did it turn? Comparing today's CVD level with
last month's is meaningless. Comparing where it is now against where it was at the last swing is the
whole technique.

**In practice.** The line looks and behaves like a price series — it trends, it makes higher highs and
lower lows, it consolidates. That is deliberate: it means every structural idea from block C works on
it. A CVD making a higher low is read the same way a price making a higher low is read.

## What it adds over raw volume

m14 taught you to read volume against its own average, and that a break needs a crowd. CVD answers a
question volume structurally cannot.

- **Volume** is a total. It counts every trade's two sides equally, so it can only ever say *how much
  participation there was*. A tall bar means many people traded. It cannot say who was pressing.
- **CVD** is a difference, accumulated. It says *which side was paying for immediacy, and for how long*.

The practical upgrade is on absorption. In m14, absorption was a strong inference: a huge volume bar with
almost no price range, so *presumably* someone large was soaking up everything thrown at them. You could
not see it; the volume bar counts both sides identically. With CVD you can look: if price is flat or
falling while CVD climbs steadily, aggressive buyers really are pressing, and the fact that price is not
moving means something is standing there taking the other side of all of it. Same event, m14's guess
turned into a reading.

## CVD divergence: the sharpest form of absorption

This is the technique the lesson is for, and it is m12's logic with the oscillator swapped out.

- **Bullish CVD divergence.** Price makes a **lower low**; CVD makes a **higher low**. Sellers pushed
  price to a fresh low, but with *less net aggression* behind it than the previous low — and the selling
  they did throw at it was eaten. Read: the supply is being absorbed.
- **Bearish CVD divergence.** Price makes a **higher high**; CVD makes a **lower high**. The new peak
  was bought with less aggression than the last one. Read: demand is being distributed into.

**Worked example (bullish).** BTC sells off to 58,200 and the session's CVD reads −24,000 at that low —
aggressive selling has dominated the session by 24,000 contracts. Price bounces, rolls over, and grinds
to a **lower** low at 57,400. But the CVD at that second low reads **−10,500**: over that whole second
leg, aggressive buying was net *positive* enough to lift the running total by 13,500 while price fell
800. Sellers got a lower price and had to give up ground in flow to get it. Someone was buying
everything they sold.

The figure shows exactly that shape, and then what followed it. Price makes its second, lower low; the
CVD line under it does not follow — it turns up well before price does. The resolution on the right is
what a divergence like this often precedes, and note the honest word: *often*.

**Why it leads.** Aggression is spent before price moves. To make a new low, sellers must overcome
resting bids; if the bids keep absorbing, the sellers exhaust themselves at a price that barely improves.
The CVD is measuring that expenditure directly, which is why it can turn while price is still making its
low. That is the same reason m12's momentum divergence leads — but one layer closer to the trades, and
measured rather than computed from price.

## The third case: when CVD confirms

A divergence is a disagreement, so you have to know what agreement looks like, or you will find
divergences everywhere.

If price makes a new low and CVD makes a **new low with it**, there is no divergence and nothing to
read as absorption. That is a straightforward, flow-backed move down: sellers pressed, nobody meaningful
stood in the way, and price went where the pressure sent it. It is the most common of the three cases,
and calling it a divergence because you wanted one is the error the exercises in this lesson are built
to train out of you.

## Where this confirms the spring (m09)

m09 left you with a specific, honest problem. A **spring** is a stab below support that recovers, and
you can only label it a spring *after* price comes back inside — live, that same wick is
indistinguishable from the start of a genuine breakdown. The lesson's advice was to wait for the
**test**: the quieter return toward the low that holds.

CVD is where you check that test. On a real spring the stab below support is selling being absorbed, so
the CVD should show it: price takes out the low, the CVD refuses to make a corresponding new low, and on
the test the price holds while the flow stays supported. On a genuine breakdown the CVD makes its new
low with price, because nothing absorbed anything. The wick looks the same in both. The flow does not.

This does not make the spring certain — it is confluence (m14), one more independent lens agreeing. But
it is a considerably better answer than "wait and see whether it holds."

## When CVD says nothing

:::note{type=info}
CVD is venue data, and this matters more for CVD than for delta over one period, because the errors
accumulate along with the flow.

- **It is per-venue.** One exchange's CVD describes aggression *on that exchange*. A large buyer working
  a different venue is invisible in it. Aggregated CVD exists and is genuinely useful, but as m25 said,
  it stacks estimates — and if one venue in the aggregate has poor reporting, it drags the line.
- **Perps and spot tell different stories.** Perp CVD is leveraged, reflexive flow, heavy with
  liquidations and stops. Spot CVD is closer to someone actually acquiring the coin. They can diverge
  from each other, and that difference is informative rather than a fault.
- **Timeframe changes the reading.** A divergence on a 1-minute chart is a few minutes of flow and is
  mostly noise. The same shape across a session's hourly bars is a real imbalance. Read CVD at the
  timeframe you intend to trade, and be sceptical of the fast ones.
:::

## Two lines, three ways to misread them

:::note{type=warning}
**Reading rising CVD as "price will rise."** This is the same error as m25's, and CVD makes it more
tempting because the line looks so much like price. Rising CVD means aggressive buyers are pressing. If
price is not going up while they press, that is not a promise of a delayed rally — it is evidence
someone is comfortably selling into all of it. The information is in the *disagreement* between the two
lines, which means you must always read them together. A CVD pane alone is nearly useless.

**Finding a divergence on every chart.** Any two wiggly lines disagree somewhere if you choose your
points freely. A divergence is only worth something at **two clear swings of the same kind** — two lows,
or two highs — with a real new extreme in price. If you have to hunt for which points to compare, there
is no divergence there. This is m12's discipline and it transfers unchanged.

**Trading it without a level or a stop.** A CVD divergence tells you the current move is running on
absorbed aggression. It does not tell you when, or from what price, the turn happens. It belongs on top
of structure — at a tested level, in a range you have already mapped — and it is sized and stopped like
anything else (m19). "The CVD diverged" is not a reason to hold a loser.
:::
