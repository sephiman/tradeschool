# Reading divergences

A **divergence** is a disagreement between price and a momentum oscillator (RSI or MACD). Price makes
a new extreme; the oscillator refuses to confirm it. That refusal is a clue that the move is running
out of fuel — not a guarantee, a clue. The whole lesson is really about one skill: knowing what that
clue is worth, and when it is worth nothing at all.

The oscillator matters because it measures something price alone hides. Price only tells you *where*
the market is; an oscillator like RSI tells you *how hard* it had to work to get there. When those two
stories stop matching, you are looking at a divergence.

## Regular divergence — the reversal warning

A **regular divergence** warns of a *reversal*. It compares two price extremes of the same kind (two
highs, or two lows) and asks whether momentum backed the second one.

- *Bearish regular:* price makes a **higher high**, the oscillator makes a **lower high**. Buyers
  pushed price to a fresh peak, but with less force than last time.
- *Bullish regular:* price makes a **lower low**, the oscillator makes a **higher low**. Sellers
  forced a new low, but the selling behind it is fading.

**Why it means "reversal":** the second extreme is the market's strongest attempt in that direction,
and the oscillator is saying that attempt was made by fewer, tired participants. A trend that can no
longer make new highs *with conviction* is a trend close to running out of buyers.

Worked example (bearish regular): BTC rallies to 68,000 and RSI reads **76**. It pulls back, then
pushes to a higher high at **71,000** — but this time RSI only reaches **64**. Price is 3,000 higher;
momentum is 12 points lower. The new high was bought by a thinner crowd. That is a textbook bearish
regular divergence.

The bullish case is the mirror image. Price grinds to a lower low — say 61,000 then 58,500 — while RSI
lifts from **22** to **31**. The new low is *lower in price but higher in momentum*: sellers reached
further and got less for it.

::figure{id=fig-m12-bullish-regular}

## Hidden divergence — the continuation signal

A **hidden divergence** points the opposite way: it signals *trend continuation*, and it typically
appears during a pullback rather than at the extreme of a move.

- *Bullish hidden:* price makes a **higher low** while the oscillator makes a **lower low**. Structure
  is still climbing (a higher low means the uptrend is intact), but the oscillator overreacts to the
  dip.
- *Bearish hidden:* price makes a **lower high** while the oscillator makes a **higher high**.
  Structure is still falling, but the oscillator overreacts to the bounce.

**Why it means "continuation":** in a healthy uptrend, a pullback is supposed to reset momentum without
breaking structure. When the oscillator plunges to a lower low but price holds a higher low, the market
has flushed out weak hands and reloaded — the dip did its job. The scary-looking oscillator is the
*reason* the trend can continue, not a warning against it.

Worked example (bullish hidden): an uptrend pulls back. Price holds a higher low at **64,000** (the
previous swing low was 62,000), yet RSI sinks to **28**, below the **34** it printed at the earlier
low. On the oscillator the dip looks worse than before; on price, the trend never broke. That gap is a
bullish hidden divergence, and it usually resolves upward.

::figure{id=fig-m12-bullish-hidden}

The bearish hidden is the same idea inside a downtrend: price makes a lower high while the oscillator
makes a higher high. The bounce looks strong on the oscillator, but structure never stopped falling.

::figure{id=fig-m12-bearish-hidden}

The trick that keeps people straight: **regular divergence compares the extremes and expects a turn;
hidden divergence appears on the retracement and expects the trend to resume.**

## Why momentum leads price at turns

Divergences work for a mechanical reason, not a mystical one. An oscillator is a **rate-of-change**
measure — it tracks how *fast* price is moving, not how far. At a genuine turning point, the last push
to a new extreme is almost always made by fewer participants: the eager buyers are already in, so the
final leg up is slower even though price still ticks higher. Rate-of-change sees that slowdown
immediately, while price — which only records the final tick — still shows a new high. Momentum rolls
over first; price follows. That lead time is the entire edge a divergence offers.

## Why divergences fail in strong trends

They fail for an equally real reason: in a **strong trend**, price can print divergence after
divergence and keep going. Momentum "should" be exhausted and simply isn't — a determined trend keeps
attracting fresh buyers faster than the old ones tire. A divergence is a statement about *momentum*,
not a dated appointment for a reversal. The single most expensive habit in this whole topic is treating
"momentum is fading" as "the top is in."

## Confirmation with structure

A divergence in isolation is a low-quality signal. It becomes tradeable only when it lines up with
**structure** — a level the chart already respects.

- A **bearish regular** divergence *into a resistance that has already rejected price* is two
  independent reasons to expect a turn: fading momentum and a proven ceiling.
- A **bullish regular** divergence *at a support that has already held* is the same logic inverted.

The concrete difference: a bearish divergence that appears as price taps the exact high that capped the
last three rallies is a signal; the identical divergence halfway between levels, in open space, is noise
wearing a signal's costume. Same oscillator reading, completely different value — because the level, not
the oscillator, is doing the work.

:::note{type=warning}
**How this burns people.** Two classic ways. First, **trading a divergence in open space** — no level,
no structure, just "RSI diverged," which fires constantly and means almost nothing on its own. Second,
and more expensive, **shorting a strong trend because it "has to be exhausted."** Fading a powerful
uptrend on the third or fourth divergence is how disciplined-looking traders get run over: each
divergence looks like confirmation of the last, and the trend eats them all. A divergence gives you a
reason to *watch* for a reversal, never a reason to stand in front of a moving trend.
:::

## Reading it off a chart

1. Find the two swing points on **price** (two highs, or two lows).
2. Read the oscillator value **under those same two points** — not nearby, the same points.
3. Compare the direction of the two lines. Agreement = confirmation; disagreement = divergence.
4. Ask where price is in its **structure**. A divergence at a meaningful level is a signal; a
   divergence in open space is noise dressed as a signal.

## Weekend divergences and thin books

Crypto makes both the signal and the failure mode sharper. Markets run **24/7**, so divergences form on
weekend and overnight candles that are thin and easy to misread — a "divergence" on low weekend volume
often evaporates when real liquidity returns on Monday. On **thin alt books**, a handful of orders can
jam price to a new high while the oscillator barely moves, manufacturing divergences that reflect a
quiet book rather than fading conviction.

Most important: crypto **parabolic** moves are the graveyard of divergence traders. A coin in a genuine
mania can print a clean bearish divergence, keep going, print another, keep going, and print a third —
each one looking like the top, each one wrong, sometimes for weeks. Persistent **funding** can bankroll
exactly this: as long as longs are willing to keep paying to hold, the trend can defy "exhausted"
momentum far longer than a beginner's account can stay short against it.
