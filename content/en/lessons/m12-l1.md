# Reading divergences

A **divergence** is a disagreement between price and a momentum oscillator (RSI or MACD). Price makes
a new extreme; the oscillator refuses to confirm it. That refusal is a clue that the move is running
out of fuel — not a guarantee, a clue.

## Regular vs hidden

- **Regular divergence** warns of a *reversal*.
  - *Bearish regular:* price makes a **higher high**, the oscillator makes a **lower high**. Buyers
    are pushing price up on fading momentum.
  - *Bullish regular:* price makes a **lower low**, the oscillator makes a **higher low**. Sellers
    are forcing a new low but with less force behind it.
- **Hidden divergence** signals *trend continuation* — it typically appears in pullbacks.
  - *Bullish hidden:* price makes a **higher low** while the oscillator makes a **lower low**. The
    dip looks scary on the oscillator, but price structure is still climbing.
  - *Bearish hidden:* price makes a **lower high** while the oscillator makes a **higher high**.

The trick that keeps people straight: **regular divergence compares the extremes and expects a turn;
hidden divergence appears on the retracement and expects the trend to resume.**

## Why they're reliable — and why they still fail

Divergences work because momentum genuinely leads price at turning points: the last push to a new
high is often made by fewer participants, and the oscillator, being a rate-of-change measure, sees
that thinning before price does.

They fail for an equally real reason: in a **strong trend**, price can print divergence after
divergence and keep going. Momentum "should" be exhausted and simply isn't — a determined trend
overrides the signal. A divergence is a statement about *momentum*, not a dated appointment for a
reversal.

:::note{type=warning}
A divergence alone is not a trade. On its own it is one of the most over-traded signals in retail. It
becomes useful only **with structure**: a bearish regular divergence into a tested resistance, or a
bullish one at a level that has held, is worth far more than the same divergence in the middle of
nowhere.
:::

## What to look for on the chart

1. Find the two swing points on **price** (two highs, or two lows).
2. Read the oscillator value **under those same two points** — not nearby, the same points.
3. Compare the direction of the two lines. Agreement = confirmation; disagreement = divergence.
4. Ask where price is in its **structure**. A divergence at a meaningful level is a signal; a
   divergence in open space is noise dressed as a signal.
