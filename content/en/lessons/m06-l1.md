# How liquidation is calculated

Liquidation is the exchange force-closing your position because your margin can no longer cover your
losses. It is not a penalty the exchange chooses to apply — it is the mechanical point where your
collateral runs out. If you can compute *where* that point is before you enter, you control your risk.
If you can't, leverage controls you.

## Why it happens

When you open a leveraged position you post **margin** — a fraction of the position's notional value.
As price moves against you, your unrealised loss eats that margin. The exchange also requires you to
keep a small **maintenance margin**: a minimum equity you must retain to keep the position open. Once
your equity would fall to that floor, the exchange closes the position to avoid your balance going
negative — because on a leveraged position your losses can exceed what you put in, and the exchange,
not you, is on the hook for the shortfall. Liquidation is the exchange protecting itself first; the
maintenance-margin buffer is the sliver it keeps so the forced close lands *before* your equity hits
zero, not after.

## Isolated vs cross: where your losses stop

The margin mode you pick decides how far a single bad position can reach into your account.

- **Isolated margin** ring-fences one position's margin. If it liquidates, you lose exactly that
  margin and nothing else — the rest of your account is untouched. This is *why* isolated "saves your
  account": it turns a catastrophe into a bounded, known loss you sized on purpose.
- **Cross margin** shares your whole balance as collateral. That extra collateral pushes the
  liquidation price further away, so the position survives small wobbles it would not survive in
  isolated. The catch: a single bad position can now draw on — and lose — your entire balance. More
  forgiving to noise, far more dangerous to a real move.

Example: 100 USDT of isolated margin on a losing trade can cost you at most 100 USDT. The same trade
in cross margin, backed by a 2,000 USDT balance, can keep bleeding until it has drawn down a large
chunk of that 2,000 — the loss is no longer bounded by what you assigned to the trade.

## The formula

For a linear (USDT-margined) perpetual, ignoring fees, the isolated liquidation price is driven by
two rates: the **initial margin rate** (= 1 / leverage) and the **maintenance margin rate** `mmr`
(a small exchange-set fraction, e.g. 0.5%).

:::note{type=info}
**Long:**  `liq = entry × (1 − 1/leverage + mmr)`

**Short:** `liq = entry × (1 + 1/leverage − mmr)`

Intuitively: a long is liquidated *below* entry by roughly your margin cushion; a short *above* it.
Higher leverage → smaller cushion → liquidation sits closer to entry.
:::

**Why it sits exactly there.** Your margin *is* the cushion. At 10× you post `1/10 = 10%` of the
notional, so a 10% adverse move wipes out that 10% — except the exchange grabs the last 0.5% (the
`mmr`) before it's fully gone, so the position dies about 9.5% away, not 10%. The formula is nothing
more than "how far can price move before my posted margin, minus the exchange's buffer, is used up".

Worked intuition, on the same shelf the figure below draws: you buy the bounce and enter a **long** at
**9,500** with **10×** leverage and `mmr = 0.5%`. Your margin cushion is `1/10 = 10%` of price, minus
the 0.5% the exchange keeps, so price only has to fall about **9.5%** before you're liquidated:

`liq = 9,500 × (1 − 0.10 + 0.005) = 9,500 × 0.905 = 8,597.50`.

Drop the leverage to 3× and the cushion becomes `1/3 ≈ 33.3%`; the same long now survives down to
roughly `9,500 × (1 − 0.3333 + 0.005) ≈ 6,381`. Same trade, wildly different survival distance —
that is the thing leverage actually changes. Leverage does not change your entry, your direction, or
the market; it changes only *how much room price has to breathe before it kills you*.

## Cascades: why liquidations move the price

**What.** A liquidation is not a paper event — the exchange has to actually close the position in the
market. To close a liquidated long it *sells* into the order book; to close a short it *buys*. That
forced order hits real bids or offers at whatever price is available.

**Why it feeds on itself.** In a thin or one-sided book, that forced sell pushes price *down*, which
drags price into the next cluster of long liquidation prices, which fire *more* forced sells, which
push price further. Each wave triggers the next: a **liquidation cascade**. The same runs upward when
trapped shorts get force-bought in a squeeze.

**In practice:** this is why "liquidation wicks" overshoot so violently and then snap back — the
spike is forced flow exhausting a thin book, not new information about value. And it is why sitting
with your own liquidation price *just inside* a crowded liquidation zone is dangerous: you are lined
up to be one of the dominoes.

## Why accounts die inside normal noise

:::note{type=warning}
**Over-leverage puts your liquidation price inside normal noise.** This is the single most common way
beginners get liquidated. Crank that same long to **50×** and your cushion is only `1/50 = 2%`:

`liq = 9,500 × (1 − 0.02 + 0.005) = 9,500 × 0.985 = 9,357.50` — a mere **1.5%** below entry.

Now look at the figure above again. That liquidation wick reaches down to **9,314** and is gone within
the same candle: the chart barely registers it. Your 50× position, though, died on the way through —
and you were not even wrong about direction, since price ends the chart far above where you entered.

Crypto routinely moves more than 1.5% in a single quiet hour. So you are no longer trading your
*view* on direction; you are betting that ordinary, meaningless volatility doesn't brush a level 1.5%
away before your thesis plays out. You can be completely right about direction and still be liquidated
by noise on the way there. High leverage doesn't magnify a good trade — it shortens the fuse until the
market's normal wiggle is enough to end you.

**Cross margin turns one mistake into an account-wide event.** Because cross shares the whole balance,
a single runaway loser keeps drawing on collateral that is also backing your *other* positions. When
the account-level maintenance margin gives way, the exchange can liquidate positions across the board
— including ones that were fine on their own — to cover the shortfall. One bad trade cascades through
the entire account. Isolated margin is the firebreak that stops exactly this.
:::

## No closing bell to save you

Everything above is worse in crypto than in traditional markets, for reasons specific to how these
venues run:

- **24/7, no closing bell, no circuit breakers.** Stocks pause overnight and halt on extreme moves;
  perpetuals never close and rarely halt. Your liquidation price is live at 3 a.m. on a Sunday while
  you sleep. There is no overnight gap to wait out and no exchange time-out to let a margin squeeze
  cool off — if price reaches your level, you are closed, whatever the hour.
- **Thin alt-books amplify every cascade.** Cascades scale with how thin the book is. Major pairs like
  BTC absorb forced flow reasonably well; small altcoins have shallow books where a modest wave of
  liquidations can gap the price several percent in seconds. The same leverage that is merely risky on
  BTC is genuinely reckless on a thin alt.
- **Weekend wicks.** Liquidity is thinnest on weekends — fewer market makers, lower volume. The same
  forced-liquidation flow that a weekday book would absorb instead shoves price far further, producing
  the notorious weekend "wicks" that stab down (or up), sweep a cluster of liquidation prices, and
  recover minutes later. Traders leaving high leverage on over a quiet weekend are the classic
  victims.
