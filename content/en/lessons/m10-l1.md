# Moving averages and trend

A **moving average (MA)** collapses the last N closing prices into a single line that trails the
price. It does one job well: it strips out the tick-by-tick noise so the underlying direction is
easier to see. It does nothing else — it does not predict, and by construction it always looks
backwards.

## SMA vs EMA

There are two you will meet constantly.

- A **simple moving average (SMA)** is the plain average of the last N closes. Every close in the
  window counts equally, and when the oldest one drops out the line can lurch — a price from N bars
  ago still has as much say as yesterday's.
- An **exponential moving average (EMA)** weights recent closes more heavily, so it hugs the price
  more closely and turns sooner. An EMA20 will react to a fresh move days before an SMA20 does.

The trade-off is the whole story: **the EMA reacts faster (less lag), but that speed also makes it
noisier** — it turns on moves that fizzle. The SMA is smoother and calmer but slower to admit the
trend has changed. Faster is not better; it is a different setting on the same dial.

## Reading trend from a moving average

Two things on one line tell you the regime:

- **Slope.** A rising MA means the average price is climbing — an uptrend. A falling MA is a
  downtrend. A flat MA means no trend: a range.
- **Price position.** Price holding *above* a rising MA is a healthy uptrend; price *below* a
  falling MA is a healthy downtrend.

The clean reads are when both agree. **Price above a rising MA = uptrend; price below a falling MA =
downtrend.** Price chopping across a *flat* MA is the market telling you there is no trend to trade —
which is exactly when moving averages are at their worst (below).

## Crosses, and why they arrive late

Put a **fast** MA (short period) and a **slow** MA (long period) on the same chart and watch where
they cross.

- A **golden cross** is the fast MA crossing *above* the slow one — read as bullish.
- A **death cross** is the fast MA crossing *below* the slow one — read as bearish.

The logic is sound: the fast line reflects recent prices, so it turns first, and when it clears the
slow line the shorter-term trend has taken over the longer-term one. But both lines are averages of
prices that already happened, so a cross **confirms a change that is already underway** — it never
calls the turn. By the time a golden cross prints, a large part of the move can be behind you.

:::note{type=warning}
A cross is a lagging *confirmation*, not a precise entry signal. Traders who "buy the golden cross"
blindly are buying a move that started well before the lines met. The cross tells you the trend has
turned; it does not tell you that this instant is a good price.
:::

## Dynamic support and resistance

In a running trend, price often pulls back, touches a moving average, and resumes — so the MA acts
like a **moving floor in an uptrend or a ceiling in a downtrend**. Part of this is real (the MA
tracks the average cost of recent buyers) and part is self-fulfilling (everyone is watching the same
20, 50 and 200 EMAs). Treat it as a **zone to watch, not a line that must hold**: in a genuine
reversal, price cuts straight through it.

## The honest caveat: they lag, and they whipsaw

Every moving average is built from past prices, so it can only ever describe the trend you have
already had, confirmed late. That is tolerable in a trending market and actively harmful in a
**range**: with no trend, a flat MA gets sliced back and forth, the fast and slow lines cross
repeatedly, and every one of those crosses is a false signal — this is **whipsaw**. A moving average
earns its keep only when there is a trend for it to smooth; hand it a sideways market and it will
hand you a string of losses dressed up as signals.
