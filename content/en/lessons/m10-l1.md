# Moving averages and trend

A **moving average (MA)** collapses the last N closing prices into a single line that trails the
price. It does one job well: it strips out the tick-by-tick noise so the underlying direction is
easier to see. It does nothing else — it does not predict, and by construction it always looks
backwards. Everything useful and everything dangerous about a moving average follows from that one
fact: it is an average of the past.

## SMA vs EMA

There are two you will meet constantly, and the whole difference is *how they weight the past*.

- A **simple moving average (SMA)** is the plain average of the last N closes. Every close in the
  window counts equally. That equal weighting has a hidden cost: when the oldest close drops out of
  the window, it drops out *completely*, so the line can lurch on a bar when nothing happened today —
  a big price from exactly N bars ago simply falls off the back. A close from N bars ago has as much
  say as yesterday's, right up until the instant it has none.
- An **exponential moving average (EMA)** weights recent closes more heavily and lets old ones fade
  rather than fall off a cliff. Each bar it updates as `EMA_today = k × close_today + (1 − k) ×
  EMA_yesterday`, with `k = 2 / (N + 1)`. Old prices never leave entirely — their weight just decays
  geometrically — so the EMA never lurches from a drop-off, and because recent bars dominate, it
  turns sooner.

**A worked reaction.** Suppose price has sat flat at 100 for a long time, so an SMA10 and an EMA10
both read 100. Now price jumps to 110 and holds there. The SMA10 climbs in a straight line — one old
100 is swapped for a 110 each bar — so it reads 101, 102, 103… and only reaches 110 after ten bars,
when the whole window is finally 110s. The EMA10 (`k = 2/11 ≈ 0.18`) jumps to about **101.8 on the
very first bar** and is already near **106.3 by bar five**, while the SMA has crawled only to 105.
Same data — but the EMA has admitted most of the move before the SMA is halfway there.

The trade-off is the whole story: **the EMA reacts faster (less lag), but that speed also makes it
noisier** — it turns on moves that fizzle. The SMA is smoother and calmer but slower to admit the
trend has changed. Faster is not better; it is a different setting on the same dial.

## Reading trend: slope, position, and order

Three things — two on one line, one across two lines — tell you the regime.

- **Slope.** A rising MA means the average price is climbing: an uptrend. A falling MA is a
  downtrend. A flat MA means no trend: a range.
- **Price position.** Price holding *above* a rising MA is a healthy uptrend; price *below* a falling
  MA is a healthy downtrend. Position tells you the *current* price agrees with the averaged
  direction.
- **Order** (with two MAs on the chart). When a fast MA sits *above* a slow one, recent prices are
  higher than older ones — the short-term trend is stronger than the long-term. Fast below slow is
  the reverse.

The clean reads are when all three agree. **Price above a rising fast MA, fast above slow =
uptrend; the mirror = downtrend.** Price chopping across a *flat*, intertwined pair is the market
telling you there is no trend to trade — which is exactly when moving averages are at their worst
(below).

The figure draws an EMA20 (fast) and an EMA50 (slow) over three markets. Read the *signature* each

The **downtrend** is the exact mirror. The **range** has the two EMAs flat and tangled while price
saws back and forth across them. Learning to recognise those three pictures at a glance is the skill
this module trains.

## Golden and death crosses

Put a **fast** MA (short period) and a **slow** MA (long period) on the same chart and watch where
they cross.

- A **golden cross** is the fast MA crossing *above* the slow one — read as bullish.
- A **death cross** is the fast MA crossing *below* the slow one — read as bearish.

The logic is sound: the fast line reflects recent prices, so it turns first, and when it clears the
slow line the shorter-term trend has taken over the longer-term one. But here is *why the signal is
always late*: for a 50-period line to climb above a 200-period line, recent prices must already have
dragged a 50-bar average above a 200-bar average — and that takes a sustained rally that is, by
definition, already dozens of bars old. Both lines are averages of prices that have already
happened, so a cross **confirms a change that is already underway** — it never calls the turn. By
the time a golden cross prints, a large part of the move can be behind you.

:::note{type=warning}
A cross is a lagging *confirmation*, not a precise entry signal. Traders who "buy the golden cross"
blindly are buying a move that started well before the lines met. The cross tells you the trend has
turned; it does not tell you that this instant is a good price.
:::

## Which averages, on which timeframe

Nothing above says what N to use, and that is the honest order — the reading comes first. But you will
meet the same handful of periods everywhere, so it is worth knowing which ones and why they recur.

- **9 and 21, on intraday charts.** A scalper or day trader working the 1-minute to 15-minute chart
  needs lines that turn inside the session, so the periods are short — and, by the lag/noise trade-off
  above, noisy.
- **20 and 50, for swing trading**, read on the 4-hour and the daily: long enough to shrug off one bad
  candle, short enough to turn inside a multi-day hold. That is the pair in the figure above.
- **50 and 200, on the daily** — the slow, most-quoted pair, and where the **golden cross** and
  **death cross** got their names: those two terms almost always mean the 50-day crossing the 200-day.

Treat those as **conventions, not results.** No market owes a 200-day average anything, and no
calculation makes 21 better than 19. They matter for the same reason m13 gives for Fibonacci levels:
enough people watch the same lines that resting orders pile up around them, so a level with no
mechanism behind it still becomes a place where price reacts. That effect is real — and it is the
whole of the effect. It works while the line is crowded, not because the number is right.

What the parameters do *not* change is how you read the chart. The three signatures — order, slope,
and where price sits relative to the pair — look the same on a 1-minute chart with 9/21 as on a daily
with 50/200, which is why this module teaches the picture and not the settings. What they change is
the *horizon the signal talks about*. A 9/21 cross on the 5-minute chart is a claim about the next
hour, and it will be reversed by lunchtime often enough that a swing trader is right to ignore it. A
50/200 cross on the daily is a claim about the next several months, and it is no reason at all to take
a trade you mean to close today. Match the pair to your holding period (**m23-l1**, trading styles),
then read it with the same three signatures you would use anywhere.

## Dynamic support and resistance

In a running trend, price often pulls back, touches a moving average, and resumes — so the MA acts
like a **moving floor in an uptrend or a ceiling in a downtrend**. Part of this is real: the MA
tracks roughly the average cost of recent buyers, so a pullback to it is a pullback to where the
average holder got in, and fresh buyers tend to step in there. Part is self-fulfilling: everyone is
watching the same 20, 50 and 200 EMAs, so orders cluster around them and the level "works" partly
because it is watched. Treat it as a **zone to watch, not a line that must hold**: in a genuine
reversal price cuts straight through it, and the same MA that was support becomes resistance on the
way back down.

## Why they lag — by construction

A moving average can only describe the trend you have already had. The lag is not a flaw to be tuned
away; it is arithmetic. An SMA of period N is centred on a price roughly `(N − 1) / 2` bars in the
past — an **SMA50 effectively reports a price about 25 bars old**. An EMA of the same period is
faster but still trails, because old bars still carry weight. Shortening the period cuts the lag but
adds noise; lengthening it smooths the line but pushes the report further into the past. You are
always trading one against the other: there is no setting that is both fast and smooth.

## Where moving averages stop helping

Every moving average is built from past prices, so it can only ever describe the trend you have
already had, confirmed late. That is tolerable in a trending market and actively harmful in a
**range**.

With no trend, a flat MA gets sliced back and forth, the fast and slow lines cross repeatedly, and every
one of those crosses is a false signal — this is **whipsaw**. A moving average earns its keep only when
there is a trend for it to smooth; hand it a sideways market and it will hand you a string of losses
dressed up as signals.

The two classic mistakes are really the same coin. Trading MA crosses inside a range is the whipsaw
version, and treating a lagging cross as a fresh entry in a trend is the buying-late version. Both come
from forgetting that the line is a rear-view mirror.

## Unbroken series, thinner books

Crypto changes the inputs in two opposite directions.

- **No gaps, one continuous series.** Stocks close overnight and at weekends, so a daily MA has to
  digest a gap every Monday and averages a "close" that skips most of the day. Crypto trades 24/7, so
  the price series is unbroken: an EMA50 on the daily genuinely averages fifty continuous days, with
  no gap-driven jumps in the line. That makes moving averages *cleaner* to read on crypto than on
  equities.
- **But far more volatility, so fast MAs are noisier.** Crypto — and especially thin alt-coins —
  moves in bigger, sharper swings than most stocks. A fast MA on a volatile alt whips constantly,
  throwing crosses that a calmer market would never produce, and a weekend low-liquidity wick can
  jerk a short EMA on nothing. The practical upshot: to get the same smoothness you would from a
  given period on equities, you usually need a *longer* period on crypto.
