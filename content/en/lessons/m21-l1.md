# The carry: funding and basis as a strategy

**m19-l1**, derivatives data, mentioned an arbitrage in passing. The perp trades above spot, so
somebody sells the rich perp and buys the cheap spot, and the two prices get pulled back together.
This lesson goes and lives inside that sentence, because the trade it describes is real, it is
enormous, and it is sold to beginners as free money.

It is not free money. It is a **thin-margin business with fat tails**, and the difference between those
two descriptions is the whole lesson.

## Cash-and-carry, laid bare

The trade is one idea with two legs:

- **Buy the spot.** 1 BTC, held on the exchange, at 60,000 USDT.
- **Short the perpetual.** 1 BTC of contracts, at the same 60,000.

You are now **delta-neutral**: if BTC goes to 70,000 you make 10,000 on the spot and lose 10,000 on the
short; if it goes to 50,000 the two swap places. Your position has no opinion about price at all, which
is exactly the point.

So why hold it? Because when the perp trades above spot, **funding is positive, and positive funding
means longs pay shorts** (**m04-l1**). You are the short. Every eight hours, the crowd of leveraged
longs pays you for the privilege of being long.

**The numbers.** Funding at **0.01% per 8h** — a common, unremarkable rate — on a 60,000 USDT short:

- per interval: `60,000 × 0.0001 = 6 USDT`
- per day (three intervals): `18 USDT`
- per year: `18 × 365 = 6,570 USDT`, which on the **120,000 USDT** the whole position ties up (60,000 of
  spot plus 60,000 of collateral behind the short) is **about 5.5% a year**.

That is the honest shape of it: a **5%-ish annual yield, delta-neutral**, on a large amount of capital,
in exchange for running two positions on an exchange. It is a real return and it is not a lottery
ticket. Notice also what it is *not*: it is not 5% of your account per year unless your whole account
is in it, and it is not free — the capital is locked, and doing nothing with 120,000 USDT would have
earned you nothing but would also have needed no exchange.

:::note{type=info}
The **basis** — the perp's premium over spot — and the funding rate are one fact seen twice, which
**m19-l1** already said. That means the carry can be entered from either side: you are collecting
funding *because* the perp is rich, and if you also close both legs once the premium compresses, you
capture the basis narrowing as well. Same trade, two ways of describing the profit.
:::

## Why the gap exists at all

If this is a positive expected return with no directional risk, why has anyone left it lying there?

Partly they have not: desks run this at size, which is precisely why the premium on major coins
normally sits well inside a tenth of a percent. The gap that survives is the part the arbitrage cannot
reach, and it survives for ordinary, boring reasons:

- **Capital is not free.** Every dollar in this trade is a dollar not doing something else, and 5% has
  to beat whatever else that money could earn — including, at times, plain lending.
- **The two legs need to sit somewhere.** Spot on one venue and the short on another means transfers,
  withdrawal limits and counterparty risk on both. On a single venue it is simpler and the risk is
  concentrated instead of split. There is no arrangement without a cost.
- **Nobody can arbitrage an infinite amount.** The trade caps out at the collateral available, and the
  people who run it are managing a book, not chasing every basis point.

And the corollary you can actually use: **the gap widens exactly when the crowd is maddest.** In a
mania, leveraged longs bid the perp far over spot and funding goes to 0.1% per 8h and beyond — over 100%
annualised. That is when the carry pays spectacularly, which is another way of saying **the carry's
yield is a leverage-mania thermometer**, the same reading m19-l1 takes from the other side of the
table.

## The risks of "riskless"

Here is the half no course-seller mentions.

### 1. Funding flips sign

Positive funding pays you. **Negative funding bills you**, at the same clock. If the perp swings to a
discount — a crash, a wave of panic shorts (**m19-l2**) — you are a short paying longs, and the
collection becomes a payment. Nothing about your position changed; the market changed sides.

Concretely: at −0.05% per 8h your 60,000 short pays out `60,000 × 0.0005 × 3 = 90 USDT a day`. Five days
of that is 450 USDT. A full month of collecting at 0.01% brings in `30 × 18 = 540`, so **five days of
paying wipes out five sixths of a month of collecting**. That is the thin-margin, fat-tail shape in one
line: you collect small amounts, reliably, and pay large amounts occasionally.

### 2. Venue risk

Two legs on two exchanges is two sets of counterparty risk, plus the transfer between them at exactly
the moment you least want a transfer to be slow. Two legs on *one* exchange removes the transfer and
concentrates the counterparty risk. Both are m02's "not your keys" wearing a strategy: your
delta-neutral position is a claim on an intermediary, and the neutrality is only as good as the
intermediary.

### 3. The short leg gets liquidated in a rally

**This is the one to burn into memory, and it is where m06, liquidation, comes back.**

Your position is delta-neutral as a *whole*. The exchange does not liquidate wholes. It liquidates
**positions, against the margin in their own account** — and it does not know, or care, that you hold
spot somewhere else.

So: BTC rallies 30%. Your spot leg gains 18,000 USDT, on paper, sitting there. Your short leg is down
18,000 USDT against whatever collateral you posted for it, and if that collateral was 60,000 you are
fine, but if you were efficient with capital and posted 20,000 the short is liquidated long before the
rally ends. The spot never rescues it. **You are left holding the winning leg of a hedge whose losing
leg has been closed at the worst possible price** — which is to say, a naked long, in a market that has
just gone up 30%, entered by accident.

> **Delta-neutral is not margin-neutral.**

The lesson generalises past this trade: a hedge protects you from the price. It does not protect you
from the *mechanics* of the venue holding it. Everything m06 taught about liquidation — that it is
computed per position, per account, against maintenance margin, without regard to your intentions —
applies here unchanged. The way to survive it is unglamorous: post far more collateral against the
short than the arithmetic strictly requires, and accept the lower yield that follows.

## The honest reading

Put the three risks next to the return and the strategy's true shape appears:

- The **upside is bounded** by construction. You collect a small rate. The very best case is that the
  rate is high for a while, and even then you are collecting a percentage, not catching a move.
- The **downside is not bounded by the same design** — not because prices can run away (they cannot
  hurt a hedged position) but because the *operational* failures are open-ended: a liquidated leg, an
  exchange you cannot withdraw from, a funding rate that stays negative.
- And the trade **compresses itself**. The more capital piles into the carry, the more perps get sold
  against spot, the smaller the premium, the smaller the funding. A crowded carry is a carry paying
  less — which is m19-l1's premium read from the other side of the table, and the reason the
  advertised yields belong to the period *before* everybody arrived.

None of that makes it a bad trade. It makes it a **business**: modest, capital-hungry, operationally
demanding margins, with a tail risk that has to be actively managed rather than hedged away. Sold as
"risk-free yield", it is the same misdescription as "more trades means more profit" in **m23-l1** — the
number quoted is real, and it is not the number that decides the outcome.
