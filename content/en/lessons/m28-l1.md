# Premium between venues

m17 taught you the **basis**: the gap between a perpetual and its own spot price, and how a sustained
premium reads as crowded leveraged positioning rather than as a forecast. This module applies the same
arithmetic to a different gap — the same coin, at the same moment, priced differently on **two different
exchanges**.

There is no single price for Bitcoin. There is a price on each venue, and they are usually very close and
occasionally not. That gap is the **premium between venues**, and when it persists it says something
specific about where money is entering the market.

## The same coin, two prices

**What it is.** Take BTC/USDT on a large global exchange and BTC against the local currency on an
exchange serving one country. Convert to a common currency and compare. Most of the time they agree to
within a few basis points. Sometimes one trades a percent or more above the other, for days.

**Why it can exist at all.** In a frictionless market it could not: you would buy on the cheap venue,
sell on the expensive one, and pocket the difference until the gap closed. The gap exists because that
trade is not frictionless. It requires having capital on both venues (or moving it), an account on both,
and the ability to get the proceeds home. Every one of those is a real cost or a real delay, and the
premium can be no smaller than the cost of removing it.

**Worked example.** BTC trades at 60,000 on venue A and 60,900 on venue B, at the same instant. The
absolute gap is 900. As a percentage of the reference venue: 900 / 60,000 = 0.015, so a **1.5% premium**
on venue B. The percentage is the number worth quoting, because 900 means nothing until you know whether
it is 900 on a 60,000 coin or on a 6,000 one. Comparing 1.5% today with 1.5% two years ago is
meaningful; comparing 900 with 900 is not.

## Two different causes, worth telling apart

A premium is a symptom, and it has two quite different explanations. Reading it usefully means knowing
which one you are looking at.

**1. Regional demand — the informative one.** One region's buyers are more eager than the rest of the
market, and they buy where they can: their local venue. Local demand lifts the local price above the
global one. This is what people mean when they say capital is "entering through" a particular venue —
often the case when one country's institutional or retail flow arrives before everyone else's. This
premium is *information*: it tells you where the buying pressure originates.

**2. Transfer friction — the boring one.** The premium reflects the cost or impossibility of moving
value in or out. Capital controls, slow or expensive banking rails, a venue where local currency is
hard to get out of, withdrawal limits or a paused withdrawal queue. Here the premium is not a demand
signal at all; it is the *price of the barrier*. A venue whose users cannot easily exit will trade at a
persistent premium almost regardless of sentiment.

The practical difference: a demand-driven premium is a flow reading that can change quickly and often
coincides with a real move. A friction-driven premium is close to a structural constant, and reading it
as bullish enthusiasm is a straightforward mistake. Notably, the same venue can show both at once — a
structural friction premium with demand-driven spikes on top of it, which is why what matters is the
premium's *change*, not its level.

## What a sustained premium signals

Read a **widening** premium on one venue as buying pressure arriving there first. If the venue represents
a distinct pool of capital, that is genuinely early information — the flow has to show up somewhere
before it shows up everywhere.

Read a **collapsing** premium as that pressure exhausting, or as arbitrage finally getting through. A
premium that spent weeks at 3% and falls to 0.2% is telling you the eager local buyer is done, or that
the barrier which sustained it just opened.

And read the level itself with m17's caution: this is a **positioning and flow** reading, not a
direction. An extreme premium means one pool of buyers is paying up conspicuously. Crowded is fragile,
here as everywhere else in this course.

## Why arbitrage does not close it instantly

The arbitrage that should erase a premium is real and constantly at work, and it is slow for concrete
reasons worth knowing:

- **Transfer time.** Moving coin between venues takes blockchain confirmations — minutes, sometimes much
  longer when a network is congested. The price can move against you the whole time you are exposed.
- **Fees at every step.** Withdrawal fees, trading fees on both sides, and the currency conversion.
  These set a floor: below roughly the round-trip cost, no premium is worth arbitraging, so small
  premiums simply persist.
- **Capital controls and banking limits.** In some jurisdictions moving the *fiat* leg is restricted,
  slow, or capped. This is the big one — it is why the most famous sustained premiums have historically
  appeared on venues in countries with tight capital controls, and why they can last months rather than
  minutes.
- **Capital already has to be in place.** The efficient version of the trade needs inventory on both
  venues simultaneously, which means tying up capital in advance and accepting the counterparty risk of
  both exchanges (m02's "not your keys").

So the honest picture is not "arbitrage eliminates premiums" but "arbitrage caps them at the cost of
doing it" — and where that cost is high, the cap is high.

## Where this is tracked

Several data sites publish cross-venue premium indices continuously; the best known is the
**Coinbase premium** (spot BTC on Coinbase against a global reference, widely watched as a proxy for US
institutional flow), and the long-running Korean-market premium usually referred to as the "Kimchi
premium" is the textbook case of the friction-driven kind. Those are examples of where the concept is
measured, not the concept itself — which is why this module is called *premium between venues* rather
than named after any one exchange's index. You can compute it yourself from two price feeds, which is
exactly what the calculation below does.

## Premium, misread

:::note{type=warning}
**Reading a premium as a buy signal.** "Venue X is at a premium, institutions are buying, go long" skips
every step that matters. You have not established which of the two causes you are looking at, whether the
premium is widening or just *high*, or whether the move it supposedly predicts has already happened. It
is a flow reading, and like funding in m17, it is most informative at extremes and near-meaningless in
the middle.

**Trying to arbitrage it as a beginner.** The gap looks like free money and is not. By the time you have
opened both accounts, funded both, and moved coin between them, you have taken on transfer-time price
risk, two sets of fees, two exchanges' solvency risk, and possibly a withdrawal freeze at the worst
moment. Professional arbitrage works because capital is already sitting on both venues and the operation
is automated. Doing it manually against those firms is paying tuition.

**Ignoring the currency leg.** A premium quoted between a USDT pair and a local-currency pair is only as
good as the conversion rate you used, and in exactly the stressed conditions where premiums blow out, the
local rate and the stablecoin's own peg are the things most likely to be wobbling. Check what you are
actually comparing before you believe a big number.
