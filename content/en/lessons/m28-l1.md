# Validating a system

**m25** handed you a number: expectancy, the average result of one trade under your rules. It is the
number that decides whether a system makes money, and everything after it — sizing, streak arithmetic,
the risk of ruin — is built on top of it.

Here is the uncomfortable part. **That number was an assumption.** You wrote down a win rate and an
average payoff, multiplied them out and got an answer, but nobody has checked that your rules actually
produce that win rate on this market. Expectancy is a *claim*, and until it is tested it is worth
exactly nothing.

This lesson is the trading version of the discipline the rest of this course keeps returning to: **test
the claim before you pay to find out.** The market will happily audit your system for you. It charges
by the trade.

## The honest manual backtest

A backtest is simple to describe: take your rules, walk them over price history that you did not use
to invent them, and write down what would have happened. Three conditions make it honest, and each one
is there because of a specific way people fool themselves.

**1. The rules are written down BEFORE you look.** Entry, stop, target, position size, what
invalidates the setup — on paper, in words a stranger could follow, before a single chart is opened.
If you cannot write them down, you do not have a system; you have a set of preferences that will
quietly reshape themselves around whatever the chart did.

That failure has a name in this course already. A backtest without pre-written rules is **m15-l1**'s
redrawn trendline: a line that gets adjusted until it fits will fit forever, and it will never tell you
anything. So will a rule.

**2. Bar by bar, with no scrolling back.** Advance the chart one candle at a time and decide with what
is on screen at that moment. This is the part everyone skips and it is the part that matters, because
**an eye that has seen the outcome cannot unsee it.** Looking at a completed move and asking "would I
have taken this?" is not a test — the answer is always yes, in the direction the move went. Hide the
right-hand side of the chart, or use a replay tool that does it for you.

**3. Every trade logged with m27's discipline.** Entry, stop, target, size, the reason, the outcome in
R. A backtest is a journal of trades that did not cost anything, and it is read the same way — you are
building the *sample* the next section is about, and a sample you did not record is not a sample.

:::note{type=warning}
The two most common ways a backtest lies to you are both about knowing the future. **Look-ahead**: using
information the bar did not have yet — an indicator that repaints, a level you drew after seeing where
price turned, a "close" you read before the candle closed. **Survivorship**: testing only the coins
that still exist and still trade, when a real system in 2021 would also have taken signals on tokens
that are now worth nothing. Both make a mediocre system look excellent, and neither leaves a trace in
the results.
:::

## Sample size: why twenty trades tell you nothing

Suppose your system is exactly a coin flip: 50% win rate, no edge at all. You take twenty trades.

**One time in four, a fair coin gives you twelve wins or more — a 60% win rate.** One time in
seventeen, it gives you fourteen or more, which reads as **70%**. Those are not unusual events; they
are what twenty flips look like. So a trader who runs twenty trades, sees 60% winners and concludes
"the system works" has learned nothing whatsoever about their system, and a trader who sees 35% and
throws the system away has done the same thing in the other direction.

Put the arithmetic on a table. Take a system whose *true* win rate is 50% and ask where the **observed**
win rate will land after N trades:

| Trades | Typical error (1σ) | Observed win rate, 19 times out of 20 | What you can honestly claim |
|---|---|---|---|
| **20** | ±11 pp | 28% – 72% | Nothing. This range contains every system. |
| **50** | ±7 pp | 36% – 64% | Still almost nothing. |
| **100** | ±5 pp | 40% – 60% | A very rough sense of direction. |
| **300** | ±3 pp | 44% – 56% | A win rate worth putting in a formula. |
| **1000** | ±1.6 pp | 47% – 53% | A number you can size against. |

Read the third column as the question it answers: *if my system were a coin, what would I be seeing?*
At twenty trades the answer is "anything between 28% and 72%", which means an observed 65% is
completely compatible with having no edge at all. The band only closes slowly, because it shrinks with
the **square root** of the sample: four times the trades halves the error, not quarters it.

> **The honest rule of thumb: dozens say nothing, hundreds begin to speak.**

And note what this does to m25's arithmetic. Expectancy is win rate times average win minus loss rate
times average loss, so an error band of ±11 points on the win rate is an error band on the expectancy
too — one wide enough, at twenty trades, to contain both "profitable" and "bleeding".

:::note{type=info}
The same logic explains why a **losing streak** proves as little as a winning one. m25 showed that eight
losses in a row is an ordinary event for a 50% system over a few hundred trades. That is the identical
fact seen from the other end: small stretches of a random-ish process contain patterns, and finding one
is not evidence.
:::

## Overfitting: the cardinal sin

You have your history and your rules, and the results are mediocre. So you adjust. RSI 14 becomes RSI
11 because that catches the March move. You add "only if volume is above the 20-bar average". You add
"but not on Mondays". Each change improves the result, and after an afternoon of this you have a system
with a spectacular equity curve.

You have also made the same mistake as the trendline that gets redrawn.

> **Every parameter you add is one more degree of freedom for the past to agree with you.**

That is what **overfitting** is: fitting the noise as well as the signal. A rule with enough knobs can
describe *any* history perfectly, including one generated by dice — and a description of the past has
no predictive content at all. The tell is easy to state and hard to accept: the more finely a system
fits the data it was built on, the less it usually survives contact with data it has not seen.

### Train and test, without the jargon

The defence is one of the simplest ideas in statistics, and it needs no vocabulary:

1. Split your history in two. **Fit on the first half.** Tune all you like — this is where the knobs
   are allowed to move.
2. Then run the finished, frozen rules over the **second half, which you never looked at.** No
   adjustments. Whatever comes out, comes out.

If the system performs on the half it was never allowed to see, you have some evidence. If it only
works on the half it was fitted to, **it is not a system, it is a description** — and the discipline is
to say so and start again, not to go back and tune the second half too.

:::note{type=warning}
The subtle version of this failure survives the split, so watch for it. If you run the train/test
procedure fifty times with fifty variations and keep the one that also passed the test half, you have
just overfitted the test half — you used it to choose. The test half is spent the first time you look
at it, which is why professionals keep a third slice they touch once, at the end, or nothing at all.
:::

## Forward-testing: the last filter

A validated backtest is still a simulation, and a simulation has no frictions in it. Before real size,
run the system **forward** — on a demo account or at the minimum position your exchange allows — for
long enough to see it behave.

What forward-testing catches is everything the historical data did not contain:

- **The costs of actually trading.** Your stop-limit does not fill (**m24**); you cross a wider spread
  than the backtest assumed; slippage on the entry eats a fifth of the edge; funding accumulates on the
  swing holds. A backtest that ignores fees and slippage is not conservative, it is wrong — and it is
  wrong in the direction that flatters you.
- **The trader.** This is the one people underestimate. Backtested you took every signal, moved no
  stops and felt nothing. Live you will skip the trade after two losses, widen a stop at 3 a.m. and
  double up to get even — the entire catalogue of **m26**. The system's expectancy is only the system's
  if you actually run it.

Demo has its own honest limitation: it costs you nothing, so it does not test the psychology. That is
precisely why **m27-l2** goes to *minimum real size* rather than staying on demo — the smallest sum
that still stings is the cheapest way to find out what you do under pressure.

## When to abandon a system

This is the hardest judgement in trading, and it is hard for a specific, structural reason:

> **In the short run, a valid system having a bad run is indistinguishable from a broken system.**

Both look like losses. Both feel the same. m25 already showed that a perfectly good system produces
long losing streaks as a matter of arithmetic, and **m26** showed what those streaks do to a person. So
the decision cannot be made *while* it hurts, by the person it is hurting.

Which leaves one workable approach: **fix the criterion before you start.**

- Decide, in writing and in advance, what would count as evidence that the system is broken — a
  drawdown deeper than the worst the backtest produced, an observed expectancy that has sat below its
  claimed value for a large enough sample, a losing streak longer than the arithmetic says should
  happen.
- Make it a **measured deviation from expectancy**, never a feeling. "Down 14R against a system whose
  backtested worst drawdown was 9R" is a criterion. "I cannot take this any more" is a state of mind,
  and the whole point of writing the rule down beforehand is that it is decided by someone who is not
  in that state.
- And keep the third possibility on the table, because it is the most common one of all: the system is
  fine and **you stopped following it**. That is what the journal is for — m27-l2's review distinguishes
  a system's bad run from your own, and it is the only tool that can.
