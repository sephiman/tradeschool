# Volume and confirmation

Every candle tells you where price went. **Volume** tells you how many people showed up to push it
there. It is the number of units traded in a period, drawn as a bar under the price, and it is the
closest thing you have to a measure of *participation* — of how much the market actually cared about a
move. On its own it points nowhere; read alongside price, it tells you whether a move had a crowd
behind it or was a handful of traders talking to themselves.

## Volume is participation, not direction

A common first mistake is to read volume as bullish or bearish by itself. It is neither. Every trade
has a buyer and a seller, so volume counts the *transactions*, not the winning side. What it measures
is conviction: a large green candle on heavy volume means a lot of participants agreed with the
up-move and acted on it; the same candle on thin volume means almost nobody did. Volume is the "how
many people care" dial, and you always read it *against* what price did.

## A breakout needs a crowd

The most useful thing volume does is tell you whether a breakout is real. When price breaks a level —
above resistance, below support — the price move itself looks the same whether one trader or ten
thousand pushed it through. The tell is underneath, in the volume bar.

- **A break on strong volume** means the crowd came with it: many participants agree the level matters
  and are acting. That break is far more likely to hold and follow through.
- **A break on weak volume** means few participants showed up. It is often a probe that quietly
  reverses — the classic false break, or "fakeout."

Same price break, very different trustworthiness. Strong volume does not *guarantee* the move
continues, but a break with no participation behind it is the setup a fakeout is made of.

:::note{type=warning}
The trap is treating the break itself as the signal. Because the price geometry is identical either
way, you have to look *down* at the volume to tell them apart. If you find yourself acting on a
breakout without having checked whether anyone came with it, you are trading the half of the picture
that lies most easily.
:::

## Absorption: big volume, little movement

Sometimes you see the opposite oddity — enormous volume, yet price barely moves. That usually means a
large participant is **absorbing**: soaking up everything thrown at the market at that price without
letting it move. Heavy volume with a tiny range means someone big is quietly taking the other side of
all that supply (or demand). It often precedes a move in the direction of whoever did the absorbing —
they were accumulating, not defending for fun. You cannot see who it is or be certain of intent; it is
a strong clue, not a receipt.

## Price/volume divergence

Volume should roughly keep pace with the move it belongs to. When it does not, that gap is worth
noticing. If price keeps grinding to new highs but the volume behind each push shrinks, fewer people
are carrying the move each time — the rally is running on fumes. Like the momentum divergences from
the oscillator module, a price/volume divergence is a **warning that participation is thinning**, not
a dated appointment for a reversal. It tells you the fuel is running low; it does not tell you the
minute the tank runs dry.

## Confluence: don't trust one thing

The habit that ties this module to everything before it is **confluence** — refusing to act on a
single signal and waiting for independent things to agree. Structure (a real, tested level or trend),
an indicator (RSI or MACD), and volume are three different lenses. A breakout at a level that has
already held, on a clear volume surge, with the oscillator confirming, is worth far more than any one
of those alone. When the three disagree — a break at a good level but on dead volume — the honest read
is *not yet*. Confluence does not make you right; it stacks the odds in your favour and filters out the
noise that any single signal throws up on its own.
