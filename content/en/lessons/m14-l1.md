# Volume and confirmation

Every candle tells you where price went. **Volume** tells you how many people showed up to push it
there. It is the number of units traded in a period, drawn as a bar under the price, and it is the
closest thing you have to a measure of *participation* — of how much the market actually cared about a
move. On its own it points nowhere; read alongside price, it tells you whether a move had a crowd
behind it or was a handful of traders talking to themselves.

## Volume is participation, not direction

**What it is.** A common first mistake is to read volume as bullish or bearish by itself. It is
neither. Every trade has a buyer and a seller, so volume counts the *transactions*, not the winning
side. What it measures is conviction: a large green candle on heavy volume means a lot of participants
agreed with the up-move and acted on it; the same candle on thin volume means almost nobody did.

**Why you always read it relative.** A raw volume number has no meaning on its own — 40,000 contracts
is large or trivial depending on what this market normally trades. That is why volume is read
*against its own recent average*, usually a moving average of the last 20 or so bars. If the 20-bar
average is 10,000 contracts an hour and the current bar prints 45,000, that is roughly 4.5× the usual
participation: a genuine crowd. The same 45,000 in a market that habitually does 200,000 an hour is a
lull, not a surge. The absolute figure is noise; the *ratio to normal* is the signal.

**In practice.** Faced with any volume bar, ask one question before any other: is this bar taller or
shorter than the ones around it? That single comparison — this bar against its neighbours — is most
of what volume analysis is.

## A breakout needs a crowd

The most useful thing volume does is tell you whether a breakout is real. When price breaks a level —
above resistance, below support — the price move itself looks the same whether one trader or ten
thousand pushed it through. The tell is underneath, in the volume bar.

- **A break on strong volume** means the crowd came with it: many participants agree the level matters
  and are acting. That break is far more likely to hold and follow through.
- **A break on weak volume** means few participants showed up. It is often a probe that quietly
  reverses — the classic false break, or "fakeout."

Concretely: resistance sits at 42,000 and has already rejected price twice. On the third attempt
price closes above it on volume three times the 20-bar average — hundreds of participants voting that
the level is gone. Compare that to a close above 42,000 on *half* the average volume: the same line
crossed, but almost nobody came. Same price geometry, opposite trustworthiness. Strong volume does
not *guarantee* the move continues, but a break with no participation behind it is the setup a fakeout
is made of.

The trap is treating the break itself as the signal. Because the price geometry is identical either
way, you have to look *down* at the volume to tell them apart. If you find yourself acting on a
breakout without having checked whether anyone came with it, you are trading the half of the picture
that lies most easily.
:::

## Absorption: big volume, little movement

Sometimes you see the opposite oddity — enormous volume, yet price barely moves. That usually means a
large participant is **absorbing**: soaking up everything thrown at the market at that price without
letting it move.

**Why it happens.** Picture price pinned at 42,000 while the volume bar runs five times its normal
height, but the candle's range is a tiny 0.1%. Sellers are hitting the market hard, yet a large
resting buyer keeps refilling a bid at 42,000 — an "iceberg" that absorbs each wave of selling as
fast as it arrives. All that supply is being eaten without the price giving way. It often precedes a
move in the direction of whoever did the absorbing — they were accumulating, not defending for fun.
You cannot see who it is or be certain of intent; heavy volume with no movement is a strong clue, not
a receipt.

## Price/volume divergence

Volume should roughly keep pace with the move it belongs to. When it does not, that gap is worth
noticing.

**What it looks like.** Price grinds to three successive new highs, but the volume behind each push
shrinks — bars of, say, 100, then 65, then 40. Each new high is being made by fewer participants than
the last; the rally is running on fumes.

**Why it leads price.** Like the momentum divergences from the oscillator module, a thinning volume
profile is an early read on participation, and participation fades before price does — the last buyers
to arrive are the fewest. So a price/volume divergence is a **warning that participation is
thinning**, not a dated appointment for a reversal. It tells you the fuel is running low; it does not
tell you the minute the tank runs dry.

## Confluence: don't trust one thing

The habit that ties this module to everything before it is **confluence** — refusing to act on a
single signal and waiting for independent things to agree. Structure (a real, tested level or trend),
an indicator (RSI or MACD), and volume are three different lenses. A breakout at a level that has
already held, on a clear volume surge, with the oscillator confirming, is worth far more than any one
of those alone. When the three disagree — a break at a good level but on dead volume — the honest read
is *not yet*. Confluence does not make you right; it stacks the odds in your favour and filters out the
noise that any single signal throws up on its own.

## How this burns people

Two mistakes account for most of the damage volume does to beginners.

:::note{type=warning}
**Trusting a low-volume breakout.** A break with no participation behind it is the single most common
way traders get trapped. The line is crossed, the chart looks decisive, and the probe quietly
reverses the moment the handful of buyers who pushed it run out. If the volume under the breakout
candle is not clearly above its recent average, treat the break as unconfirmed — a question, not an
answer.

**Reading volume in isolation.** Volume answers *how many*, never *which way* and never *by how much*.
A big red bar is not bearish and a big green bar is not bullish — both just mean "a lot of people
traded." Volume means something only (a) against a baseline, so you know whether the bar is actually
large, and (b) against price, so you know what all that participation was buying or selling into.
Strip away either context and you are staring at a number that tells you nothing.
:::

## Volume in a 24/7 market

Crypto trades around the clock, with no closing bell and no official daily volume print, and that
changes how you read the bars.

- **No session boundaries.** Because there is no open and close, "average volume" only means anything
  as a *rolling* figure — the last N bars — and even that breathes across the day. Liquidity ebbs and
  flows with the Asian, European and US hours; a bar that looks thin at 04:00 may be perfectly normal
  for that time of day. Compare like with like.
- **Thin weekend volume.** Saturday and Sunday participation is routinely a fraction of weekday
  participation. A breakout on a quiet Sunday clears a much lower bar to count as "strong volume," so
  weekend moves overstate their own conviction and are more prone to unwinding when the weekday crowd
  returns. Be extra sceptical of a break that happens while most of the market is asleep.
- **Wash-trading noise.** Reported volume is only as honest as the venue reporting it. Some exchanges
  have historically inflated their figures with **wash trading** — the same entity buying and selling
  to itself to fake activity — so raw volume on a thin or unregulated venue can be largely fiction.
  Prefer volume from venues with real order flow, and lean on aggregated or on-chain measures rather
  than trusting one small exchange's number.
