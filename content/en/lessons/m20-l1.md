# Trading styles: scalping, day trading, swing

Three traders can run the same account on the same exchange and lead completely different lives. What
separates them is not the coin or the indicator — it is **how long they hold** and **how often they
trade**. That single choice, the trading style, decides how much of your day the market eats, how
much you pay in costs, and which mistakes you are most exposed to. It is worth getting right early,
because almost everything else — your timeframe, your fee bill, even your sleep — falls out of it.

## The three styles, by holding period

The styles form a spectrum from fastest to slowest. Three things move together along it: the
**holding period** (how long a position stays open), the **trade count** (how many you take), and the
**timeframe** (which chart you read). Pick a point on one and the other two are largely decided for
you.

- **Scalping.** Positions last **seconds to a few minutes**. A scalper may take **dozens or hundreds
  of trades a day**, hunting tiny moves, and reads the fastest charts (tick, 1-minute, 5-minute).
  Nothing is held for long; most days end flat, with no open risk. *In practice:* a scalper works BTC
  on the 1-minute through a busy hour, clipping a few tenths of a percent at a time and closing each
  trade within ninety seconds.
- **Day trading.** Positions last **minutes to hours** and are **closed before the day is out** — a
  day trader does not carry a position overnight on purpose. A handful of trades a day is typical,
  read off the 5-minute to 1-hour charts. *In practice:* a day trader takes a morning breakout at
  09:00, manages it for a couple of hours, and is flat again well before the evening — the "day" is a
  single session, not several days.
- **Swing trading.** Positions last **days to weeks**. Trades are few — sometimes only a couple a
  week — and the decisions are made on the 4-hour and daily charts. The position stays open while you
  eat, work and sleep. *In practice:* a swing trader enters a daily-chart breakout on Monday and
  holds it through Friday, checking it for a few minutes each evening.

The boundaries are not laws; a "day trade" that runs a little long is not a sin. But the holding
period is the honest way to tell the styles apart — not how clever the setup looks, and not which
coin you happen to be trading.

## The two costs: fees and funding

Every style pays to trade, but the two big costs answer to **different clocks**. One tracks *how
often* you trade; the other tracks *how long* you hold. Getting this straight is the heart of
choosing a style, because each style is punished by a different one.

**Fees track the number of trades.** Every time you open or close you cross the **spread** (the gap
between the best bid and the best ask, which you give up when you take liquidity) and pay a **fee**
(the exchange's cut for matching your order). Both are charged **per fill** — once going in, once
coming out — so a single round-trip pays the toll twice. On one trade the toll is small; multiplied
by the number of trades, it is the biggest and most predictable cost you have.

- Because it is per-fill, this cost **scales with the number of trades, not with the size of the
  move**. A scalper doing 100 round-trips a day pays the toll 200 times a day, whether the market
  went anywhere or not.
- That is why a high-frequency style needs a genuinely **higher win rate just to break even**: before
  a scalper keeps a single cent, their edge has to beat the entire stack of fees and spreads. The
  busier you are, the taller that stack.

**Funding tracks the time you hold.** On **perpetual futures**, longs and shorts exchange a small
payment called **funding** every few hours (commonly every 8h). *Why it exists:* an ordinary futures
contract expires, and that settlement date is what drags its price back toward spot. A perpetual
never expires, so it needs another leash — funding is that leash. When the perp trades above the spot
price, longs pay shorts; when it trades below, shorts pay longs. It is not a fee the exchange keeps;
it is a transfer between the two sides that nudges the perp price back toward spot.

- Because it is charged on a clock, funding **scales with time held**. A scalper is almost never in a
  position when the funding clock ticks, so funding barely touches them. A swing trader holding for
  days crosses many funding payments — a cost (or, on the right side, a small credit) that quietly
  compounds over the hold.
- **Funding only exists on perpetuals.** Hold the same coin as **spot** or as a **dated future** and
  there is no funding at all. This is a crypto-specific quirk: the swing trader's funding bill is a
  feature of the *instrument*, not of holding in general.

## One move, three bills

Put numbers on it. Take a **10,000 USDT** position that captures a **3% gross move** — 300 USDT of
raw profit before any cost — and hand the same move to each style. The taker fee is **0.04% per
fill**, so each fill costs `10,000 × 0.0004 = 4 USDT` and each round-trip (in + out) costs **8 USDT**.
Funding runs at **0.03% per interval**, so each interval a held position owes `10,000 × 0.0003 = 3
USDT`.

- **Scalper — 15 round-trips, no funding.** Chops the move into 15 entries and exits: `15 × 8 = 120
  USDT` in fees. Flat when every funding clock ticks, so funding is zero. **Net: `300 − 120 = 180
  USDT`.**
- **Day trader — 1 round-trip, no funding.** In once, out once, closed before the day ends: `1 × 8 =
  8 USDT` in fees, no funding. **Net: `300 − 8 = 292 USDT`.**
- **Swing trader — 1 round-trip, 6 funding intervals.** One entry and exit (`8 USDT`), but holds
  across 6 funding payments: `6 × 3 = 18 USDT`. **Net: `300 − 8 − 18 = 274 USDT`.**

Same 300 USDT move, three different take-home results — **180, 292 and 274 USDT** — and the gap is
*entirely* cost. Fees punished the scalper's many round-trips; funding taxed the swing trader's
overnight hold; the day trader, in and out once with nothing held over, kept the most of this
particular move.

:::note{type=info}
Two honest caveats. First, this counts only the explicit fee — the **spread** you cross on every fill
sits on top, which hurts the scalper more still. Second, the scalper here is *idealised*: we assumed
they reassembled the whole 300 USDT move from 15 clean pieces. A real scalper takes 15 separate bets
hunting 15 small moves and will not perfectly rebuild one big one — so the cost side is, if anything,
understated. The module's final exercise makes you run these numbers yourself, with fresh values each
time.
:::

## A market that never closes

Crypto trades **24 hours a day, 7 days a week**. There is no closing bell, no weekend, no session the
exchange imposes on you — unlike a stock market, which shuts overnight and on weekends and hands you a
natural stopping point. In crypto you get none of that for free.

- For a **day trader**, this means there is no natural "day" — you have to *invent* your own session
  and, crucially, stop when it ends instead of drifting into a 14-hour screen marathon. The
  discipline the exchange used to enforce is now entirely on you.
- For a **swing trader**, it means your position is live while you sleep. The move that matters can
  happen at 3 a.m. in your timezone, and there is no overnight gap to wait out. You cannot watch it,
  so your **stop and your position size have to do the watching for you**.

The 24/7 clock is freedom and trap in one: you can always trade, which means you can always
over-trade.

## Time, attention and temperament

Each style asks for a different kind of person and a different slice of your life.

- **Scalping** demands continuous, intense focus for the whole session, fast reflexes and clean
  execution; costs and slippage matter enormously, and the emotional churn is high.
- **Day trading** asks for a few hours of concentrated attention and the discipline to close out and
  walk away.
- **Swing trading** takes only minutes a day to manage, but asks for **patience** and the stomach to
  hold open risk overnight without meddling.

Notice these are not degrees of the same thing: scalping's hard part is *attention*, swing trading's
hard part is *patience*, and the two rarely live comfortably in the same person.

## Matching a style to your real life

Pick the style your actual constraints can sustain, not the one that sounds most exciting.

- A **full-time job** during market hours makes scalping and day trading nearly impossible to do
  well — swing trading fits around a job.
- If **overnight risk wrecks your sleep**, day trading (flat by session's end) may suit you better
  than swing.
- If you are **impulsive or easily bored**, high frequency will bleed you through fees and forced
  trades faster than any single bad call.

## How this burns people

:::note{type=warning}
**"More trades means more profit."** This is the most expensive beginner belief in the module, and
the worked example above is the refutation: the scalper caught the same 300 USDT move as everyone
else and took home the *least*, purely because trading more often means paying the fee toll more
times. Frequency is a **cost, not a strategy**. A slow, selective trader with a real edge routinely
beats a busy one who is feeding the exchange.

**Picking a style that fights your schedule.** The second classic mistake is choosing the style that
looks thrilling rather than the one your life can carry. Scalp with a full-time day job and you will
trade distracted, half-watching a chart between meetings, and take the sloppy entries that
distraction produces. Swing trade when overnight risk keeps you up at night and you will panic-close
good positions at 3 a.m. A style that fights your schedule or your temperament does not just make you
uncomfortable — it walks you straight into broken rules and forced trades. The market does not reward
heroics; it charges for them.
:::

:::note{type=info}
There is no "best" style, and none is inherently more profitable. The best style for you is the one
that fits your schedule, your sleep and your temperament — because that is the one you can actually
run without breaking your own rules.
:::
