# What order flow adds

Everything you have read so far comes from one source: the OHLCV candle. Open, high, low, close,
volume — five numbers per period. Trend and range, levels, moving averages, RSI, MACD, volume
confirmation: every one of them is arithmetic performed on those five numbers.

That is a great deal of information, and none of it stops being true now. But a candle is a **summary**.
It tells you where price started, how far it stretched each way, where it finished, and how much
changed hands. It does not tell you *who* was doing the trading, or which side was the impatient one.
**Order flow** is the name for the data that does.

## A candle is an aggregate, not a record

**What it is.** A one-hour candle might contain forty thousand individual trades. It keeps four prices
and one total out of all of them and discards the rest. That compression is what makes charts readable
— but some of what it throws away is exactly what you wanted.

**Why it matters.** Picture two one-hour candles. Both close at 60,000, both have a 0.2% range, both
trade 5,000 contracts. In the first, buyers were the impatient side for almost the whole hour — they
kept lifting offers, and a large resting seller kept refilling and soaking them up. In the second, the
reverse: sellers pressed all hour into a buyer who would not move. Those are opposite situations —
supply quietly distributing into demand, versus demand quietly absorbing supply — and they print the
**same candle**. No arithmetic on OHLCV can separate them, because the difference was never in the
five numbers.

**In practice.** This does not make candles wrong; it locates their blind spot. When a candle reads
ambiguously — heavy volume with a tiny range, a new low that refuses to follow through — the ambiguity
is very often precisely where the discarded detail was.

## The vocabulary: maker, taker, aggressor

Every trade needs two parties, and they do not arrive the same way.

- A **maker** places a resting order into the book and waits. They are *offering* a price.
- A **taker** hits an order that is already resting there. They are *accepting* a price.
- The taker is the **aggressor**: the impatient side, the one who chose to trade *now* instead of
  waiting for a better price.

That asymmetry is the foundation of this whole block. Volume counts both sides of every trade equally —
m14's point that a trade always has a buyer and a seller, so volume can never be bullish or bearish by
itself. But the *taker side* is not symmetric. On any single trade, exactly one party paid for
immediacy. Tallying which one, trade after trade, is what order flow does.

One terminology note, because it trips people up: "taker buy volume" means the **taker was the buyer** —
someone lifted the offer. It does not mean "volume that was bought," which would be all of it.

## Delta: the number a candle hides

**What it is.** Add up the volume of every trade in a period where the taker was buying — that is
**taker buy volume**. Do the same for taker sells. The difference is the period's **delta**:

`delta = taker buy volume − taker sell volume`

A positive delta means aggressive buying dominated; negative means aggressive selling did.

**Why it is not volume.** Volume *adds* the two sides; delta *subtracts* them, and that changes what the
number can tell you. A period with 3,000 contracts of taker buying and 2,800 of taker selling has 5,800
volume and a delta of **+200**: a great deal of activity, almost perfectly balanced aggression. A period
with 600 of buying and 100 of selling has 700 volume and a delta of **+500**: far less activity, far
more one-sided. Volume answers *how many showed up*. Delta answers *which side was pressing, and by how
much*.

**Worked example.** Over one hour a perp trades 2,400 contracts on the taker-buy side and 1,750 on the
taker-sell side. Total volume is 4,150 — that is the figure a normal volume bar would show you. The
delta is 2,400 − 1,750 = **+650**: buyers were the aggressors on balance, by about 16% of the hour's
total activity. Now notice what you can say that you could not say from the volume bar alone: not just
that the hour was busy, but that the impatience in it leaned one way.

**A caveat worth having now.** Delta is real reported data, not a derived indicator — but it is
*venue-specific*, and its quality varies. Major exchanges label the taker side on their trade feeds
directly. Elsewhere it is *estimated*, classically with the "tick rule": assume a trade that printed at
the asking price was taker-buy and one at the bid was taker-sell. That estimate is decent and not
exact. Aggregate several venues together and you are stacking several such estimates. So treat delta
from one good venue as data, and cross-venue delta as a well-informed approximation.

## What this block covers — and what it honestly cannot

Four instruments, in the order they arrive:

- **m26 — CVD.** Delta accumulated over a whole session into a running line, and the divergence between
  that line and price. This one you will practise on generated charts.
- **m27 — the order book.** The resting orders themselves: depth, walls, imbalance. Taught
  conceptually.
- **m28 — premium between venues.** The same coin priced differently on different exchanges, and what a
  sustained gap means. This one comes with a calculation.
- **m29 — footprint charts and volume profile.** Volume distributed *by price* rather than by time.
  Taught conceptually.

Two of those four come with generated, gradeable chart exercises; two do not. That split is
deliberate and worth stating plainly: an honest exercise needs data this course can actually generate
credibly. A depth ladder and a footprint chart are a different *shape* of data from every time series
here — an instant snapshot of a book, a distribution inside a single bar — and faking them would teach
you to read a picture that isn't the one real tools show. Where that is the case, the lesson explains
the concept properly and names the real tools instead of pretending.

## A finer instrument, not a new idea

None of this is a new way of thinking about markets. It is a better measuring device for things this
course already taught you to look for:

- m14's **absorption** — heavy volume, no movement — is a guess made from a volume bar and a candle
  range. Order flow measures the same event directly.
- m09's **spring** and its test — a stab below support that recovers — is the story of selling being
  absorbed. Order flow is where you check whether it actually was.
- m12's **divergence** — price makes a new extreme, momentum refuses to confirm — is a pattern in an
  oscillator computed from price. m26 runs the identical logic one layer closer to the trades.

The habit stays what it was in m14: confluence. Order flow is another lens, and a good one. It is not
a lens that stops being wrong.

## How this burns people

:::note{type=warning}
**Treating delta as a direction signal.** A positive delta does not mean price will rise. Aggressive
buyers can spend an entire hour buying into a seller who never yields, and price ends the hour flat or
lower — that is absorption, and it is one of the most useful things order flow shows you. "Buyers were
aggressive" and "price went up" are different claims, and the gap between them is where the information
is.

**Assuming the aggressor is the informed side.** Being the impatient party is not a sign of being
right. Takers pay the spread and, on a perp, often the higher fee; a great deal of taker flow is retail
market orders, stop-losses firing, and liquidations being force-closed. Frequently the *maker* — the
one patiently absorbing — is the size that knows what it is doing. Read delta as pressure, not as a
verdict.

**Trusting a cross-venue number as if it were exact.** Aggregated delta is a stack of estimates from
venues with different reporting quality, and m14's wash-trading warning applies here too. Use it for
the *shape* — is aggression building or fading — and never quote it to the contract.
:::
