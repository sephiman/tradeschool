# Wyckoff: accumulation and distribution

**Wyckoff** is a way of reading a chart as a story about who is buying and who is selling. The core
idea is that big players — funds, desks, anyone moving size — cannot buy or sell everything at once
without moving the price against themselves. So they work quietly, over time, inside a sideways
range. That patient buying or selling leaves a recognisable shape on the chart. Wyckoff is a **lens
for reading that shape**, not a mechanical signal and not a guarantee.

This lesson is the **map**: the whole cycle, what a range is, and the two tells — spring and
upthrust — that give a range away. It stays at the overview level on purpose; a companion lesson
walks the life of a single range event by event. Here the goal is to see how the pieces fit.

## Why size has to hide

Start with the problem Wyckoff is built around, because every shape on the chart comes from it. A
desk that wants a large position cannot simply buy it. Say it wants **2,000 contracts** but the
visible order book only offers **150** near the current price. It has two choices: pay up through
every offer above — moving the price 8–10% against itself and announcing its intent to the whole
market — or feed small orders in over days and weeks, buying **100–200 at a time** on every dip,
letting sellers come to it. It picks the second. The reverse is true when it wants *out*: it sells
into strength in small clips so it does not crater the price it is trying to exit at.

That constraint is *why* a range forms at all. The sideways chop is not indecision — it is a large
order being worked in slices, and each slice needs a willing counterparty on the other side.

## The four-stage cycle

Wyckoff frames a market as moving through a repeating cycle of four **stages** (kept distinct here
from the lettered *phases* A–E that describe the life of a single range, below):

- **Accumulation** — after a downtrend, price stops falling and moves sideways. Large players are
  quietly *building* long positions from sellers who have given up. **Why here:** a downtrend
  manufactures the panicked, exhausted sellers a big buyer needs to fill against cheaply.
- **Markup** — accumulation is done; price trends up and leaves the range. The buying is finished,
  so nothing is left to cap price, and it lifts on thin supply.
- **Distribution** — after the markup, price stops rising and moves sideways again. Now large
  players are quietly *unloading* the positions they built, selling into eager buyers. **Why here:**
  an uptrend manufactures the confident, FOMO buyers a big seller needs to unload into.
- **Markdown** — distribution is done; price trends down and leaves the range. Demand is spent, so
  price falls under its own weight.

The trends (markup, markdown) are the parts everyone notices. The **ranges** (accumulation,
distribution) are where the important, invisible work happens. The cycle repeats because the same
constraint repeats: whoever accumulated eventually wants to distribute, and the crowd that bought the
top eventually capitulates into the next accumulation. The schematic below traces one full turn — a
prior downtrend stalling into a range, the phases A–E, the spring at phase C, and the markup that

## What a trading range actually is

A trading range is that boring sideways stretch bounded by a **support** below and a **resistance**
above. To most retail traders it looks like nothing is happening. Wyckoff's claim is that a great
deal is happening: a large player needs many sessions of two-way churn to fill a big order without
spiking the price, so the range *is* the accumulation or distribution.

Picture it concretely. Support at 100 has held for two weeks. Every time price sags toward 100, it
gets bought and lifts back to the middle; every push toward resistance stalls and fades. To a chart
watcher that is dead money. To Wyckoff it is a buyer sitting on the bid at 100, absorbing everything
offered, session after session, until the sellers are used up. The key question is therefore never
"is it going sideways?" but **"who is doing the absorbing, and on which side?"**

## The phases inside a range (A–E)

Wyckoff labels the life of a range with five phases. This is the vocabulary for the story — you do
not need to name them live, and the detailed, event-by-event walk belongs to its own lesson. For the
map, this is enough:

- **Phase A** — the prior trend stops. Selling (or buying) climaxes and the range's first boundaries
  form.
- **Phase B** — the long, choppy middle. This is where the "cause" is built: the bulk of the
  quiet buying or selling, back and forth between support and resistance.
- **Phase C** — the test. This is where the **spring** or **upthrust** appears.
- **Phase D** — the range starts to resolve, with price favouring one side and pushing toward a
  boundary.
- **Phase E** — price leaves the range: the markup or markdown begins.

Keep the two vocabularies apart: **stages** (accumulation → markup → distribution → markdown) name
the *cycle* the whole market moves through; **phases A–E** name the *interior life of one range*.

## The spring and the upthrust

These two are the tells this module drills, because they are the moments the range shows its hand.

- A **spring** is a false break *below* range support that quickly recovers back inside. Price dips
  under the floor — triggering the stop-losses and tempting the shorts — and then snaps back up. In
  Wyckoff's reading, that dip was large players grabbing the last cheap supply from panicked sellers.
  A spring sits inside **accumulation** and precedes a **markup**. *Concretely:* support holds at
  100 for two weeks; a burst of selling flushes price to 97, tripping the stops stacked just under
  100; within a session or two price reclaims 100 and holds. That 3% flush was the last supply
  changing hands, not a breakdown.
- An **upthrust** is the mirror image: a false break *above* range resistance that fails and drops
  back inside. Price pokes above the ceiling — triggering breakout buyers — and then rolls over. That
  poke was large players unloading into the last eager demand. An upthrust sits inside
  **distribution** and precedes a **markdown**. The decisive phase-C upthrust that marks the top of a
  distribution has its own name — the **UTAD** (upthrust after distribution) — to set it apart from
  the smaller, inconclusive upthrusts that can appear earlier, in phase B.

The logic behind both is the same trap: a break that everyone can see attracts orders, and those
orders are exactly the liquidity a big player needs to finish their position on the *opposite* side.
A visible support with stops parked beneath it is a pool of forced sell orders waiting to be
harvested; a visible resistance with breakout buyers above it is a pool of demand waiting to be sold
into. The distribution schematic below shows the mirror of the accumulation one: a prior uptrend, the

## How this burns people

:::note{type=warning}
Wyckoff is a heuristic, not a mechanism you can trust blindly. Two traps do most of the damage:

**Hindsight labeling.** Once price has already run, *every* range looks like a textbook
accumulation or distribution — the eye fits the schematic to a resolution it already knows. Live, you
have no such gift. A "spring" that keeps falling was just a breakdown; a "UTAD" that keeps climbing
was just a breakout. You only learn which one it was after the fact, and a chart you label after the
move proves nothing about your ability to call it before.

**Forcing the story.** Not every range is accumulation or distribution — plenty are just ranges that
break at random. Nobody rings a bell to confirm the phase. Treat the schematic as a *hypothesis*
about who is in control, demand confirmation before you act on it, and be willing to label a tidy
range with no clear prior trend and no false break as **none** rather than inventing a narrative for
it.
:::

## Wyckoff in crypto

The classic Wyckoff schematics come from early-1900s stock charts with a daily open and close and a
closing bell. Crypto changes the texture in ways that matter:

- **24/7, no close.** There is no session boundary to anchor the phases against and no overnight gap
  to reset the tape. A range a stock might build over 30 sessions can form and resolve over a single
  weekend — precisely when liquidity is thinnest and a move meets the least resistance.
- **Thin alt books make springs cheap.** In a shallow altcoin order book, a comparatively small
  market sell can wick 5–8% below support, sweep the stop-losses and liquidation levels clustered
  just beneath it, and reverse. Sometimes that is genuine accumulation; sometimes it is a deliberate
  **stop-hunt** — a whale manufacturing the spring on purpose to trigger the forced selling, then
  buying the flush. The shape looks identical, so a "spring" in a thin alt is far weaker evidence
  than the same shape in a deep, liquid market like BTC.
- **Funding and leverage cluster the stops.** Perpetual funding and heavy leverage pile stop-losses
  and liquidation prices into obvious spots just past support and resistance. That crowding is what
  makes the false break so reliable a harvest — and it also means a "markup" out of a spring can be
  nothing more than a **short squeeze**: crowded shorts being force-closed, not real demand.

:::note{type=info}
So the same shape carries less information in crypto than in the textbook. Weight a spring or upthrust
by the *depth of the book it happened in* and the *trend that led into it*, and distrust a clean
false break in an illiquid alt over a quiet weekend.
:::

## What to look for on the chart

1. Read the **prior trend** into the range. Accumulation follows a downtrend; distribution follows
   an uptrend. The trend before the range is half the story.
2. Find the **range** — the support and resistance the price has been bouncing between.
3. Look for a **false break** of a boundary that recovers: below support (spring) or above
   resistance (upthrust).
4. Combine them. A spring after a downtrend says *accumulation*; an upthrust after an uptrend says
   *distribution*. A tidy range with no clear prior trend and no false break is just a range —
   label it **none** rather than forcing a story onto it.
