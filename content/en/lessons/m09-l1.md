# Wyckoff: accumulation and distribution

**Wyckoff** is a way of reading a chart as a story about who is buying and who is selling. The core
idea is that big players — funds, desks, anyone moving size — cannot buy or sell everything at once
without moving the price against themselves. So they work quietly, over time, inside a sideways
range. That patient buying or selling leaves a recognisable shape on the chart. Wyckoff is a **lens
for reading that shape**, not a mechanical signal and not a guarantee.

## The four-stage cycle

Wyckoff frames a market as moving through a repeating cycle of four **stages** (kept distinct here
from the lettered *phases* A–E that describe the life of a single range, below):

- **Accumulation** — after a downtrend, price stops falling and moves sideways. Large players are
  quietly *building* long positions from sellers who have given up.
- **Markup** — accumulation is done; price trends up and leaves the range.
- **Distribution** — after the markup, price stops rising and moves sideways again. Now large
  players are quietly *unloading* the positions they built, selling into eager buyers.
- **Markdown** — distribution is done; price trends down and leaves the range.

The trends (markup, markdown) are the parts everyone notices. The **ranges** (accumulation,
distribution) are where the important, invisible work happens.

## What a trading range actually is

A trading range is that boring sideways stretch bounded by a **support** below and a **resistance**
above. To most retail traders it looks like nothing is happening. Wyckoff's claim is that a great
deal is happening: a large player needs many sessions of two-way churn to fill a big order without
spiking the price, so the range *is* the accumulation or distribution. The key question is never
"is it going sideways?" but **"who is doing the absorbing, and on which side?"**

## The phases inside a range (A–E)

Wyckoff labels the life of a range with five phases. You do not need to name them live — they are a
vocabulary for the story:

- **Phase A** — the prior trend stops. Selling (or buying) climaxes and the range's first boundaries
  form.
- **Phase B** — the long, choppy middle. This is where the "cause" is built: the bulk of the
  quiet buying or selling, back and forth between support and resistance.
- **Phase C** — the test. This is where the **spring** or **upthrust** appears.
- **Phase D** — the range starts to resolve, with price favouring one side and pushing toward a
  boundary.
- **Phase E** — price leaves the range: the markup or markdown begins.

## The spring and the upthrust

These two are the tells this module drills, because they are the moments the range shows its hand.

- A **spring** is a false break *below* range support that quickly recovers back inside. Price dips
  under the floor — triggering the stop-losses and tempting the shorts — and then snaps back up. In
  Wyckoff's reading, that dip was large players grabbing the last cheap supply from panicked sellers.
  A spring sits inside **accumulation** and precedes a **markup**.
- An **upthrust** is the mirror image: a false break *above* range resistance that fails and drops
  back inside. Price pokes above the ceiling — triggering breakout buyers — and then rolls over. That
  poke was large players unloading into the last eager demand. An upthrust sits inside
  **distribution** and precedes a **markdown**. The decisive phase-C upthrust that marks the top of a
  distribution has its own name — the **UTAD** (upthrust after distribution) — to set it apart from
  the smaller, inconclusive upthrusts that can appear earlier, in phase B.

The logic behind both is the same trap: a break that everyone can see attracts orders, and those
orders are exactly the liquidity a big player needs to finish their position on the *opposite* side.

:::note{type=warning}
Wyckoff is a heuristic, not a mechanism you can trust blindly. Ranges break for real in both
directions; a "spring" that keeps falling was just a breakdown, and you only know which it was in
hindsight. Nobody rings a bell to confirm the phase. Treat the schematic as a hypothesis about who
is in control — and demand confirmation before you act on it.
:::

## What to look for on the chart

1. Read the **prior trend** into the range. Accumulation follows a downtrend; distribution follows
   an uptrend. The trend before the range is half the story.
2. Find the **range** — the support and resistance the price has been bouncing between.
3. Look for a **false break** of a boundary that recovers: below support (spring) or above
   resistance (upthrust).
4. Combine them. A spring after a downtrend says *accumulation*; an upthrust after an uptrend says
   *distribution*. A tidy range with no clear prior trend and no false break is just a range —
   label it **none** rather than forcing a story onto it.
