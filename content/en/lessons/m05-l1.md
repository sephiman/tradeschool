# Leverage and margin

Leverage is the single feature that makes futures dangerous, and almost everything beginners believe
about it is slightly wrong. It does not make you more likely to win, and it does not raise the return
of a good trade. It changes exactly one thing: how much position you control per dollar you put up —
and everything else, including how fast you can be wiped out, follows from that.

## Notional vs margin: what you control vs what you paid

Two numbers describe every leveraged position, and keeping them apart is the whole game.

- The **notional value** is the full size of the position you control: `entry price × quantity`. Long
  0.5 BTC at an entry of 40,000 and your notional is `0.5 × 40,000 = 20,000` USDT. That is the amount
  that gains and loses money when price moves — a 1% move is 1% of the *notional*, not of your deposit.
- The **margin** is what you actually put up: a deposit the exchange holds as collateral. On the same
  position at 10× you post far less than 20,000. Leverage is exactly the ratio between the notional you
  control and the margin you post.

Beginners quote their leverage as if it were their profit. It is not. It is the size of the lever
between these two numbers, nothing more.

## Initial vs maintenance margin

There are two margin numbers, they mean different things, and confusing them is the most common early
mistake.

- **Initial margin** is what you must post to *open* the position — the notional divided by your
  leverage. On the 20,000 USDT long above, at 10× you post `20,000 / 10 = 2,000`; at 20× you post
  1,000; at 5× you post 4,000. Higher leverage → smaller initial margin for the *same* position. Why:
  leverage is precisely the fraction of the notional the exchange makes you fund up front.
- **Maintenance margin** is the minimum equity you must keep to *hold* the position open — a small,
  exchange-set fraction of the notional (often around 0.5%), well below the initial margin. On our
  20,000 notional at 0.5% that floor is just 100 USDT. Why it exists: it is the exchange's safety
  buffer, the point at which it closes you *before* your losses can turn your balance negative and
  leave it holding the bill.

So initial margin is the price of admission; maintenance margin is the eviction line. The **gap
between them is your breathing room**: on the 10× trade you posted 2,000 and are closed when equity
falls to 100, so your loss can grow to about 1,900 USDT — roughly a 9.5% adverse move on a 20,000
notional — before the position is liquidated. (Computing that liquidation price exactly is the next
module.)

:::note{type=info}
**Liquidation** in this course means one specific thing: the exchange **force-closing** your position
because your equity fell to the maintenance level. It is not you choosing to sell, and it is not the
market being "illiquid". Forced close, nothing else.
:::

## Cross vs isolated margin

The exchange lets you choose *which* balance stands behind a position. Take an account holding 3,000
USDT and the same 0.5 BTC long that needs 2,000 initial margin.

- **Isolated margin** puts only the margin you allocated to that one position at risk. You ring-fence
  2,000; if BTC falls far enough to liquidate it, you lose exactly that 2,000 and the other 1,000 in
  the account is untouched. Why use it: it converts a blow-up into a bounded, known loss — the most
  you can lose is the number you chose.
- **Cross margin** uses your whole account balance as collateral. Here all 3,000 backs the position,
  so the extra 1,000 acts as additional cushion and the trade survives a deeper drop before being
  liquidated. The catch: if the move keeps going, the loss keeps eating into that full 3,000 — a
  single bad trade can drag your *entire* balance to liquidation, not just the stake you had in mind.
  Why anyone uses it: a winning position can subsidise a losing one, and you suffer fewer liquidations
  from small wobbles.

Neither is "safer" in the abstract. Isolated caps the damage from one position; cross uses your full
balance to keep positions alive, at the cost of putting that full balance on the line.

## What leverage actually amplifies

Here is the part the marketing gets wrong. Leverage does **not** increase your expected return. The
price still has to move the same direction, the same amount, for the trade to work. A good trade is
good and a bad trade is bad, at 2× or at 50×.

What leverage scales is the size of the position per dollar of margin — and therefore two things
follow:

- **Your gains and your losses, symmetrically.** On the 2,000-margin trade at 10×, a 1% move in your
  favour is 200 USDT on the 20,000 notional — that is +10% on your margin; a 1% move against you is
  −10%. It cuts both ways, always, and identically.
- **How close your liquidation sits.** Higher leverage means a thinner margin cushion, so a smaller
  adverse move reaches your maintenance level and forces you out. At 10× the cushion is about 10% of
  price; at 20× about 5%; at 50× barely 2%.

Put together: leverage doesn't improve the odds of the move, it just raises the stakes and shortens
the distance to a forced close. Once you add fees and the risk of being liquidated by ordinary noise
*before* your thesis plays out, high leverage usually makes expected outcomes **worse**, not better.

## The leverage misunderstanding

Two mistakes on this topic drain more beginner accounts than any chart pattern ever will.

- **Believing leverage raises expected return.** The trader who picks 50× "to make more" has not made
  the trade any more likely to work; the price move needed is identical to the 5× version. All they
  changed is that a wiggle ten times smaller now liquidates them — and a position closed at a loss
  can't recover when price comes back, because it no longer exists. More leverage is more *risk per
  correct call*, not more edge.
- **Cross margin quietly staking the whole account.** A beginner opens on cross "for the extra
  cushion", puts on what they think of as a 500 USDT trade, and forgets that the entire balance is
  collateral. One sharp move against them keeps eating past the 500 and takes the whole account to
  liquidation. The stake they had in mind and the money actually at risk were never the same number.

:::note{type=warning}
The trap in one line: "more leverage = more profit." Only if you're right, and only until a normal
wiggle liquidates you first. The same 20× that doubles your upside on paper is why a 5% pullback —
noise, on most assets — can force your position closed at a loss you can never recover.
:::

## Leverage in crypto specifically

The generic story above is worse in crypto, for reasons particular to this market.

- **Absurd leverage is on the menu.** Exchanges routinely offer 100× or 125× on the majors, and high
  leverage on illiquid alts. At 100× your entire cushion is about 1% of price — and an alt that swings
  10% on an ordinary day breaches that in seconds. The number being *available* is not the number
  being *sensible*; the high tiers exist because they generate liquidations and fees, not because they
  help you.
- **Liquidation cascades.** Because crowds cluster at similar high-leverage liquidation prices, one
  forced close is a market order that pushes price into the next cluster, which forces the next close,
  and so on. In a thin alt-book this feedback loop overshoots violently — the "liquidation wick" that
  spikes and instantly recovers. (The mechanics are covered in the liquidation module.)
- **No close, thin weekends.** Crypto trades 24/7, so there is no daily close or circuit breaker to
  pause a move and no session gap to hide behind — you can be liquidated at 3am on a Sunday. Liquidity
  also thins on weekends and holidays, so a sudden move over a quiet weekend can slice through a whole
  cluster of liquidation prices with heavy slippage. A position that would have survived a weekday can
  be force-closed over a weekend on the very same news.
