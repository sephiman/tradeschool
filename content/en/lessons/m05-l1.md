# Leverage and margin

Leverage is the single feature that makes futures dangerous, and almost everything beginners believe
about it is slightly wrong. It does not make you more likely to win, and it does not raise the return
of a good trade. It changes exactly one thing: how much position you control per dollar you put up —
and everything else, including how fast you can be wiped out, follows from that.

## Margin: the deposit that backs the position

When you open a leveraged position you don't pay for the whole thing. You post **margin** — a deposit
the exchange holds as collateral. Two different margin numbers matter, and beginners confuse them
constantly.

- **Initial margin** is what you must post to *open* the position. It is the position's notional value
  divided by your leverage. Control 1,000 USDT of BTC at 10× and you post 100 USDT; at 20× you post
  50. Higher leverage → smaller initial margin for the same position.
- **Maintenance margin** is the minimum equity you must keep to *hold* the position open. It is a
  small, exchange-set fraction of the notional, well below the initial margin. Once your losses eat
  your equity down to that floor, the position is liquidated.

So initial margin is the price of admission; maintenance margin is the eviction line. The gap between
them — how far your losses can grow before you're closed — is your breathing room.

:::note{type=info}
**Liquidation** in this course means one specific thing: the exchange **force-closing** your position
because your equity fell to the maintenance level. It is not you choosing to sell, and it is not the
market being "illiquid". Forced close, nothing else.
:::

## Cross vs isolated margin

The exchange lets you choose *which* balance stands behind a position.

- **Isolated margin** puts only the margin you allocated to that one position at risk. If it
  liquidates, you lose exactly that allocated margin and the rest of your account is untouched. It
  turns a blow-up into a bounded, known loss.
- **Cross margin** uses your whole account balance as collateral. A winning position can subsidise a
  losing one — but a single bad trade can also drag your entire balance toward liquidation. More
  forgiving to small wobbles, far more dangerous to a real move against you.

Neither is "safer" in the abstract. Isolated caps the damage from one position; cross uses your full
balance to keep positions alive, at the cost of putting that full balance on the line.

## What leverage actually amplifies

Here is the part the marketing gets wrong. Leverage does **not** increase your expected return. The
price still has to move the same direction, the same amount, for the trade to work. A good trade is
good and a bad trade is bad, at 2× or at 50×.

What leverage scales is the size of the position per dollar of margin — and therefore:

- **Your gains and your losses, symmetrically.** At 10×, a 1% move in your favour is +10% on your
  margin; a 1% move against you is −10%. It cuts both ways, always.
- **How close your liquidation sits.** Higher leverage means a thinner margin cushion, so a smaller
  adverse move reaches your maintenance level and forces you out. (Computing exactly where is the
  next module.)

Put together: leverage doesn't improve the odds of the move, it just raises the stakes and shortens
the distance to a forced close. Once you add fees and the risk of being liquidated by ordinary noise
*before* your thesis plays out, high leverage usually makes expected outcomes **worse**, not better.

:::note{type=warning}
The trap: "more leverage = more profit." Only if you're right, and only until a normal wiggle
liquidates you first. The same 20× that doubles your upside on paper is why a 5% pullback — noise, on
most assets — can close your position at a loss you can never recover, because the position no longer
exists.
:::
