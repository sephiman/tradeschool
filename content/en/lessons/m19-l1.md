# Managing risk

The habit that separates traders who last from traders who blow up is not picking winners — it is
deciding, *before* every trade, exactly how much they are willing to lose. Risk management is that
discipline: sizing each position so that being wrong is survivable, and being wrong many times in a
row is *still* survivable.

## Risk a fixed, small fraction per trade

The core rule is boring and it works: on any single trade, risk a **fixed small fraction of your
account** — commonly **1%**, rarely more than 2%. "Risk" here means the money you lose if the trade
hits your stop, not the size of the position.

- On a 10,000 USDT account, 1% risk is **100 USDT**. That is the most this trade is allowed to cost
  you.
- The fraction stays fixed as the account grows and shrinks. Win, and 1% is a larger number; lose,
  and 1% is a smaller number. The rule sizes you down automatically in a drawdown — exactly when you
  need it to.

Why so small? Because you will be wrong often, and in streaks. If one trade can only cost 1%, ten
losses in a row cost about 10% — painful but recoverable. If one trade can cost 20%, two bad calls
have wrecked you.

## Size the position FROM the stop, not the other way round

Beginners pick a position size first ("I'll buy 2 units") and discover their risk afterwards. Do it
backwards. **The stop defines the risk; the risk defines the size.** The order of operations:

1. Decide your **risk budget** in money: equity × risk %. (10,000 × 1% = 100 USDT.)
2. Decide where your **stop** goes — the price at which you admit the trade is wrong — and measure
   the **distance** from entry to that stop.
3. **Position size = risk budget ÷ stop distance.** You lose the stop distance per unit if you are
   stopped, so holding that many units loses exactly your budget.

A wide stop therefore forces a *smaller* position and a tight stop allows a *larger* one — for the
**same** risk. The stop is not an afterthought; it is the input that sets everything else.

:::note{type=warning}
The most common sizing mistake is choosing size for the *reward* you imagine ("if this 5×es I want
a big bag") and letting the stop fall wherever. That inverts the whole method. Pick the risk first,
place the stop on the chart where the idea is genuinely invalidated, and let the arithmetic hand you
the size — even when the size comes out disappointingly small.
:::

## R-multiples: measure everything in units of risk

Once every trade risks the same fixed amount, that amount becomes your natural unit of measure. Call
it **R** — the money you put at risk on a trade.

- A loss at your stop is **−1R**.
- A win of three times your risk is **+3R**, whatever the dollar figure.
- A setup whose target sits three stop-distances away is a **3R trade** before you ever take it.

Thinking in R frees you from the dollar amount and points you at the ratio that actually matters. A
trader who wins only 40% of the time is still profitable if the winners average +2R and the losers
−1R. R-multiples let you judge a system by its shape, not by whether the last trade happened to feel
good.

## Losses hurt more than equal gains: drawdown asymmetry

Here is the arithmetic that ruins over-leveraged traders. **A percentage loss needs a *larger*
percentage gain to recover**, because the gain is calculated on a smaller base.

- Lose 10%, and you need +11.1% to get back to even.
- Lose 50%, and you need **+100%** — you have to double what is left.
- Lose 80%, and you need +400%.

:::note{type=warning}
The seductive wrong belief is: "a 50% drawdown just needs a 50% gain to recover." It does not. Going
100 → 50 is −50%, but climbing 50 → 100 is *+100%*. The deeper the hole, the more brutally this curve
turns against you — which is the whole reason to cap risk per trade before you ever get near a deep
hole.
:::

## Portfolio risk: correlated positions stack

Risking 1% per trade quietly assumes each trade is independent. Often it isn't. If you are **long
five different altcoins**, you do not really hold five separate 1% bets — in a market-wide sell-off
they fall together, and you effectively hold **one 5% bet** wearing five costumes.

- Positions that move together are **correlated**; their risks **add up** rather than diversifying
  away.
- Your real portfolio risk is your *total* exposure to a single move, not the number of tickets you
  are holding.
- Cap the risk of each correlated *cluster*, not just each line item — otherwise several "small"
  positions combine into one large, undiversified bet.

:::note{type=info}
Diversification only helps when the things you hold are genuinely uncorrelated. In a hard crypto
sell-off almost everything correlates toward 1 — the "diversified" book you built in calm weather
behaves like a single leveraged long exactly when it matters most.
:::
