# Managing risk

The habit that separates traders who last from traders who blow up is not picking winners — it is
deciding, *before* every trade, exactly how much they are willing to lose. Risk management is that
discipline: sizing each position so that being wrong is survivable, and being wrong many times in a
row is *still* survivable. Everything in this lesson concerns a **single trade on its own** — how to
size and cap one position. Holding several at once, where those caps stop being independent, is the
next lesson.

## Risk a fixed, small fraction per trade

**What it is.** The core rule is boring and it works: on any single trade, risk a **fixed small
fraction of your account** — commonly **1%**, rarely more than 2%. "Risk" here means the money you
lose if the trade hits your stop, not the size of the position and not the margin you post to open
it.

- On a 10,000 USDT account, 1% risk is **100 USDT**. That is the most this trade is allowed to cost
  you.
- The fraction stays fixed as the account grows and shrinks. Win, and 1% is a larger number; lose,
  and 1% is a smaller number. The rule sizes you down automatically in a drawdown — exactly when you
  need it to.

**Why so small.** Because you will be wrong often, and in streaks. Losing runs are not a sign you are
doing it wrong; they are normal and expected. Since 1% is taken from the *current* balance each time,
losses compound gently: ten losers in a row leave you with about **90.4%** of the account (0.99 to
the power of ten), i.e. down roughly 9.6% — painful but fully recoverable. Twenty in a row is about
81.8%, down some 18%. Now compare a trader risking 20% per trade: two bad calls (0.8 × 0.8 = 0.64)
and the account is already down 36%; three and it is nearly halved. A small fixed risk is what buys
you the right to be wrong repeatedly without being removed from the game.

**The frequency dimension.** The same percentage does not mean the same thing at every pace. 1% per trade
at 3 trades a week and 1% per trade at 15 trades a day are very different exposures per unit of *time*:
the fast trader can meet a perfectly ordinary run of eight losers in a single afternoon — roughly 8% of
the account gone before dinner — while the slow one meets the identical streak spread over a month, with
plenty of room to notice and slow down. (Why such runs are normal rather than malfunctions is the streak
arithmetic later in the course, **m22-l1**.) None of the formula changes; what changes is the honest
choice of fraction. The faster the style, the stronger the case for sitting at the low end — 0.5% rather
than 1% — and for capping the **day** as well as the trade, which is what the **daily stop** in the
trading-plan module (**m24-l1**) is for.

## Size the position FROM the stop, not the other way round

**What it is.** Beginners pick a position size first ("I'll buy 2 units") and discover their risk
afterwards. Do it backwards. **The stop defines the risk; the risk defines the size.** The order of
operations:

1. Decide your **risk budget** in money: equity × risk %. → 10,000 × 1% = **100 USDT**.
2. Decide where your **stop** goes — the price at which you admit the trade is wrong — and measure
   the **distance** from entry to that stop. → say the stop sits **250 points** away (a "point" here
   is one unit of price on the chart).
3. **Position size = risk budget ÷ stop distance.** → 100 ÷ 250 = **0.4 units**. If price runs the
   full 250 points against you, 0.4 units × 250 = exactly **100 USDT** lost — your budget, no more.

**Why this order.** Because the stop is the part of the trade the market can force on you, while the
size is the part you fully control. Fixing the loss first and solving for size means a **wide stop
forces a smaller position** and a **tight stop allows a larger one — for the same risk**:

- Same 100 USDT budget, stop **500 points** away → 100 ÷ 500 = **0.2 units** (half the size).
- Same 100 USDT budget, stop **100 points** away → 100 ÷ 100 = **1.0 unit** (five times the size).

The stop — not your conviction, not the size you *feel* like taking — sets the number of units. It is
the single input that fixes everything else.

:::note{type=warning}
The most common sizing mistake is choosing size for the *reward* you imagine ("if this 5×es I want a
big bag") and letting the stop fall wherever. That inverts the whole method. Pick the risk first,
place the stop on the chart where the idea is genuinely invalidated, and let the arithmetic hand you
the size — even when the size comes out disappointingly small. A small position on an honest stop
beats a large one on a stop you will not respect.
:::

## Leverage is not risk: the same trade at 5× and 20×

The most common beginner confusion in this whole course is that the leverage selector *is* the risk
dial — that 20× is four times as dangerous as 5×. The formula above already refutes it, but the point
deserves to be shown head-on, because the truth is more useful than the slogan: **the size came from
the stop, so the leverage cannot change it.**

Take one trade and change nothing except the leverage setting. Account **10,000 USDT**, risk **1%** =
**100 USDT**. Long BTC, entry **60,000**, stop **58,000** — a stop distance of **2,000**, so `size = 100
÷ 2,000 = 0.05 BTC`, a notional of `0.05 × 60,000 = 3,000 USDT`. Every one of those numbers came out of
the risk budget and the chart. The leverage selector was never an input.

**What is identical at 5× and at 20×:**

- **Position size** — 0.05 BTC either way. It is the quotient of two numbers, neither of which mentions
  leverage.
- **Notional** — 3,000 USDT either way.
- **Money lost at the stop** — `0.05 × 2,000 = 100 USDT` either way, exactly 1% of the account.

**What actually changes:**

- **The collateral you post.** At 5× the margin is `3,000 ÷ 5 = 600 USDT`; at 20× it is `3,000 ÷ 20 =
  150 USDT`. That is the honest use of leverage: the *same* position ties up less of your balance,
  leaving capital free for other things. It is a capital-efficiency lever, not a risk lever.
- **Where liquidation sits relative to your stop.** Using m06's formula for a long, `liq = entry × (1 −
  1/leverage + mmr)` with `mmr = 0.5%`:
  - At **5×**: `60,000 × (1 − 0.20 + 0.005) = 48,300`. Your stop is at 58,000, so liquidation sits
    **9,700 below it** — unreachable without the stop being hit first, several times over. It is simply
    irrelevant to this trade.
  - At **20×**: `60,000 × (1 − 0.05 + 0.005) = 57,300`. Still below the stop, but the cushion beyond it
    has shrunk from 9,700 to **700**.

Same trade, same risk, a quarter of the collateral — and a safety margin behind your stop that went
from enormous to thin.

**Where it actually breaks.** Keep turning the dial on the *same* trade. At **30×**: `60,000 × (1 −
0.0333 + 0.005) = 58,300`, and the liquidation price is now **above** your stop. The exchange
force-closes you at 58,300 — three hundred points before price reaches the 58,000 where you decided the
idea was wrong. Note carefully what did and did not change: the loss itself is no bigger (`0.05 × 1,700
≈ 85 USDT`, plus fees). What you lost is **control of the exit**. You are out of a trade whose thesis
was still alive, at a price you never chose, and a position the exchange closed cannot be re-opened at
the price you meant to hold from. Your stop stopped being your stop.

:::note{type=info}
That crossing point is the whole criterion for choosing a leverage number, and it is the only one you
need: **liquidation must never be your effective stop.** Pick the leverage that leaves the liquidation
price comfortably beyond the stop you already placed on the chart — with room to spare, because a
violent move can fill you further out than your stop price. Below that ceiling, leverage is arithmetic
about collateral. Above it, you have handed your exit to the exchange.
:::

The second-order danger is what the freed collateral invites. At 20× the same balance can carry
positions whose combined size no risk budget ever approved — so high leverage does not add risk to a
*sized* trade, it removes the friction that used to stop you from taking an unsized one. That is a
discipline problem rather than an arithmetic one, and it is waiting for you in the failure modes below.

## R-multiples: measure everything in units of risk

**What it is.** Once every trade risks the same fixed amount, that amount becomes your natural unit of
measure. Call it **R** — the money you put at risk on a trade (here, 100 USDT).

- A loss at your stop is **−1R**.
- A win of three times your risk is **+3R**, whatever the dollar figure.
- A setup whose target sits three stop-distances away is a **3R trade** before you ever take it.

**Why it matters.** Thinking in R frees you from the dollar amount and points you at the ratio that
actually decides whether you make money: the size of your winners *relative to* your losers. Take a
run of ten trades in which you win only four:

- 4 winners × **+2R** = +8R
- 6 losers × **−1R** = −6R
- **Net = +2R** over the ten trades — about +200 USDT — despite losing **60%** of the time.

A 40%-win-rate system is profitable when the winners are big enough; a 70%-win-rate system loses money
when the losers are too big. R-multiples let you judge a system by its *shape* — win rate against
payoff — rather than by whether the last trade happened to feel good. (Exactly how win rate and payoff
combine into an expected value has its own lesson later in the course.)

## Losses hurt more than equal gains: drawdown asymmetry

**What it is.** Here is the arithmetic that ruins over-leveraged traders. **A percentage loss needs a
*larger* percentage gain to recover**, because the recovery gain is calculated on the smaller base
that the loss left behind:

- Lose **10%** → you need **+11.1%** just to get back to even.
- Lose **20%** → you need **+25%**.
- Lose **33.3%** (a third) → you need **+50%**.
- Lose **50%** → you need **+100%** — you have to double what is left.
- Lose **75%** → you need **+300%**.
- Lose **90%** → you need **+900%**.

**Why it curves like that.** The recovery gain is a percentage of what *remains*, not of what you
started with. Going 100 → 50 is a 50% loss, but climbing 50 → 100 is a +100% gain, because the 50 you
must double is now your entire account. Every further percent of drawdown removes capital *and* raises
the bar for the recovery at the same time, so the curve turns against you faster the deeper you fall —
which is the whole reason to cap risk per trade before you are ever near a deep hole.

:::note{type=warning}
The seductive wrong belief is: "a 50% drawdown just needs a 50% gain to recover." It does not — it
needs **+100%**, a doubling. This is the failure mode wearing the costume of common sense, and it is
why traders who "only" lost half their account so rarely climb back: they are trying to clear a bar
twice as high as the one they fell from.
:::

## How a good system still blows up

Two of the traps above are so common they deserve to be named on their own: **sizing for the reward
instead of the stop**, and believing **"a 50% loss just needs a 50% gain."** Those are the beginner
versions. The mistakes that catch people who already *know* the rule are quieter:

- **Moving or widening the stop once you are in.** Price nears your stop, you decide the level "needs
  more room," and you slide it further away. You have just converted a defined 1% risk into an
  open-ended one — the fastest way to turn a planned small loss into an account-ending one.
- **Oversizing "just this once."** After a winning streak, or to make back a loss quickly, you take a
  trade at 5% or 10% risk. One oversized trade can undo the protection of a hundred disciplined ones.
  The rule only works if it has no exceptions.
- **Confusing leverage with position size.** "I'm only on 3× leverage" says nothing about your risk.
  Risk is set by *how many units you hold and where your stop is*, not by the leverage setting. You
  can be recklessly oversized at 2× and perfectly sized at 20× — that is the two-scenario demonstration
  above, and the only thing the leverage number decides on a properly sized trade is whether
  liquidation can reach you before your stop does.

:::note{type=warning}
Every one of these is the same act: overriding the arithmetic because a trade *feels* different. The
method has no power unless the size it hands you is the size you actually take — especially on the
trades where you would rather not.
:::

## Why oversizing is lethal here

The mechanics above are universal, but crypto sharpens the cost of getting them wrong.

**Volatility plus leverage makes oversizing lethal.** A 10–15% move in a day is ordinary for an
altcoin and would be a once-a-decade shock in a blue-chip stock. Size a crypto position as if a 2%
move were the worst case and an unremarkable Tuesday will hit your stop; oversize it with leverage on
top and that same Tuesday can take a large slice of the account. Leverage by itself does not add risk
*if* you size from the stop — that is the demonstration above — but crypto is what makes the *cushion*
matter. The 20× case left only 700 points between the stop and liquidation, about 1.2% of price, and in
a fast market price can jump straight through a stop by more than that. A margin that looks safe on a
calm chart is not automatically safe in a cascade, which is the practical reason to sit well under the
crossing point rather than just beneath it.

**Gap risk, 24/7.** Crypto never closes, which sounds safer than a market that gaps over a weekend —
it is not. A stop is a *request* to exit at the next available price, not a guarantee of your price.
In a violent move — a liquidation cascade, an exchange outage, thin weekend liquidity, a news shock at
3 a.m. — price can jump straight past your stop and you are filled well beyond it. Your "1% risk"
becomes 2% or 4% realised. Treat the 1% as a ceiling that holds in calm conditions, not a floor the
exchange guarantees, and size with a little room for the fill to come out worse than planned.

:::note{type=info}
Everything here sizes **one** trade on its own. The moment you hold several positions that move
together — five long alts in the same sell-off — their separate 1% risks stop being independent and
stack into a single larger bet. Sizing that correlated exposure is the subject of the next lesson,
**Portfolio risk** (m19-l2).
:::
