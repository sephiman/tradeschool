# Portfolio risk

The previous lesson sized each trade on its own: risk a fixed 1%, place the stop, let the arithmetic
hand you the size. That method quietly assumes something that is often false — that your trades are
**independent**, that being wrong on one says nothing about the others. This lesson is about what
happens when that assumption breaks. **Portfolio risk is your total exposure to a single market move,
no matter how many separate positions you have split it across.**

## Correlation: when two positions are really one

**Correlation** measures how strongly two positions move together, on a scale from +1 to −1.

- **+1** — they move in lockstep: when one rises 5%, so does the other.
- **0** — they are independent: one tells you nothing about the other.
- **−1** — they move exactly opposite: a perfect hedge.

Why this decides everything: **risk only diversifies away when correlation is below +1.** Two
positions at +1 are not two bets — they are the *same* bet held twice. The number of tickets open in
your account is irrelevant; what matters is the number of *independent* bets those tickets represent.
Ten longs on ten coins that all move together is one bet wearing ten costumes.

## Aggregate directional exposure

To see your real risk, stop counting positions and add up your **directional exposure**: how much you
stand to lose if the whole market moves against you at once.

The test is a single question: **"If the market dropped 10% right this second, what would I lose?"**
Add up the answer across every correlated position. That number — not the count of tickers, not the
per-trade 1% caps — is your portfolio risk. If it is larger than you would knowingly stake on one
idea, you are over-exposed, however many lines it is spread across.

A long and a short in correlated instruments partly cancel each other (that is a *relative* bet, and
it carries far less directional risk). But five longs on five correlated alts cancel nothing — they
stack into one large directional bet: "alts go up."

## The three correlated longs, worked in full

Here is the arithmetic that catches people. Account: **10,000 USDT**. You open **three** long
positions on three different alts — shitcoins, in the trader's honest vernacular — and you size each
one correctly by the last lesson's rule: **1% risk, 100 USDT, each with its stop placed 10% below
entry.**

Sizing each position from its stop:

- Size it so that a 10% adverse move costs exactly 100 USDT → **notional = 100 ÷ 0.10 = 1,000 USDT
  per position.**
- Three positions → **3,000 USDT of long exposure**, i.e. 30% of the account all pointed the same
  way.

You feel diversified: three coins, three tickets, three separate "small" 1% bets. Now a market-wide
sell-off arrives and — because these alts move together — all three fall about **10% at the same
time**. Each one hits its stop:

- Position A: **−100 USDT**
- Position B: **−100 USDT**
- Position C: **−100 USDT**
- **Total: −300 USDT = 3% of the account, from a single move.**

Those were never three independent 1% risks. Correlated, they are **one ~3% bet in three costumes.**
Had the coins truly been independent, the odds of all three stopping out in the same window would be
low, and the losses would have arrived spread out over time — giving you room to react between them.
Correlated, they arrive together, inside a single candle.

:::note{type=warning}
This is how the diversification illusion burns people: the *feeling* of being diversified (many
names, many tickets) hides a *concentrated* bet (one direction, one correlated complex). Splitting one
3% idea across three coins does not turn it into three 1% ideas — it just makes the 3% harder to see.
Diversification is about independent risks, never about the number of tickers on your screen.
:::

## Crypto nuance: alts track BTC more than they look

In calm markets, alts *look* independent — each one pumps or dumps on its own narrative, its own
listing, its own influencer. That is exactly what lulls traders into treating them as separate bets.
But correlation is not a constant. **In a drawdown, crypto correlations snap toward +1:** when people
de-risk, they sell everything at once and the per-coin stories stop mattering. The diversification you
thought you measured in calm weather evaporates precisely when you need it.

Worse, most alts have a **beta above 1** to BTC — they fall *harder* than BTC does. On a day BTC drops
10%, alts commonly drop 15–25%. So the 10% stops in the example above get blown straight through: on a
thin alt book you are not filled at −10%, you are filled lower, after slippage. Each position then
loses *more* than its 100 USDT budget, and the "3% bet" quietly becomes 4% or worse — all of it
landing in one move, 24/7, including on a thin weekend when there is barely anyone to fill you.

:::note{type=info}
If those positions share one margin pool (cross-margin), a correlated drop draws them all down against
the *same* collateral at once, which can push several of them toward **liquidation** — the exchange
force-closing them — simultaneously. Positions that looked independent can fail as a single cascade.
:::

## What to actually do

- **Cap the cluster, not just the line.** Decide a maximum risk for each correlated *group* (for
  example, "no more than 2% at risk across all my alt longs combined"), then size the individual
  trades to fit inside that ceiling.
- **Count independent bets, not tickers.** Before adding a position, ask whether it is genuinely a new
  idea or the same bet in a fresh costume.
- **Stress-test the book.** Regularly ask the one question — "market −10% right now, what do I lose?"
  If the answer surprises you, you are carrying more portfolio risk than you thought.
