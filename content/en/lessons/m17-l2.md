# Liquidity maps and squeezes

The first lesson read the crowd from a distance — open interest for how *backed* a move is, funding
for how *one-sided* it is. This lesson zooms all the way in to the order book: *where* the exits and
protective orders are stacked, why price is so often drawn to run through them, and how, when the
crowded side is leveraged, that run turns into a self-feeding cascade — a squeeze. None of this
predicts direction. It maps the fuel and shows you where the market tends to burn it.

## Liquidity vs. liquidation — two different words

Keep these strictly apart, because the topic muddles them at every turn:

- **Liquidity** is resting orders waiting to be filled — limit orders and, crucially, *stop orders*
  parked at prices where they will trigger. Deep liquidity means there are lots of orders to trade
  against.
- **Liquidation** is the exchange **forcibly closing** a leveraged position when its margin can no
  longer cover the loss. A forced close, and nothing else.

A liquidity map (or "liquidation heatmap") tries to show where both pile up. They pile up together,
which is why the words get tangled — but they are not the same thing, and this lesson uses each one
literally.

## Where the pools form

Traders reason alike, so their orders bunch in the same few places.

- **Below a recent low** sits a shelf of **long stop-losses** — a long's protective sell order goes
  *just under* the low that "should hold." Stacked in the same zone are the **liquidation prices of
  leveraged longs**, which also sit below entry. Both are *sell* orders waiting to fire.
- **Above a recent high** sits a shelf of **short stop-losses** — a short's protective buy order goes
  *just above* the high — plus the **liquidation prices of leveraged shorts**, which sit above entry.
  Both are *buy* orders waiting to fire.

**Why they cluster:** everyone is looking at the same chart. The obvious swing low, the round number,
the level that held twice — that is where "put your stop just beyond it" sends thousands of orders to
the same handful of ticks. The map is not random; it is built by shared, predictable reasoning.

Worked picture, using the numbers you will see drawn in the figure below: BTC has a swing low around
**25,900** that has held three times. Longs everywhere set stops just under it, around **25,850**;
leveraged longs opened near the low have liquidation prices spread out beneath that, down to about
**25,400**. That whole band is a dense shelf of resting sell orders — a pool of liquidity sitting right
below price.

## Why price sweeps them

A large player who needs to fill size has a problem: buying a lot lifts price against them, and
selling a lot drops it against them. They need a **pool of opposite orders** to fill into quietly. A
shelf of stops is exactly that pool.

Follow the BTC example. A player wanting to accumulate a big long cannot buy at 25,900 without pushing
it higher. Instead, in **a single candle**, price drops from the 26,200 it was trading at all the way
to **25,350**, cutting through the entire shelf: the long stop-losses trigger as market sells, the
leveraged longs down there are liquidated (more forced selling), and all of that selling is absorbed
by the large player's resting buy orders. That same candle closes back at **26,220**, far above the low
it just pierced. That is a **sweep** (a "stop run" or "liquidity grab"): the people who were stopped
out and liquidated sold the exact low, and their orders were the liquidity someone else filled into.

:::note{type=info}
This is why the last push into an obvious level so often *overshoots* it and reverses. The overshoot
is not a failed breakout — it is the pool being taken. The tell is the snap-back: a genuine break
holds beyond the level; a sweep pokes through it and reclaims within a candle or two.
:::

## Anatomy of a squeeze

:::note{type=info}
**One word, two mechanisms.** The squeeze in this lesson is a *market event*: real participants are being
forcibly closed, and their forced orders push price the way that forces more of them. **m32-l1** teaches a
completely unrelated thing under the same name — a **volatility squeeze**, which is a *measurement*: one
envelope drawn on a chart sitting inside another. Nobody is forced to do anything in that one. The two
share a word and nothing else, so when you meet "squeeze" in the wild, the first thing to settle is which
of them is being claimed — they differ on whether anything is happening at all.
:::


A sweep becomes a **squeeze** when the pool that fires is a *crowded, leveraged* one, so the orders
that trigger are not just voluntary stops but **liquidations** — and each forced close pushes price
into the next cluster of forced closes. It runs in four phases:

1. **Build.** One side crowds in with leverage. Funding goes one-sided (from lesson 1), and OI climbs
   on that side. The fuel is loaded.
2. **Trigger.** Price reaches the cluster — often via a deliberate sweep — and the first liquidations
   fire.
3. **Cascade.** A liquidation is a *forced* order in the direction that hurts the crowd: shorts
   force-closed must **buy**, longs force-closed must **sell**. That forced flow moves price into the
   next shelf, which fires, which moves price again — self-feeding.
4. **Exhaustion.** The crowded side empties. With no more forced orders behind it, the move that was
   pure mechanics simply stops — and usually snaps back, because nothing durable was ever driving it.

**Short squeeze:** crowded shorts → price forced up → shorts buy to close → higher → more shorts
liquidated → cascade *up*. **Long squeeze:** crowded longs → price forced down → longs sell to close →
lower → more longs liquidated → cascade *down*. The same machine, mirrored.

## A worked short squeeze

ETH has bled lower for days. Funding is deeply **negative** (shorts paying to stay short) and OI has
climbed the whole way down — a **crowded short**. The recent high is **3,000**; short liquidation
prices stack in shelves above it: around 3,050, 3,150, and 3,300.

- **Trigger.** A burst of spot buying lifts ETH to 3,000 and pokes above. The first short stops (buy
  orders) and the 3,050 liquidations fire — forced *buying*.
- **Cascade.** That buying lifts price to 3,150, hitting the next shelf; those forced buys carry it to
  3,300, hitting the next. In under an hour ETH runs from 3,000 to **3,450** — about **15%** — on no
  news that justifies it. The move is the crowded short side being emptied, not fresh conviction.
- **Exhaustion.** By 3,450 the crowded shorts are gone; funding has flipped positive. There are no
  more forced buyers behind price. It stalls and bleeds back toward 3,150. The violent leg was
  mechanical, and mechanics run out.

## Arriving late to a squeeze

:::note{type=warning}
The classic mistake is **chasing the squeeze after it is spent.** The cascade looks exactly like a
powerful breakout, so a beginner buys the ETH move at 3,400 believing a new trend has started. But the
fuel was forced buying from a *finite* pool of shorts — and by 3,400 that pool is nearly empty. You
are buying precisely as the mechanical bid disappears, which makes you the liquidity the early sellers
take profit into. The move that looked strongest was weakest: it was ending, not beginning.
:::

A second, quieter mistake: parking **your own** stop just inside an obvious pool. If you can see the
shelf, so can everyone — including whoever benefits from sweeping it. Put stops *beyond* the round
number and the swing extreme, not one tick inside the crowd.

## Why cascades run unopposed here

The generic version of this exists in every leveraged market. Crypto amplifies all of it:

- **24/7, no closing bell.** There is no overnight pause to let a crowded position cool off. Cascades
  fire at 3am your time and run unopposed.
- **Extreme leverage.** Crypto perps allow 50–100×+, so liquidation prices sit *right next to* entry.
  Clusters are dense and go off on tiny moves — a ~1% wick can liquidate a 100× position, so the
  trigger in phase 2 needs almost nothing.
- **Thin books, especially weekends and alts.** Less resting liquidity means the same forced flow
  moves price much further. Weekend and holiday sessions on thin books, and small-cap alts any day,
  produce the most violent cascades — the wick overshoots because there was nothing there to absorb
  it.

## Trading around the map, not at it

Read the map as zones of **volatility and likely sweeps**, not as targets to trade toward: a cluster tells
you where it may get violent, not which way the day ends. And keep your stops **outside** the obvious pool
— beyond the level, not one tick inside it.

When a squeeze is actually running, the honest edge is **fading exhaustion**, never chasing the cascade —
and if you cannot tell which phase you are in, the correct trade is none. Combine that with lesson 1:
one-sided funding plus a fat cluster on the crowded side is loaded fuel. The data still does not predict
the spark; it shows you a market primed to burn.
