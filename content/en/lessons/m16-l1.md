# Mass sentiment

Price is set by people, and people move in herds. When almost everyone feels the same way about the
market at the same time, that agreement itself becomes information — usually a warning. **Mass
sentiment** tools try to measure that mood. The best known in crypto is the **Fear & Greed index**,
and like every tool in this course it is useful only once you know exactly what it measures and what
it doesn't.

## What the Fear & Greed index actually is

**What it is.** The index (the widely used one is published by alternative.me) is a single number
from **0 to 100**: 0 is "extreme fear", 100 is "extreme greed". It is a *composite* — it does not
measure one clean thing, it blends several inputs and boils them down to one score. The published
version weights them roughly like this:

- **Volatility (~25%)** — how turbulent price is versus its own recent average. Calm, orderly moves
  read as confidence (greedy); sudden, violent swings read as panic (fearful). *Why it is in there:*
  fear shows up as jagged, whippy price before people can put it into words. **Computed entirely from
  the price series.**
- **Market momentum and volume (~25%)** — how strong and stretched recent buying is versus normal.
  Heavy, sustained buying into a rising market is the fingerprint of a crowd piling in. *Why:* greed
  *is* chasing, and chasing is visible as momentum and volume. **Also computed straight from price
  and volume.**
- **Social media (~15%)** — the volume and tone of posts about crypto. *Why:* a flood of excited
  posting is the signature of a crowd that is emotionally, not analytically, engaged.
- **Bitcoin dominance** — whether money is crowding into Bitcoin or spilling out into speculative
  alts. *Why:* in a scare, money flees to the "safe" corner of crypto (dominance rises → fear); when
  the crowd feels bold it chases riskier alts (dominance falls → greed).
- **Search trends** — Google search interest in crypto terms. *Why:* a spike in "bitcoin crash" or
  "is bitcoin dead" is fear; a surge in "buy bitcoin" is greed.

**Why this matters — the half that is really just price.** Look hard at the first two inputs.
**Volatility and momentum are both derived from price itself**, and together they are about **half
the score**. So the index is, to a large degree, a re-description of recent price action wearing an
emotional label. That is the single most important thing to remember about it: it is *not* an
independent second opinion that confirms what price is doing. Much of the time it is the same
information you already have from the chart, relabelled as a feeling.

**In practice.** Imagine BTC grinds smoothly from 40,000 to 60,000 over three weeks, with only
shallow dips along the way. Watch the needles: volatility is low and orderly (greedy), momentum and
volume are strong and stretched (greedy), timelines fill with celebration and price targets (greedy),
and "buy bitcoin" searches spike (greedy). The composite prints something like **88 — extreme
greed**. But notice what actually happened: the rising price *fed most of those inputs*. The index
did not independently discover euphoria; the price did the work, and the index simply put a name on
it. That is why a fresh price high and an extreme-greed reading almost always arrive together — they
are largely the same event described twice.

## What it measures — and what it doesn't

It measures **the tone of the recent past**: a fast rally with heavy volume and euphoric posts scores
greedy; a sharp drop with panic search spikes scores fearful. That is a real, useful summary of the
crowd's current mood.

It does **not** measure any of the things a beginner assumes it does. It does not know the future. It
does not tell you where price is going next, when a move will turn, or how much room is left. Because
its heaviest inputs are computed from price that has *already* printed, the index can only ever tell
you how the recent past felt — it is a lagging, backward-looking read of *how it has felt lately*,
not a forecast.

:::note{type=info}
Sentiment is a **context** indicator, not a signal generator. It answers "how crowded and one-sided
is the mood right now?" — never "should I enter here?". Treat the number as a description of the
weather, not an instruction.
:::

## Greed and euphoria: a zone for caution

**What it is.** When the index sits in **extreme greed**, the crowd is euphoric — confident,
leveraged, and already long. **Euphoria** is the emotional state where people buy *because* price is
rising and cannot imagine it falling.

**Why it warns you.** The contrarian logic is simple and honest: if almost everyone who wanted to buy
has already bought, there are few buyers left to push price higher, and a lot of nervous late longs
who will sell fast the moment it dips. A market can only rise while there is fresh money still willing
to lift the offer; extreme greed is the condition where that fresh money is running out. So extreme
greed does not mean "sell now" — it means **the ground is crowded: size down, tighten risk, stop
chasing.**

**In practice.** Concretely, this is what a greedy top *looks* like from the inside: the index has
sat above 90 for a week, funding is deeply positive (longs are paying shorts every few hours just to
hold the position), your feed is wall-to-wall price targets, and every dip is bought within minutes.
Every one of those is a symptom of the *same* fact — the buy side is already all-in. That is the
moment to be smaller and more careful, not braver.

## Fear and capitulation: a zone of opportunity

**What it is.** At the other end, **extreme fear** means the crowd is scared, and its endpoint is
**capitulation** — the moment holders finally give up and sell at a loss just to stop the pain of
watching it fall further.

**Why it marks opportunity.** By the same logic inverted: once the people who were going to panic
have already sold, the selling pressure that was driving price down is largely spent, and price is
often near a low. A market can only keep falling while there are still frightened holders left to
sell; capitulation is the point where that supply exhausts itself. That is why the deepest fear tends
to appear near bottoms, not tops.

**In practice.** Capitulation usually looks like a single violent session: BTC drops 15% in a few
hours on enormous volume, a cascade of **liquidations** (forced closes of over-leveraged longs) dumps
even more supply into a thin book, the index gaps down to 6, and social flips to "it's over, crypto
is dead." That flush — panic, forced selling, and a spike of fear all at once — is what a capitulation
low can look like. Again, though: this is a **zone of interest**, a place to *look* for setups, not a
command to buy.

## Why it is a zone, not a trigger

Here is the mistake that costs people money. They see the index hit 10 and buy, or hit 90 and sell,
as if the number were an entry button. **Extremes can persist for weeks or months.** Greed stayed
extreme through entire bull runs while price kept climbing; fear stayed extreme through long grinding
bear markets while price kept bleeding. A contrarian who shorted the *first* "extreme greed" print,
or bought the *first* "extreme fear" print, got run over — the crowd stayed lopsided far longer than
their account could survive.

Sentiment marks a **contrarian zone** — a region where the odds and the risk/reward start to shift,
and where you should get *more careful* about the crowded side. It does not mark a moment. The
trigger to actually enter still has to come from your own tools: structure, a level, confirmation.
Sentiment tells you *which side is dangerous*; it never tells you *when*.

## Extremes are not timing

The classic trap is treating an extreme reading as a precise buy or sell signal. "Index at 8, back up
the truck" and "index at 92, short it" are exactly how contrarians get liquidated early: they are
standing in front of a trend that is still being fed, on the strength of a number that only says the
crowd is lopsided — not that the lopsidedness is about to end. An extreme can deepen and stay extreme
for a long time, and a fixed-size contrarian bet placed *at* the extreme has to survive all of it.

There is a second, quieter trap: **double-counting**. Because half the index is price in disguise, a
beginner will "confirm" a bullish price breakout by pointing at rising greed — and think they have two
independent reasons when they really have one reason counted twice. The index is not a second witness
to what price is telling you.

:::note{type=warning}
**How this burns people.** Extremes tell you the crowd is one-sided; they say **nothing** about
timing, and they do not turn the moment you notice them. Never treat 8 or 92 as an entry button, and
never mistake a greedy reading for independent confirmation of a rising price — it is largely the same
price, relabelled. Wait for a trigger from your own structure and levels, and size for the fact that
the extreme can get more extreme.
:::

## Crypto-specific nuance

Crypto sharpens all of this. Traditional markets close; **crypto runs 24/7**, so the mood never gets
a nightly reset. Sentiment compounds through overnight and weekend sessions on thin liquidity, and an
extreme can be reached *and* unwound while you sleep — the index you check at breakfast may already
have swung from panic to relief and be lagging both moves.

Crypto euphoria is also **socially driven** to a degree stocks rarely see. A single viral thread, an
influencer call, or a new exchange listing can spike social volume, search interest, and price *at the
same time* — and remember three of the index's inputs feed on exactly those things, so one narrative
can push the whole gauge into extreme greed fast. Persistent positive **funding** can then bankroll
that greed: as long as longs are willing to keep paying to hold, the crowded, euphoric state can last
far longer than "it must be exhausted" suggests.

Finally, the headline index tracks **Bitcoin and the broad market**, not each coin. A single **alt**
can have a wildly euphoric or terrified crowd of its own — on a thin book, where a handful of orders
moves price and sentiment violently — while the market-wide index reads calm. In alts, sentiment is
even more local, faster, and more extreme than the one number on the dashboard suggests.
