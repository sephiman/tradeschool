# What an exchange is and who holds your coins

An exchange is where you turn one asset into another — dollars into bitcoin, bitcoin into a
stablecoin. That part sounds simple, and the trading mostly is. What catches people out is quieter:
**for as long as your coins sit on an exchange, the exchange is holding them for you.** This module
is about how the trading works and, more importantly, who actually controls your money at each step.

## Centralised vs decentralised: CEX and DEX

A **centralised exchange (CEX)** is a company. You open an account, you pass identity checks, you
deposit funds, and the company matches your buy against someone else's sell on its own **order
book**.

*Why it works this way:* a single operator can match orders in microseconds, run a support desk, and
undo its own mistakes — which is exactly what makes it fast and familiar. The catch is the price of
that convenience. When you deposit, your coins go into *the exchange's* wallets, and your balance
becomes a number in the company's database — a promise to pay, not the coins themselves.

*In practice:* you sign up to a mainstream exchange, deposit 500 USDT, and buy BTC in one tap.
Nothing touches a blockchain — the exchange just moves numbers between its own internal ledgers.

A **decentralised exchange (DEX)** is a set of programs (smart contracts) running on a blockchain.
You trade directly from your own wallet; the trade settles on-chain and the coins never leave your
control except to complete the swap.

*Why it works this way:* the matching and the custody are done by public code, not a company, so
there is nobody to open an account with and nobody who can freeze you. That same design removes the
safety net — no support desk, no password reset, and a different set of risks: buggy contracts,
network fees, and mistakes that are final because you signed them yourself.

*In practice:* on a DEX you connect a wallet, approve the contract, and swap; you pay a network fee
and the tokens land in your wallet seconds later. Approve a *malicious* contract, though, and it can
drain that wallet — and no one can undo it.

:::note{type=info}
Neither model is "safe" in the abstract. A CEX concentrates trust in one company; a DEX shifts that
trust onto code and onto you. Beginners usually start on a CEX for the convenience — just be
clear-eyed that convenience is exactly what you are paying for.
:::

## Spot trading: owning the asset itself

**Spot** trading means buying or selling the asset itself for immediate settlement: you pay now, you
get the coin now.

*Why the name matters:* spot is the opposite of the futures contracts covered later, where you post
margin and never take possession of the coin — you only settle the price difference. Spot is the
plain case: your gain or loss is simply the coin's price, and you can never be force-closed, because
you owe nobody anything.

*In practice:* you spot-buy 0.10 BTC at 30,000, so 3,000 USDT leaves your balance and 0.10 BTC
arrives. On a CEX that BTC is still an IOU until you withdraw it to your own wallet, but economically
you own the asset and its full price swing.

## Market orders vs limit orders

Two order types cover almost everything you'll do at the start.

- **Market order** — "fill me now, at whatever the best available price is." *Why:* it executes
  immediately by taking the offers already resting in the book, so you are guaranteed to trade — but
  you are *not* guaranteed a good price.
- **Limit order** — "fill me only at this price or better." *Why:* you name your price and wait, so
  you control the price you pay — but the order may sit unfilled forever if the market never reaches
  it.

The trade-off is always the same: a market order buys certainty of *execution* at the cost of
certainty of *price*; a limit order does the reverse. The order book below shows exactly what that
costs.

## Reading the order book

The **order book** is the live list of what everyone is willing to trade and at what price. It has
two sides:

- **Bids** — buy orders, stacked from the highest price down.
- **Asks** — sell orders, stacked from the lowest price up.

The gap between the best bid and the best ask is the **spread**. The size resting at each price is
the **depth**.

*Why this shapes your fill:* a market order walks the book, filling against each level in turn, so a
large order in a **thin** book (little depth) gets progressively worse prices as it climbs. The gap
between the price you expected and the price you actually got is **slippage**.

*Worked example.* Suppose the asks on a thin altcoin read: 500 units at 2.00, then 800 at 2.02, then
1,500 at 2.05. You send a **market buy for 2,000 units**:

- 500 × 2.00 = 1,000
- 800 × 2.02 = 1,616
- 700 × 2.05 = 1,435
- Total: 4,051 USDT for 2,000 units → average **2.026**.

You saw 2.00 on screen and paid an average of 2.026 — about **1.3% slippage**, before fees. A **limit
buy at 2.00** would instead have filled only the 500 units actually offered at 2.00 and left the
other 1,500 waiting until sellers came down to your price. That is the whole choice in one picture:
the market order got all 2,000 but at a worse average; the limit order protected the price but might
never complete.

:::note{type=warning}
On a wide spread or a thin book, a market order can fill far from the price you saw a second ago.
This is one of the most common ways beginners lose money before their view is even wrong — not to a
bad call, but to a careless order. On a deep BTC book that 2,000-unit order is nothing; on a
small-cap alt it moves the price itself. When in doubt, use a limit order.
:::

## Who actually holds your coins

Here is the distinction the whole module is built around.

- **Exchange (custodial) custody:** the exchange holds the private keys; you hold a login and a
  balance. *Why it is convenient and fragile:* the company can reset your password and fix your
  mistakes precisely because it controls the coins — which means it can equally freeze, lose, or
  spend them. Your balance is a **liability on the exchange's books**, pooled with everyone else's
  coins in its wallets.
- **Self-custody:** you hold the private keys in your own wallet. *Why it is powerful and
  unforgiving:* no company can freeze or lose your coins for you — and no company can help you if you
  lose the keys or sign a bad transaction. The responsibility is entirely yours.

*In practice:* when a custodial exchange becomes insolvent, its customers are not protected
depositors — they are **unsecured creditors**. Exchanges that looked untouchable have frozen
withdrawals overnight and entered bankruptcy, leaving customers to wait years for a court to hand
back cents on the dollar. That is the day the difference between "coins" and "an IOU for coins" stops
being academic.

This is the meaning of the old warning **"not your keys, not your coins."** Coins on an exchange are
a claim on a company; coins in a wallet you control are yours. Keeping funds on an exchange to trade
is a reasonable trade-off; keeping your *savings* there is quietly making that company your bank — an
unregulated one, with none of a bank's guarantees.

## Crypto's hard edges: finality and a market that never closes

Two properties make crypto custody unlike a brokerage account, and both cut against you when you're
careless.

- **On-chain settlement is final.** *What and why:* once a blockchain transaction is confirmed, there
  is no operator who can reverse it — that irreversibility is the entire design. *In practice:* send
  a withdrawal to a wrong address, or over the wrong network, and the coins are simply gone; there is
  no chargeback and no support ticket that brings them back. In self-custody the same finality
  applies to *signing* — approve one malicious transaction and the wallet can be emptied with no
  recourse.
- **The market runs 24/7.** *What and why:* there is no closing bell and none of the "circuit
  breakers" a stock exchange uses to pause trading, because there is no central operator to pause it.
  *In practice:* liquidity thins out overnight and at weekends, so the same market order slips more
  and price can gap hard while you sleep. Thin alt books make this worse — a size that is trivial on
  BTC can move a small-cap several percent.

## Locking down a CEX account

If you do use a CEX — and most people do — the boring hygiene matters more than any single trade
you'll place. Each control below limits the *blast radius* when something goes wrong.

- **Two-factor authentication (2FA).** *What:* a second proof of identity on top of your password.
  *Why:* passwords leak constantly through data breaches and phishing, so a password alone is not a
  lock. *In practice:* use an **authenticator app** (a rotating six-digit code), not SMS.
- **Scoped API keys.** *What:* a credential a program uses to reach your account instead of your
  password. *Why:* any key can be leaked, or the program holding it can be breached, so you grant it
  only the powers it actually needs. *In practice:* a portfolio tracker gets a **read-only** key; a
  trading bot gets a trade-enabled key with **withdrawals disabled**. An API key that can withdraw is
  a spare key to your funds.
- **Withdrawal address whitelist.** *What:* an allowlist of the addresses your account is permitted
  to send to. *Why:* it breaks the last step of almost every account takeover — actually moving the
  coins out. *In practice:* pre-approve your own wallet addresses and put a time delay (say 24–48h)
  on new ones. Then even an attacker who logs in as you cannot send coins to an address you never
  approved.

## How this burns people

The disasters in this module are rarely exotic. Three plain mistakes account for most of them.

- **SMS 2FA and the SIM-swap.** Text-message codes feel like security, but they ride on your phone
  number — and an attacker who social-engineers your carrier into porting that number receives every
  code. People with strong passwords have been cleaned out this way overnight. An authenticator app
  has no number to steal.
- **Leaving everything on the exchange.** The exchange is the most convenient place to keep coins and
  the worst place to store wealth. Convenience is fine for the working balance you actively trade;
  the day an exchange freezes withdrawals, everything still sitting there is frozen with it.
- **Over-scoped API keys.** A tracker or a "trading signals" tool asks for API access and the
  beginner pastes in a key with full permissions — withdrawals included. If that third party is
  breached or simply dishonest, the key withdraws the funds directly, and because the transfer
  settles on-chain it is final before you even notice.

:::note{type=warning}
Notice the theme running through all three: security isn't about being un-hackable, it's about making
sure a single failure can't empty the account. Un-hackable is not on the menu; **bounded loss** is.
Every control above exists to turn a total loss into a survivable one.
:::
