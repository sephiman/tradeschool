# What an exchange is and who holds your coins

An exchange is where you turn one asset into another — dollars into bitcoin, bitcoin into a
stablecoin. That part sounds simple, and the trading mostly is. What catches people out is quieter:
**for as long as your coins sit on an exchange, the exchange is holding them for you.** This module
is about how the trading works and, more importantly, who actually controls your money at each step.

## Centralised vs decentralised: CEX and DEX

A **centralised exchange (CEX)** is a company. You open an account, you pass identity checks, you
deposit funds, and the company matches your buy against someone else's sell on its own **order
book**. It is fast, familiar, and has a support desk. The catch is that when you deposit, your coins
go into *the exchange's* wallets. Your balance is a number in the company's database — a promise to
pay, not the coins themselves.

A **decentralised exchange (DEX)** is a set of programs (smart contracts) running on a blockchain.
You trade directly from your own wallet; the trade settles on-chain and the coins never leave your
control except to complete the swap. There is no account and no company holding your funds — but also
no support desk, no password reset, and a different set of risks: buggy contracts, network fees, and
mistakes that are final because you signed them yourself.

:::note{type=info}
Neither model is "safe" in the abstract. A CEX concentrates trust in one company; a DEX shifts that
trust onto code and onto you. Beginners usually start on a CEX for the convenience — just be
clear-eyed that convenience is exactly what you are paying for.
:::

## Spot trading and the two orders you must know

**Spot** trading means buying or selling the asset itself for immediate settlement: you pay now, you
get the coin now. (This is the opposite of the futures contracts covered later, where you never take
possession.) On a CEX your "coin" is still an IOU until you withdraw it, but economically you own the
asset.

Two order types cover almost everything you'll do at the start:

- **Market order** — "fill me now, at whatever the best available price is." It executes immediately
  by taking the offers already resting in the book. You are guaranteed to trade; you are *not*
  guaranteed a good price, especially in a thin market.
- **Limit order** — "fill me only at this price or better." You name your price and wait. You control
  the price you pay, but the order may sit unfilled forever if the market never reaches it.

## Reading the order book

The **order book** is the live list of what everyone is willing to trade and at what price. It has
two sides:

- **Bids** — buy orders, stacked from the highest price down.
- **Asks** — sell orders, stacked from the lowest price up.

The gap between the best bid and the best ask is the **spread**. The amount of size resting at each
price is the **depth**. A market order walks up (or down) the book, filling against each level in
turn — so a large order in a **thin** book (little depth) gets progressively worse prices as it goes.
That gap between the price you expected and the price you actually got is **slippage**.

:::note{type=warning}
On a wide spread or a thin book, a market order can fill far from the price you saw a second ago.
This is one of the most common ways beginners lose money before their view is even wrong — not to a
bad call, but to a careless order. When in doubt, use a limit order.
:::

## Who actually holds your coins

Here is the distinction the whole module is built around.

- **Exchange (custodial) custody:** the exchange holds the private keys; you hold a login and a
  balance. Convenient, recoverable if you forget a password, and completely dependent on that company
  staying solvent and honest. If it freezes withdrawals, gets hacked, or goes under, your IOU is only
  worth whatever the company eventually pays back.
- **Self-custody:** you hold the private keys in your own wallet. No company can freeze or lose your
  coins for you — and no company can help you if you lose the keys or sign a bad transaction. The
  responsibility is entirely yours.

This is the meaning of the old warning **"not your keys, not your coins."** Coins on an exchange are
a claim on a company; coins in a wallet you control are yours. Keeping funds on an exchange to trade
is a reasonable trade-off; keeping your *savings* there is quietly making that company your bank.

## Basic account security

If you do use a CEX — and most people do — the boring hygiene matters more than any trade you'll
place:

- **Two-factor authentication (2FA)** via an authenticator app, not SMS. SIM-swap attacks make
  text-message codes the weakest common option.
- **Scoped API keys.** If you connect a bot or a tracker, give it a **read-only** key, or a
  trade-only key with **withdrawals disabled**. An API key that can withdraw is a spare key to your
  funds.
- **Withdrawal address whitelist.** Lock withdrawals to addresses you've pre-approved, ideally with a
  time delay on new ones. Then even an attacker who gets into your account can't send coins somewhere
  new.

:::note{type=info}
Notice the theme: every one of these limits the damage when — not if — something goes wrong.
Security isn't about being un-hackable; it's about making sure a single failure can't empty the
account.
:::
