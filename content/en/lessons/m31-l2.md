# Wedges and directional compression

**m08-l2** taught you a single candle whose range collapses: a small body, small wicks, a bar where almost
nothing happened. The reading was that disagreement has run out of energy, and that when it comes back
it tends to come back hard.

This lesson is that phenomenon at the scale of a **pattern**. Instead of one narrow bar you get twenty,
each a little narrower than the last, squeezed between two lines that are closing on each other. Same
mechanic, different scale — and, because it takes twenty bars instead of one, you can watch it happen.

## Compression with a direction

Take the channel from **m31-l1** and let the two lines converge instead of running parallel. Now each
swing costs less than the one before: price still travels from floor to ceiling and back, but the
journey shortens every time. That is the whole family.

What separates its members is **how the convergence is shared** between the two lines, and that is worth
holding onto because it turns six named patterns into one object seen from six angles:

- **Symmetric triangle** — the ceiling comes down as fast as the floor comes up. Nothing about the
  shape points anywhere.
- **Ascending triangle** — a flat ceiling, a rising floor. Buyers keep paying more; sellers keep
  offering at the same price.
- **Descending triangle** — the mirror: a flat floor, a falling ceiling.
- **Rising wedge** — both lines rise, but the floor rises *faster*. Price is making higher highs and the
  ceiling is conceding less each time.
- **Falling wedge** — both lines fall, the ceiling falling faster.

The figures show one of each end of that range. First a rising wedge, where the whole convergence is
carried by the floor outrunning the ceiling:

...and then a symmetric triangle, where the same convergence is split evenly. Read the two side by side
and the "six patterns" become what they are — the same coil with the work divided differently. Both are

*(Flags and pennants belong to this family too — a very short, very steep version after a sharp move.
They are a minor variant and this course does not treat them separately.)*

## The directional bias, split in two

The canon attaches a direction to some of these: **"rising wedges break down, falling wedges break up."**
That sentence is two claims wearing one coat, and they are not worth the same.

**The part with a mechanism.** In a rising wedge, buyers are making progress and it is costing them
more each time: every new high is a smaller step, against a ceiling giving ground more slowly than the
floor is gaining it. That is *observable exhaustion of a rhythm* — the same reading **m14**'s price/
volume divergence gives you, and it says something real: whoever is buying is having to work harder for
less. It says the rhythm is running down.

**The part without one.** "Therefore it breaks *down*." Nothing in the geometry forces that. The
convergence tells you the rhythm is ending; it does not tell you which side is standing when it does.
Every textbook percentage attached to this — the "68% of rising wedges resolve downward" kind — comes
from a pattern somebody counted on a chart set they chose, with a definition of "wedge" they chose,
and it is not built on a mechanism you can point at. It is **statistics without a mechanism**, which is
exactly what **m13** says about a Fibonacci ratio and **m10-l1** about the 200-day average. If a tilt
exists, the exhaustion is enough to explain it; what does not exist is the percentage that quantifies
it, or the permission to front-run the break.

So use the first half and hold the second loosely: a rising wedge tells you buyers are tiring. Where
price goes next you read from the break, like anything else.

## Resolution

Compression resolves into expansion. That much is not a prediction — it is what compression *is*, and
it is the same statement **m32** makes about a whole market regime.

**The break follows the same discipline as everything else in this block:**

- **A body closing beyond the line**, not a wick (**m08-l1**, and see **m31-l1** on why a wick through a
  diagonal is worth even less).
- **Participation behind it** (**m14**). A coil is quiet by construction, so volume falls off through
  it — which makes the surge on a genuine break easy to see, and its absence just as easy.

The conventional target is the **base height projected from the break**: measure the widest part of the
shape, and add that distance to the point where price left it. Treat this as a **convention, not a
promise.** It is a reasonable way to pick a first target because a lot of people use the same one, which
is the same self-fulfilling argument **m13** makes about the 0.618 — and it has the same expiry date.
Nothing about a coil's opening width obliges the move that follows to be that size.

:::note{type=warning}
The failure mode specific to this pattern: **a coil that has not resolved yet is not a signal.** A
narrowing shape can keep narrowing for a long time, and it can leave through either line. Entering
"because the wedge is about to break" is entering on a shape, which is the m31-l1 error — thesis first,
line second — with an extra line drawn on it.
:::
