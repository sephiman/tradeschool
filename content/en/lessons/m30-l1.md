# The SMC dialect (order blocks, FVG, BOS)

If you have spent ten minutes on trading Twitter or YouTube you have met these words: *order block*,
*FVG*, *BOS*, *liquidity grab*, *premium and discount*. They are presented as a system — "Smart Money
Concepts" — and that packaging makes it sound as though there is a body of knowledge this course skipped.

There isn't. Almost all of the substance is already taught, under other names: liquidity pools and
sweeps in **m17-l2**, the spring and absorption in **m09**, the change of character in **m08-l1**, role
reversal and support-as-a-*zone* also in **m08-l1**, the liquidity void in **m08-l2**. What is missing is
not mechanics. It is the dictionary.

So this lesson does to the SMC lexicon exactly what **m08-l2** did to the named Japanese candle patterns:
it maps each name onto a mechanic plus a location, never as a standalone signal. And it takes the same
stance **m13** takes towards Fibonacci and **m10-l1** towards the 200-day average: **this dialect is
worth understanding because the crowd speaks it**, not because it predicts. Those are not the same claim,
and the difference is what the last section is about.

## The origin zone of an impulse (the "order block")

Start with the mechanical problem rather than the name. A participant who needs to execute size cannot
fill it all at one price: buying heavily pushes the price against you (**m17-l2**). So they work an
area, absorbing what is sold to them, until the available supply runs out and price leaves in a hurry.
That is, word for word, m09's buyer absorbing inside the range.

Now look at the trace that leaves on a chart. The move that breaks structure sets off from **the last
opposing candles before it**: a few down candles, and then the rally. If the size that caused the rally
was working there and never got fully filled, what is left is still there. That is why a return to that
area meets buying more often than an arbitrary price does. The dialect calls that area an **order
block**; here we call it the impulse's **origin zone**, which is what it is.

Notice two things that keep that sentence honest. First, it is an **inference**, not an observation.
Nobody announces anything and the order book shows nothing; what is being reasoned is that size capable
of moving price like that was unlikely to fit at a single price. Second, it is a **zone**, like every
support in this course (**m08-l1**), because it is defined by the extremes of a handful of candles and
because the reaction is expected *somewhere inside it*.

And above both of those, the order matters: **the break comes first.** Without a clean close beyond the
high the market had been respecting, there is no impulse that broke anything, and the candles before it
are an ordinary pullback of the kind every trend is full of. This is the costliest error in the whole
dialect: the zone gets drawn first and the structure is never checked, which means a zone can be found
on any chart at all.

The figure walks the full sequence with numbers. The range had been testing a high near **1,960**. The
last down candles before the impulse run from **1,810** to **1,855**: that shaded band is the origin
zone. Price then closes at **1,965**, above the high it had been respecting — that is the break. And it
is not a wick poking through: what decides is the body, as in **m08-l1**, and the impulse goes on to
close as high as **2,045**, more than 4% clear of the high it broke. It comes back, wicks into the band
down to **1,834**, holds it and leaves; the closing stretch — the one an exercise cuts off — carries it
above **2,100**.

:::note{type=warning}
An origin zone is not a promise. It fails constantly, and when it fails the dialect tends to relabel it —
it was "mitigated", or the "real" block was the one above — which is how failures leave the record and
successes stay in it. Treat it the way **m08-l1** treats any level: a place where something is *more
likely* to happen, needing the same close and the same retest you would want anywhere else.
:::

## The imbalance (the "fair value gap")

**m08-l2** already warned you about this phenomenon from the other end: a very long wick printed in a
thin weekend book is a **liquidity void**, a stretch almost nothing traded through, and it should be
distrusted. That warning stands. What **m08-l2** left out is that the same emptiness, when a fast move
leaves it *behind*, is a **zone**, and that price returns to it often enough to be worth a name.

The test is geometric, and it is about the **neighbouring** candles rather than the big one:

- Take three consecutive candles. If the **high** of the first sits below the **low** of the third, the
  span between those two prices was crossed **inside a single bar**.
- A span crossed inside one bar is a span where almost nothing that was resting there got hit. Almost
  nothing, not nothing: the middle candle did trade through it. That word is the difference between a
  description and a story.

The useful consequence follows. On the far side of that span sat orders that wanted to trade and could
not, and whoever joined the move did so without the price reference a traded range provides. Coming back
there is coming back to somewhere with business left unfinished, so price revisits it often. Mind the
word: **often**, not always.

In the figure, the impulse candle opens at **26,780** and closes at **28,320** — almost 6% in a single
bar. The previous candle's high is **27,100** and the next candle's low is **28,000**, so that roughly
900-point span — the shaded band — was crossed inside that one bar. For the rest of the window price
stays clearly above it and the gap stays open; the closing stretch is it coming back for it, the wick
reaching down to **27,320**.

::figure{id=fig-m30-imbalance}

Two things an imbalance is **not**. It is not a session gap: traditional markets close and reopen with a
jump, and crypto runs 24/7 (**m10-l1**), so this is always an imbalance *inside* a continuous series. And
it is not a big candle. An equally fast move whose candles overlap traded through its entire range and
left nothing behind — what qualifies is what the neighbours did.

:::note{type=info}
A gap price has already traded back through is **spent**. What made it a zone was the *absence* of trade
in that span; once something trades there, the thing being pointed at is gone. This follows from the
definition, which makes it one of the few rules in this dialect that is not open to argument.
:::

## BOS: the twin that never got a name

**m08-l1** taught you the ladder — higher highs and higher lows — and named the break that **ends** it:
the change of character, **CHoCH**, the first lower low in an uptrend.

Its twin went unnamed there. An uptrend making *another* higher high is also breaking a swing level, just
one that **confirms** the pattern instead of contradicting it. That is a **BOS** (break of structure).

One ladder, two kinds of break: the one that continues the sequence and the one that ends it. The second
acronym adds a word, not an idea — and **m08-l1** now names it too, where the ladder lives.

## The vocabulary map

Here is the dictionary, with the mechanic each term renames and the module where you already learned it:

- **Order block** — the impulse's **origin zone**: the last opposing candles before the move that broke
  structure. *m08-l1* (role reversal, zones rather than lines) and *m09* (absorption).
- **Fair value gap / imbalance** — a **span crossed inside a single candle**, where almost nothing
  traded. *m08-l2*'s liquidity void, now taught as a zone as well.
- **Liquidity grab / sweep** — a **sweep** of a pocket of resting stops, with the level reclaimed
  immediately. *m17-l2*, and *m08-l1*'s stop-hunting wicks.
- **BOS / CHoCH** — the **two breaks of the ladder**: the one that continues the sequence and the one
  that ends it. *m08-l1*.
- **Premium / discount** — **above or below the midpoint** of a range you drew yourself. *m13*, the 0.5
  line.

On that last one, because it is the one that gets stretched furthest: **premium** and **discount** mean
*you are in the upper or the lower half of a range you drew*. It is a real observation — buying nearer
the bottom of a range you have identified beats buying nearer the top — and it stops there. The word
sounds like a measurement of value and is not one. Where this tips into numerology is the bands with
decimal places, the "optimal trade entry is the 0.705–0.79 retracement" kind: **m13** already settled
that no mechanism forces a market to respect a ratio, and that all such a level has going for it is that
plenty of people watch it. A number with three decimal places has *less* of that going for it, not more.
And in crypto the word collides with two genuinely different things that do have mechanisms: the
**inter-venue premium** (**m28**) and the perp-versus-spot **basis** (**m20**).

The conclusion is **m08-l2**'s, in another language: **there is no new system, there are three mechanics
with more names.**

## Where the dogma leaves the mechanics

This is where the dialect parts company with what this course is willing to assert, and it is worth
seeing exactly at which point.

Compare two accounts of the same wick through an obvious low:

- *"Stops and liquidation prices cluster just under an obvious low, so a move into that band triggers a
  burst of selling that someone on the other side absorbs."*
- *"Smart money engineered that wick to take retail's liquidity before the real move."*

The first names a mechanism with parts you can point at: where protective orders sit and why they cluster
(**m17-l2**), and what forced selling does to a thin book (**m06-l1**). You can argue with it, check it,
and be wrong about it.

The second names an actor and a motive. Ask which observation would contradict it: if price reverses,
they engineered it; if it does not, they were accumulating. None would. A claim that explains every
outcome equally well predicts none of them, which is why this course describes the same event without it.
It is the same boundary **m25** draws around order flow and **m29** around the footprint: say what the
tool does, say where it stops, and never let a story stand in for a mechanism.

Two more versions of the same move, because they come up daily:

**The zone drawn in hindsight.** A chart posted with four zones marked and reactions at three of them is
a chart where the candles that reacted are also the candles that suggested where to draw. The dialect is
unusually exposed to this: zones are bands, bands can be nudged, and every impulse has candles before it,
so a hindsight reader will always find one near a reaction. The test is whether the sequence was complete
**beforehand**: break, zone, return, decision.

**The tendency promoted to a law.** "An unfilled gap *has to* be filled." No. Gaps are revisited often
and some are never revisited at all, especially the ones a trend ran away from. The *has to* is where the
damage is: it turns a probabilistic magnet into a promise, and a promise justifies holding a losing
position (**m23**).

## Using it without being used by it

Nothing you have read here changes how a trade is built, and that is exactly why this module sits at the
end of the course:

1. **Read the sequence forwards, never backwards.** First the level the market was respecting, then the
   opposing candles, then the close that breaks, then the return. If the order is not complete there is
   no zone — there is a drawing.
2. **Treat the zone as a zone.** A reaction *inside* the band, with the tolerance **m08-l1** and **m13**
   demand of any level, and with the close — never the extreme — deciding whether it was lost.
3. **If you cannot name the mechanic, there is no trade.** This is **m08-l2**'s rule unchanged: if you
   cannot say what would be resting there and why, the drawing is the only thing on the chart. The
   dialect gets no exception; it gets the same test.
4. **Risk hears none of this.** Size comes from the distance to the stop and the stop comes from the
   structure (**m19**). A stop beyond a zone's far edge is a perfectly sensible structural stop; a stop
   widened so the zone has "room to work" is **m23**.

And keep the honest reason this was worth reading: half of what is written about charts is written in
this dialect, and being unable to read it is a handicap with no upside. Plenty of people watching the
same zones does make orders pile up around them, and that effect is real — as real as the 0.618 and the
200-day average — but it works **while the zone is crowded**, not because the name is right. That is the
whole of the effect, and also its expiry date.
