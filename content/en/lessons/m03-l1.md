# Reading a price chart

A price chart is not a crystal ball and it is not a work of art. It is a record of what actually
traded: at what price, when, and how much. Before any indicator or strategy, you need to read that
record fluently — what a single bar is telling you, and what it is not. This module is about that
literacy, stripped of mysticism. The market does not "want" anything and it is not sending you
messages; a chart is the summed footprints of people buying and selling, nothing more.

## The OHLC candle

Most charts are drawn as **candlesticks**. Each candle summarises one slice of time with four
numbers — **O**pen, **H**igh, **L**ow, **C**lose (OHLC):

- The **open** is the price at the start of the period; the **close** is the price at the end.
- The **high** and **low** are the most extreme prices reached during the period.

The candle draws these as a **body** and two **wicks** (also called shadows):

- The **body** spans from open to close. If price closed **higher** than it opened, the candle is
  **bullish** (usually green or hollow); if it closed **lower**, it is **bearish** (usually red or
  filled).
- The **wicks** stick out above and below the body, reaching up to the high and down to the low. A
  wick is price that was touched but *rejected* — it went there and came back before the period
  ended.

So a small body with a long lower wick means price fell hard, then buyers pushed it back up before
the close. That is a completely different story from a big bearish body with no lower wick, even if
both happened to close near the same place. The **close** is the number most traders weight most
heavily, because it is the price both sides were willing to leave on the table when the period
ended.

:::note{type=info}
Colour only tells you open-versus-close direction. A green candle can still close *below* the
previous candle's close — it just closed above its own open. Read the actual levels, not the colour
alone.
:::

## What one candle actually represents

A candle is meaningless until you know its **timeframe** — the length of time it covers. On a
1-minute chart each candle is one minute; on a daily chart each candle is a full day of trading
compressed into one bar. The open, high, low and close are all measured over that one window.

This matters because the same market looks calm or violent depending on the timeframe you choose. A
day that shows up as one modest red candle on the daily chart is hundreds of candles — rallies,
dips, panic and recovery — on the 5-minute chart. Neither view is "the truth"; they are different
zoom levels of the same data. Beginners often reach for a very low timeframe because it feels like
more information, when mostly it is more **noise**.

:::note{type=warning}
A common mistake is reading a signal on a 1-minute candle as if it carried the weight of a daily
one. It does not. The higher the timeframe, the more participants and money went into forming that
candle, and the more seriously its levels are worth taking.
:::

## Volume: how many people showed up

**Volume** is the amount traded during the candle's period — the number of units that changed hands.
On its own it does not tell you direction; it tells you **participation**. A big move on high volume
means a lot of people agreed to trade at those prices; the same move on thin volume means very few
did, and it is that much easier to reverse.

Volume is context, not a signal by itself. "Price broke to a new high" is a different statement from
"price broke to a new high on the strongest volume in weeks." The second has conviction behind it;
the first might be a handful of trades in a quiet hour.

## Trend vs range

Zoom out and price is almost always doing one of two things:

- **Trending** — making net progress in one direction. An **uptrend** prints **higher highs and
  higher lows**: each peak is above the last, and each dip bottoms out above the previous dip. A
  **downtrend** is the mirror image: **lower highs and lower lows**.
- **Ranging** — going sideways, bouncing between a rough ceiling and a rough floor with no net
  progress. The highs sit at similar levels and so do the lows.

Most of the time, markets range. Trends are the exception, which is exactly why they are worth
identifying. The single most useful question you can put to a chart is: *is this going somewhere, or
is it chopping sideways?* — because tactics that work in a trend get shredded in a range, and vice
versa.

## Support and resistance are zones, not lines

**Support** is a price area where buying has previously been strong enough to stop a fall;
**resistance** is an area where selling has stopped a rise. They are simply places where a lot of
people, for whatever reason, have been willing to act.

The crucial word is **area**. Beginners draw a single pixel-perfect line and expect price to reverse
exactly on it — then feel betrayed when it overshoots by a bit. Real support and resistance are
**zones**, usually a band of some width, because the orders and decisions that create them are
spread across a range of prices, not stacked on one exact number. Treat a level as a neighbourhood,
not a street address.

A zone earns credibility by being **tested**: the more times price has reacted there, the more
traders are watching it, which is partly why it keeps working — until enough of them give up and it
breaks. Nothing about it is magic. It holds because people remember it and act on it, and it fails
when they stop.

:::note{type=warning}
Exact-line thinking fails for a concrete reason: it produces false "the level broke!" alarms every
time price pokes a fraction past your line and snaps straight back. Read the level as a zone and that
same wick is just price testing the edge of the area — which is normal — instead of a breakout you
feel forced to react to.
:::
