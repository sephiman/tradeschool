# Reading a price chart

A price chart is not a crystal ball and it is not a work of art. It is a record of what actually
traded: at what price, when, and how much. Before any indicator or strategy, you need to read that
record fluently — what a single bar is telling you, and what it is not. This module is about that
literacy, stripped of mysticism. The market does not "want" anything and it is not sending you
messages; a chart is the summed footprints of people buying and selling, nothing more.

This first lesson stays close to the raw data: how to read **one candle**, what its **timeframe**
changes, and what **volume** adds. How price organises itself over many candles — trend, range, and
support/resistance — gets its own, deeper treatment in the next lesson.

## Reading a single candle

Most charts are drawn as **candlesticks**. Each candle summarises one slice of time with four
numbers — **O**pen, **H**igh, **L**ow, **C**lose (OHLC):

- The **open** is the price at the start of the period; the **close** is the price at the end.
- The **high** and **low** are the most extreme prices reached during the period.

The candle draws these as a **body** and two **wicks** (also called shadows):

- The **body** spans from open to close. If price closed **higher** than it opened, the candle is
  **bullish** (usually green or hollow); if it closed **lower**, it is **bearish** (usually red or
  filled).
- The **wicks** stick out above and below the body, reaching up to the high and down to the low. A
  wick is price that was touched but *rejected* — it went there and came back before the period
  ended.

::figure{id=fig-m03-candle-anatomy}

Now the **why**. Of the four numbers, the **close** is the one most traders weight most heavily,
because it is the price both sides were willing to leave on the table when the clock ran out — the
temporary truce, not the shouting in between. The **wicks** matter for the opposite reason: they
mark prices that were reached and *refused*. A long wick is the market saying "we tried this level
and it didn't stick."

Here is what that looks like in practice. Take a candle that **opens at 100, runs up to 108, falls
to 96, and closes at 101**. The body is tiny (100→101) but the lower wick is long (down to 96):
price was dumped hard, then bought all the way back before the close. Now take a second candle that
**opens at 108 and closes at 101 with no lower wick** — a big bearish body. Both candles *end* near
101, but they are opposite stories: the first shows buyers stepping in at 96, the second shows
steady selling with nobody defending the lows. Reading only the closing price, or only the colour,
would flatten those two into "the same thing." They are not.

:::note{type=info}
Colour only tells you open-versus-close direction. A green candle can still close *below* the
previous candle's close — it just closed above its own open. Read the actual levels, not the colour
alone.
:::

## Timeframes: one candle, many zoom levels

A candle is meaningless until you know its **timeframe** — the length of time it covers. On a
1-minute chart each candle is one minute; on a 4-hour chart, four hours; on a daily chart each
candle is a full day of trading compressed into one bar. The open, high, low and close are all
measured over that one window, whatever its length.

**Why it matters:** the same market looks calm or violent depending on the timeframe you choose, and
the two views are not equally trustworthy. The higher the timeframe, the more participants and more
money went into forming that candle — a daily candle is the settled verdict of everyone who traded
that day, while a 1-minute candle can be a single large order in a quiet moment. Higher timeframes
are slower to form but harder to fake, which is why their levels carry more weight.

In practice, a day that shows up as one modest red candle on the daily chart is **hundreds of
candles** — rallies, dips, panic and recovery — on the 5-minute chart. Say BTC opens the day at
60,000 and closes at 58,800: on the daily that is a single unremarkable −2% candle. Zoom to
5-minute and that same day might be a staircase down, a sharp bounce back to 59,500, and a slide
into the close — dozens of little "reversals," almost all of them noise. Neither view is "the
truth"; they are different zoom levels of the same data. Beginners often reach for a very low
timeframe because it *feels* like more information, when mostly it is more **noise**: more candles,
more wiggles, and a far worse signal-to-noise ratio.

## Volume: how much conviction is behind the move

**Volume** is the amount traded during the candle's period — the number of units (or contracts) that
changed hands. On its own it does not tell you direction; it tells you **participation**.

**Why it matters:** price tells you *where* trades happened; volume tells you *how many people
agreed*. A move on high volume means a lot of capital was committed at those prices, so it is harder
to unwind — plenty of people now hold positions there and have a reason to defend them. The same
move on thin volume means very few did, and it is that much easier to reverse, because there is
little conviction behind it.

In practice, compare two identical-looking breakouts to a new high. In the first, the breakout
candle trades **three times** the recent average volume: many participants are buying the breakout,
and the move has fuel. In the second, the same new high prints on **half** the average volume — a
handful of trades in a quiet hour nudging price up with nobody behind it. On the chart the two
candles look the same; the volume bar underneath is the tell. "Price broke to a new high" and "price
broke to a new high on the strongest volume in weeks" are completely different statements. Volume is
context, not a signal by itself.

## What a candle tempts you to conclude

The single most common misread is the **long wick**. A wick is *rejected* price, but beginners
routinely treat the extreme it reaches as if price meaningfully "belonged" there — placing a stop a
hair past a wick's tip, or panicking when a wick briefly pierces a level and snaps back. The body
and close are the signal; the wick is the failed attempt. A long wick against your position is often
good news, not bad: it means price went there and got pushed back.

The second trap is **lower-timeframe noise**. Drop to the 1-minute chart and every position looks
like it is constantly reversing — because at that zoom level it is; the chart is mostly wiggle.
Traders who try to "manage" a multi-day swing on a 1-minute chart end up reacting to noise that
would be invisible, and irrelevant, one or two timeframes up.

:::note{type=warning}
Do not read a signal on a 1-minute candle as if it carried the weight of a daily one, and do not
treat a wick's tip as a real "visit" to that price. Both mistakes share one root: forgetting that a
candle is a *summary of a period*, and that the period's length and the wick-versus-body distinction
are exactly what that summary is telling you.
:::

## Crypto is different: 24/7 candles

Everything above applies to any market, but crypto changes the mechanics in ways worth naming.

- **There is no open or close ritual.** Stock markets shut overnight and at weekends, so a stock's
  daily candle has a real open (the auction at the bell) and a real close, and price can *gap*
  between one day's close and the next day's open. Crypto never closes: trading runs 24 hours a day,
  7 days a week, so a "daily" candle simply spans a fixed 24-hour window, conventionally **00:00 to
  00:00 UTC**. That open and close are genuine prices, but nothing special happens at them — no
  bell, no settlement, no overnight gap. The "daily close" traders talk about is a convention, not
  an event.
- **Timeframe boundaries are arbitrary.** Because the cutoff is a clock time in UTC, the shape of
  your daily candle shifts if your charting tool uses a different timezone. Two traders can look at
  "the daily" and see slightly different candles. Nothing is broken; they have simply cut the same
  continuous tape at different points.
- **Weekend volume is thin.** Participation sags on Saturdays and Sundays, when much of the
  professional and institutional flow steps back. A thinner book means the same order pushes price
  further, so weekend moves can look dramatic on the chart while representing far fewer participants
  than a weekday move of the same size. Read a big weekend candle with that volume caveat firmly in
  mind.

## Trend, range, and levels — a first look

Zoom out from the single candle and price is almost always doing one of two things. It is
**trending** — making net progress in one direction, printing higher highs and higher lows (an
uptrend) or lower highs and lower lows (a downtrend) — or it is **ranging**, moving sideways between
a rough ceiling and a rough floor with no net progress. Most of the time markets range; trends are
the exception, which is exactly why identifying them is worth the effort.

Running through both are **support** and **resistance**: areas where buying has previously stopped a
fall (support) or selling has stopped a rise (resistance). The key word is *areas* — they are zones,
not pixel-perfect lines. We will build out trend, range, and support/resistance properly in the next
lesson; for now just hold the distinction: one candle is a data point, but many candles together
form *structure*, and structure is where the next lesson goes.
